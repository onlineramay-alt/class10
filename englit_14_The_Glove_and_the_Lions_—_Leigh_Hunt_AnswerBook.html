B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q3. (i) King Francis, a man who loved royal sport, is sitting one day with his nobles about him to watch the lions fight. His court is ranged round him, the ladies in their pride of place above, and among them Count de Lorge with the lady he loves. Below, in the pit, are the lions. The poet remarks that it was a gallant sight — valour and love in the gallery, a king above them, and the royal beasts beneath.

(ii) Hunt appeals to eye and ear together. Visually: the lions are 'ramped and roared', they lie 'with horrid laughing jaws'; 'they bit, they glared'; the blows of their paws beat 'a thunderous sound'; and the wind is filled with sand and mane as they wallow. Aurally: the roaring, the thunderous beating, and the alliterative 'horrid laughing jaws' which makes the sound of the line as ugly as the sight. The cumulative effect is to establish the real and lethal danger of the pit, so that what the lady asks a moment later is understood to be a demand that a man risk being torn apart.

(iii) She was the lady loved by Count de Lorge, seated beside him in the gallery — a beautiful, lively, self-regarding woman. She deliberately dropped her glove down into the pit among the lions and then, with a smile, looked at de Lorge and asked him to fetch it. She was prompted by vanity: she reasoned that his love was great, that the danger was great, and that if he retrieved the glove before the king, the court and the ladies, the glory would be hers — 'I'll drop my glove to prove his love; great glory will be mine.'

(iv) (a) Her expression never altered — the same fixed smile and the same bright, watchful eyes — whatever was happening; a face that gave nothing away and felt little, which is why the compliment is really a criticism.
(b) 'This is the perfect opportunity' — the moment, with the whole court watching, is ideal for her purpose of displaying her power over him.
(c) 'Truly, gentlemen, we are better off up here than down there' — the king's dry, humorous remark on watching the lions from the safety of the gallery. Its lightness makes the horror of the pit more real, not less.

(v) He applauds de Lorge's throwing the glove back into the lady's face. The count leaped into the pit among the lions, picked up the glove, and returned in an instant — and then, instead of presenting it to her, flung it straight in her face and left her.

He behaved so because he understood what had been done to him. He had gone down not to save her but to satisfy her vanity before an audience; his life had been risked as an entertainment and a proof of her importance. The gesture returns the insult exactly: she wanted a public display, and she receives one. King Francis, who has read the whole thing correctly from the start, pronounces the poem's judgement — 'No love, but vanity, sets love a task like that.'

Official Answer

Q4. (i) The stanza is full of internal rhyme — 'glove… love', 'quick… quick', 'glove… love' again — falling in the middle of each long line as well as at the end. The effect is to speed the verse up sharply. The rhymes come twice as often as the ear expects, so the lines seem to hurry, and the whole crisis of the poem — the drop, the bow, the leap, the return, the throw — is over in four lines. Hunt has made the metre do the work of the action.

(ii) It compresses the most dangerous moment in the poem into almost nothing. A man jumping among fighting lions might have been given a stanza of suspense; instead we get 'The leap was quick, return was quick' — six words, and he is back. The repetition tells us that de Lorge did not hesitate for an instant either going or returning, which establishes both his courage and, more importantly, that he had already decided what he was going to do when he got back.

(iii) It is the turn of the whole poem, and it works by echo. The lady dropped the glove 'to prove his love'; he throws it back 'but not with love'. The same rhyme, reversed in meaning, is the poem's judgement in four words. Everything the gesture rejects is contained in the negative: not the courage, which he has just proved, but the terms on which it was demanded.

(iv) King Francis rises and says, 'Rightly done!' — and adds, 'No love, but vanity, sets love a task like that.'

His verdict is that the lady's demand was not a test of love at all but an exhibition of her own importance, and that de Lorge was right to refuse to be used. He had seen it coming: the description of her 'smiling lips and sharp bright eyes, which always seemed the same' comes from the king's own observation, and his dry joke about being better off up here than down there shows that he alone in the gallery has been thinking about what the pit actually means.

It is hard to disagree. The lady risked another person's life for an audience, and told herself it was about love. The poem's economy is that it never argues this — it simply lets the king say it, in one line, at the end.

C. Short Answer Type

Q5. That love must not be used as an instrument of vanity, and that a demand for proof is not a sign of love but a symptom of self-regard. King Francis states it directly in the last line: 'No love, but vanity, sets love a task like that.'

The poem makes two supporting points. First, that courage is not the same as devotion — de Lorge's leap proves his bravery and settles nothing about the relationship. Second, that the person who requires public proof has already made the relationship a performance, and de Lorge's answer is simply to complete the performance she asked for.

Hunt keeps the moral for the final couplet and lets the story deliver it, which is why the poem does not read as a sermon.

Q6. The lady is beautiful, lively and entirely self-absorbed. Her one recorded thought is a calculation — that de Lorge's love is great, the danger is great, and the glory will be hers. Her face is fixed in a smile with 'sharp bright eyes, which always seemed the same', which suggests a woman who has learnt an expression rather than one who feels. She does not consider that she is asking a man to be killed for her prestige.

De Lorge is brave, quick, and — the poem's surprise — clear-sighted. He does not argue, plead or refuse; he bows and jumps, and the leap and the return are over in a line. What raises him above a merely gallant hero is what he does next. He has evidently understood, in those few seconds, exactly what was being done to him, and he answers it precisely: not by reproaching her, which would have been another performance, but with a single gesture that returns the insult in the same public currency she chose.

The contrast is between someone who treats another person as an instrument and someone who declines to be one. Hunt gives the lady no defence and de Lorge no speech, and the poem is the stronger for both silences.

D. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q7. SUBJECT AND STORY. A court watches lions fight; a lady drops her glove into the pit to make her lover fetch it and win herself glory; he fetches it and throws it in her face; the king approves. It is a narrative poem of about thirty lines that tells a complete story and delivers a judgement.

FORM. Rhyming couplets in very long lines — fourteen syllables or so — which gives the poem a swinging, ballad-like gallop. Hunt reinforces this with heavy internal rhyme at the crisis: 'She dropped her glove, to prove his love… Then threw the glove, but not with love.' The doubled rhymes accelerate the verse exactly where the action accelerates.

IMAGERY. Concentrated almost entirely on the pit, and deliberately violent: the lions 'ramped and roared', lay with 'horrid laughing jaws', bit and glared, and beat 'a thunderous sound' with their paws, the wind full of sand and mane. This is not decoration — the danger must be made physically real or the lady's request is merely rude rather than monstrous.

CHARACTERISATION BY DETAIL. Hunt draws all three principals in a phrase apiece. The lady has 'smiling lips and sharp bright eyes, which always seemed the same'. The king is caught by one dry joke — 'Faith, gentlemen, we're better here than there.' De Lorge is defined by an action and no words at all.

THEME. The poem attacks a convention it was written inside: the courtly idea that a lady may set her lover a task and that his willingness to die proves his devotion. Hunt's answer is that such a demand is vanity wearing love's clothes, and that the proper response is refusal.

CRITICAL COMMENT. The strength of the poem is its speed and its economy — the whole reversal takes four lines, and the moral takes one. Its limitation is that the lady is given no inner life and no chance to answer, so the judgement, though satisfying, is easily won. It is a superb piece of storytelling rather than a subtle study of character, and it does not pretend otherwise.
