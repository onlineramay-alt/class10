Identifying landforms from contour patterns is examined in Question 1 of every paper, and the same half-dozen patterns recur. This chapter is worth learning as a set of shapes: once the pattern is recognised, the answer follows.

A. Multiple Choice Questions

Q1. (b) A steep slope. The closer the contours, the greater the rise in height over a given horizontal distance — so closely packed contours mean a steep slope and widely spaced contours a gentle one.

B. Short Answer Type

Q4. A contour is an imaginary line drawn on a map joining all places that are at the same height above mean sea level.

The contour interval is the constant vertical difference in height between two consecutive contour lines on a map. On the Survey of India sheets used in the ICSE examination it is 20 metres, and because it is constant, the horizontal spacing of the contours becomes a direct measure of the steepness of the slope.

Official Answer

Q5. TRIANGULATED HEIGHT — a height fixed accurately by the process of triangulation and shown on the map by a small triangle with the height in metres beside it. It is the most accurate height given, and is usually the summit of a hill.

SPOT HEIGHT — a height measured at a particular point and shown by a dot with the figure beside it, for example .437. It is accurate but is not a surveyed station.

BENCH MARK — a height marked permanently on a fixed object on the ground, such as a stone, a wall or a building, and shown on the map as B.M. with the height. It is a reference point from which other heights can be measured.

RELATIVE HEIGHT — the height of a feature above the ground immediately around it, rather than above sea level. It is written with an 'r' after the figure, for example .3r, meaning that the feature (often a sand dune or an embankment) rises 3 metres above the surrounding land.

NOTE — '.3r in grid square 3501' was asked in both the ICSE 2026 and 2026 Improvement papers, so relative height is worth knowing exactly.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q6. (a) A HILL — a set of closed concentric contours, the values increasing towards the centre, usually with a triangulated or spot height at the top. A conical hill has evenly spaced rings; a hill with a broad top has a wide gap before the innermost ring.

(b) A VALLEY — contours bending in a V or U shape, with the point of the V directed upstream, towards the higher ground. A stream is normally drawn along the line of the V. A V-shaped valley has sharply pointed contours and indicates a young river; a U-shaped or broad valley has rounded ones.

(c) A RIDGE or WATER DIVIDE — an elongated area of high ground, shown by long narrow closed contours, with the land falling away on both sides. It is a water divide because streams flow away from it in opposite directions, and identifying it usually means finding the line from which the drainage separates.

(d) An ESCARPMENT — a steep slope on one side and a gentle slope on the other: the contours are packed very close together on the steep face and widely spaced on the gentle back-slope. It is the contour pattern that is asymmetrical, and that asymmetry is how it is recognised.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q7. GAP — a break or opening in a ridge or line of hills, through which a road, a railway or a river may pass.

PASS — a narrow gap through a mountain range which provides a route from one side to the other. On a map it appears as a low point in a line of high contours, often with a road or track shown through it.

SADDLE — a low-lying stretch between two hills or summits, so called because the contours resemble a saddle: two areas of closed contours with a dip between them. A pass is often found at a saddle.

KNOLL — a small isolated hill or mound, shown by a small set of closed contours, usually with a low height value.

SPUR — a tongue of high land projecting outwards from a hill or ridge into lower ground. Its contours form a V or U pointing DOWNHILL, away from the high ground — the exact opposite of a valley, and the two are constantly confused.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q8. From the drainage, the vegetation and the man-made responses to water, taken together.

DRAINAGE. Streams shown by broken blue lines are seasonal, which means rain falls only in one part of the year and the region is comparatively dry. Continuous blue lines mean perennial streams and therefore a wetter climate with a more reliable water supply.

VEGETATION AND LAND USE. Open scrub, scattered scrub and large areas of stony waste indicate low and unreliable rainfall. Dense forest, plantations and extensive cultivation indicate a moist climate. Terraced cultivation and rice fields imply plentiful water.

MAN-MADE RESPONSES. A great many wells, tanks and reservoirs show that people must store and lift water, and therefore that surface water fails for part of the year. Many causeways, rather than bridges, show that the streams are dry for most of the year. Canals show irrigation, and therefore a rainfall insufficient by itself.

A sheet covered with broken blue streams, causeways, wells, tanks and open scrub describes a region of low, seasonal rainfall — the semi-arid conditions of Rajasthan and Gujarat, which is exactly what sheets G43S7 and G43S10 show.

C. Topographical Sheet Questions (from the papers)

Q9. (a) METHOD. Go to grid square 3796 and look at the contour pattern around the spot height. Closed concentric contours with the height at the centre make it a hill; a small isolated set makes it a knoll; a long narrow set makes it a ridge. Name the feature and, where possible, add its height. The 2026 paper asked the same square as 'one relief feature found in grid square 3796', so the answer is a relief feature.

(b) Black curved lines on a Survey of India sheet are contours drawn in black rather than brown, which on these sheets indicates broken ground, or in some cases the edge of a sandy or gullied area. Check the index at the foot of the sheet before answering: it distinguishes contours, form lines, broken ground and sand features, and the examiner is testing whether the index has been used.

Q10. (a) It is a RELATIVE HEIGHT of 3 metres. The letter 'r' after a figure means that the height is measured not from sea level but from the ground immediately surrounding the feature — so the feature concerned stands 3 metres above the land around it. On these sheets it usually marks a sand dune or a small embankment.

This is the single most reliably repeated item in the Geography paper: '.3r in grid square 3501' was asked in both the 2026 and the 2026 Improvement papers, in almost the same words.

(b) METHOD. Examine the contours in the square and name the landform they describe — a hill, knoll, ridge, spur, valley, saddle or escarpment — supporting the identification with the contour pattern and the spot height (.522) printed there.
