Q1. (b) Sodium chloride. It is an IONIC compound, made of a giant lattice of oppositely charged ions held by strong electrostatic forces throughout the crystal; a great deal of heat is needed to break that lattice down. Q2. (a) HCl. Chlorine is much more electronegative than hydrogen, so the shared pair lies closer to the chlorine, giving the molecule a positive and a negative end — a dipole. In CCl₄ the four C–Cl bonds are individually polar, but they are arranged tetrahedrally and their polarities cancel, so the molecule as a whole is non-polar.

Official Answer

Q3. (c) two lone pairs of electrons. Oxygen has six valence electrons; two of them are shared, one with each hydrogen atom, forming two O–H bonds. That leaves four electrons — two non-bonding pairs — on the oxygen. Those two lone pairs are what push the molecule into its bent shape and make it polar.

Official Answer

Q4. (b) water. In H₂O the oxygen forms two bond pairs and keeps two lone pairs, and the O–H bonds are polar because oxygen is far more electronegative than hydrogen. Ammonia has only one lone pair; chlorine and oxygen molecules are non-polar (identical atoms).

Official Answer

Q5. (d) Sodium loses an electron to form Na⁺ and chlorine gains an electron to form Cl⁻. The metal, with one valence electron, transfers it to the non-metal, which needs one to complete its octet. Both then have noble-gas configurations, and the oppositely charged ions attract to form the ionic bond.

Official Answer

Q6. (d) Carbon tetrachloride. CCl₄ is a covalent molecular liquid; only weak forces act between its separate molecules, so it boils at a low temperature. The other three are ionic solids held by a strong lattice of electrostatic forces and have very high melting and boiling points.

Official Answer

Q7. (b) consists of molecules. A covalent compound is built of discrete molecules held together by weak intermolecular forces — which is why covalent compounds have low melting points, are usually insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents, and do not conduct electricity in any state (no ions). Options (a), (c) and (d) describe ionic compounds.

Official Answer

Q8. (b) H₃O⁺. In the hydronium ion the oxygen forms three O–H bonds and keeps ONE lone pair (it started with two lone pairs in water and used one to form a co-ordinate bond with H⁺). OH⁻ has three lone pairs, NH₄⁺ has none, and H⁺ has no electrons at all.

Official Answer

Q9. (d) triple covalent bond. Each nitrogen atom has five valence electrons and needs three more for an octet, so the two atoms share three pairs — a triple bond, N≡N. The bond is non-polar because the two atoms are identical.

Official Answer

Q10. (a) one. Nitrogen has five valence electrons; it shares three of them, one with each hydrogen, forming three N–H bonds. The remaining two electrons stay as ONE lone pair on the nitrogen — the pair that makes ammonia basic and lets it form a co-ordinate bond.

B. Identify / Name / Very Short Answer

Q11. A LONE PAIR (also called a non-bonding pair).

Official Answer

Q12. (a) An IONIC or ELECTROVALENT BOND is the bond formed by the complete TRANSFER of one or more electrons from the atom of a metal to the atom of a non-metal, so that both attain a stable noble-gas configuration; the oppositely charged ions produced are then held together by electrostatic attraction.

(b) A COVALENT BOND is the bond formed by the mutual SHARING of one or more pairs of electrons between two atoms, each contributing an equal number, so that both attain a stable noble-gas configuration.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q13. A CO-ORDINATE or DATIVE BOND is a covalent bond in which BOTH of the shared electrons are contributed by the SAME atom. The atom supplying the pair is the donor and must have a lone pair; the atom receiving it is the acceptor and must have an incomplete octet. Once formed, the bond is indistinguishable from an ordinary covalent bond, and it is shown by an arrow pointing from donor to acceptor.

Example: the AMMONIUM ION, NH₄⁺. The nitrogen of ammonia has a lone pair, which it donates to a hydrogen ion H⁺, which has no electrons at all. (The hydronium ion H₃O⁺ is an equally good example.)

(b) A POLAR COVALENT BOND — formed between two atoms of different electronegativity, so the shared pair lies closer to the more electronegative atom, giving the bond a positive and a negative end (e.g. H–Cl).

The HYDRONIUM ion H₃O⁺ has ONE lone pair (on the oxygen). The AMMONIUM ion NH₄⁺ has NO lone pair — the nitrogen's original lone pair has been donated to a hydrogen ion to form the fourth N–H bond, a co-ordinate bond.

Q16. (a) Inert gases already have a stable, complete octet (or duplet, for helium). They have no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons, so they neither form ions nor bond.

(b) A covalent compound is made of separate molecules held together only by weak intermolecular forces. Little energy is needed to overcome those forces, so the compound melts and boils at a low temperature. (The strong covalent bonds inside each molecule are not broken on melting.)

(c) An ionic compound is a giant lattice of oppositely charged ions held throughout by strong electrostatic attraction. A large amount of heat is needed to break that lattice down, so the melting point is high.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q17. POLAR vs NON-POLAR: in a polar covalent compound the shared pair is pulled towards the more electronegative atom, so the molecule has a permanent dipole (e.g. HCl); in a non-polar covalent compound the sharing is equal and there is no dipole (e.g. CCl₄, CH₄).

(a) SOLUBILITY — electrovalent compounds dissolve readily in water and other polar solvents but not in organic solvents; covalent compounds are generally insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents such as benzene and carbon tetrachloride.

(b) STRUCTURE — an electrovalent compound is a giant three-dimensional lattice of ions with no discrete molecules; a covalent compound consists of separate, individual molecules.

C. Short Answer Type

Q18. (i) STATE AND MELTING POINT. Ionic compounds are hard crystalline solids with high melting and boiling points, because the whole crystal is a lattice held by strong electrostatic forces. Covalent compounds are usually gases, liquids or soft solids with low melting and boiling points, because only weak intermolecular forces hold the separate molecules together.

(ii) CONDUCTION OF ELECTRICITY. Ionic compounds conduct when molten or dissolved in water, because the ions become free to move; they do not conduct as solids, since the ions are locked in the lattice. Covalent compounds do not conduct in any state, because they contain no ions — the exception being the polar covalent compounds such as HCl, which ionise in water.

(iii) SOLUBILITY. Ionic compounds dissolve readily in water and other polar solvents but not in organic solvents. Covalent compounds are generally insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents such as benzene, ether and carbon tetrachloride.

(A fourth: ionic reactions are fast, being simply the meeting of ions in solution, while covalent reactions are slow, requiring bonds to be broken and formed.)

Q19. (a) Conduction requires charged particles that are FREE TO MOVE. In a solid ionic compound the ions exist but are locked in fixed positions in the crystal lattice by strong electrostatic forces, so although charges are present they cannot migrate and no current flows. On melting, the lattice breaks down and the ions become mobile, so they can move to the electrodes and carry the current. Dissolving the compound in water has the same effect.

(b) Because carbon tetrachloride is a NON-POLAR COVALENT compound. It consists of neutral molecules and contains no ions at all, either free or fixed; and being non-polar it does not ionise in water either. With no charged particles available in any state, it cannot conduct.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q20. Because in the pure gaseous state HCl exists as discrete POLAR COVALENT MOLECULES. The bond is polar, but it has not been broken, so there are no free ions and nothing to carry a current — the gas is a non-electrolyte.

When HCl is dissolved in water, the polar water molecules attract the oppositely charged ends of the HCl molecule strongly enough to break the bond completely, and the molecule IONISES into H⁺ (as H₃O⁺) and Cl⁻. Because the ionisation is virtually complete, the solution contains a high concentration of mobile ions and is a strong electrolyte.

This is why water is called the essential condition for HCl to behave as an acid at all.

Q23. (i) METHANE. Carbon has four valence electrons (dots); each of four hydrogen atoms contributes one (crosses). Four shared pairs are formed, each with one dot and one cross, and carbon attains an octet while each hydrogen attains a duplet. There are NO lone pairs on the carbon.

(ii) MAGNESIUM CHLORIDE. Magnesium (2,8,2) TRANSFERS its two valence electrons, one to each of two chlorine atoms (2,8,7). The result is written as Mg²⁺ with an empty outermost shell shown, beside two separate Cl⁻ ions, each drawn with a complete octet of eight electrons — seven of its own plus the one received. The charges must be marked, and the ions must be drawn SEPARATELY, not joined by a shared pair; that is the difference from a covalent diagram.

(iii) METHANE, being covalent and molecular: it is a gas at room temperature with a very low melting and boiling point, because only weak forces act between its molecules; and it does not conduct electricity in any state, because it contains no ions. It is also insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents.

MAGNESIUM CHLORIDE, being ionic: it is a hard crystalline solid with a high melting point, because the whole crystal is a lattice held by strong electrostatic attraction; and it conducts electricity when molten or in aqueous solution, but not when solid, because the ions are only free to move once the lattice has broken down. It is also readily soluble in water.
