Q2. (a) HCl. Chlorine is much more electronegative than hydrogen, so the shared pair lies closer to the chlorine, giving the molecule a positive and a negative end — a dipole. In CCl₄ the four C–Cl bonds are individually polar, but they are arranged tetrahedrally and their polarities cancel, so the molecule as a whole is non-polar.

Q3. A LONE PAIR (also called a non-bonding pair).

Q4. (a) An IONIC or ELECTROVALENT BOND is the bond formed by the complete TRANSFER of one or more electrons from the atom of a metal to the atom of a non-metal, so that both attain a stable noble-gas configuration; the oppositely charged ions produced are then held together by electrostatic attraction.

(b) A COVALENT BOND is the bond formed by the mutual SHARING of one or more pairs of electrons between two atoms, each contributing an equal number, so that both attain a stable noble-gas configuration.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q5. A CO-ORDINATE or DATIVE BOND is a covalent bond in which BOTH of the shared electrons are contributed by the SAME atom. The atom supplying the pair is the donor and must have a lone pair; the atom receiving it is the acceptor and must have an incomplete octet. Once formed, the bond is indistinguishable from an ordinary covalent bond, and it is shown by an arrow pointing from donor to acceptor.

Example: the AMMONIUM ION, NH₄⁺. The nitrogen of ammonia has a lone pair, which it donates to a hydrogen ion H⁺, which has no electrons at all. (The hydronium ion H₃O⁺ is an equally good example.)

C. Short Answer Type

Q6. (i) STATE AND MELTING POINT. Ionic compounds are hard crystalline solids with high melting and boiling points, because the whole crystal is a lattice held by strong electrostatic forces. Covalent compounds are usually gases, liquids or soft solids with low melting and boiling points, because only weak intermolecular forces hold the separate molecules together.

(ii) CONDUCTION OF ELECTRICITY. Ionic compounds conduct when molten or dissolved in water, because the ions become free to move; they do not conduct as solids, since the ions are locked in the lattice. Covalent compounds do not conduct in any state, because they contain no ions — the exception being the polar covalent compounds such as HCl, which ionise in water.

(iii) SOLUBILITY. Ionic compounds dissolve readily in water and other polar solvents but not in organic solvents. Covalent compounds are generally insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents such as benzene, ether and carbon tetrachloride.

(A fourth: ionic reactions are fast, being simply the meeting of ions in solution, while covalent reactions are slow, requiring bonds to be broken and formed.)

Q7. (a) Conduction requires charged particles that are FREE TO MOVE. In a solid ionic compound the ions exist but are locked in fixed positions in the crystal lattice by strong electrostatic forces, so although charges are present they cannot migrate and no current flows. On melting, the lattice breaks down and the ions become mobile, so they can move to the electrodes and carry the current. Dissolving the compound in water has the same effect.

(b) Because carbon tetrachloride is a NON-POLAR COVALENT compound. It consists of neutral molecules and contains no ions at all, either free or fixed; and being non-polar it does not ionise in water either. With no charged particles available in any state, it cannot conduct.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q8. Because in the pure gaseous state HCl exists as discrete POLAR COVALENT MOLECULES. The bond is polar, but it has not been broken, so there are no free ions and nothing to carry a current — the gas is a non-electrolyte.

When HCl is dissolved in water, the polar water molecules attract the oppositely charged ends of the HCl molecule strongly enough to break the bond completely, and the molecule IONISES into H⁺ (as H₃O⁺) and Cl⁻. Because the ionisation is virtually complete, the solution contains a high concentration of mobile ions and is a strong electrolyte.

This is why water is called the essential condition for HCl to behave as an acid at all.

Q9. (a) NITROGEN. Period 2 gives two shells and Group 15 gives five valence electrons, so the configuration is 2,5 — atomic number 7.

(b) NH₃, ammonia.

(c) The structure to draw: nitrogen at the centre with five valence electrons shown as dots, and three hydrogen atoms each contributing one electron shown as a cross. Three shared pairs are formed, each pair consisting of one dot and one cross, and ONE LONE PAIR of dots remains on the nitrogen.

The lone pair must be shown — it is the commonest omission, it is what makes ammonia basic and able to form a co-ordinate bond, and examiners deduct for leaving it out.

Official Answer

Q10. (i) METHANE. Carbon has four valence electrons (dots); each of four hydrogen atoms contributes one (crosses). Four shared pairs are formed, each with one dot and one cross, and carbon attains an octet while each hydrogen attains a duplet. There are NO lone pairs on the carbon.

(ii) MAGNESIUM CHLORIDE. Magnesium (2,8,2) TRANSFERS its two valence electrons, one to each of two chlorine atoms (2,8,7). The result is written as Mg²⁺ with an empty outermost shell shown, beside two separate Cl⁻ ions, each drawn with a complete octet of eight electrons — seven of its own plus the one received. The charges must be marked, and the ions must be drawn SEPARATELY, not joined by a shared pair; that is the difference from a covalent diagram.

(iii) METHANE, being covalent and molecular: it is a gas at room temperature with a very low melting and boiling point, because only weak forces act between its molecules; and it does not conduct electricity in any state, because it contains no ions. It is also insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents.

MAGNESIUM CHLORIDE, being ionic: it is a hard crystalline solid with a high melting point, because the whole crystal is a lattice held by strong electrostatic attraction; and it conducts electricity when molten or in aqueous solution, but not when solid, because the ions are only free to move once the lattice has broken down. It is also readily soluble in water.
