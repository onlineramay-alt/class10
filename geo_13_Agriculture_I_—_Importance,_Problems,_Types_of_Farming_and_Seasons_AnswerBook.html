B. Short Answer Type

Q5. It is the LIVELIHOOD of the majority of Indians — nearly half the working population depends on it directly, and a great many more indirectly, so the condition of agriculture decides the condition of most of the country.

It provides the country's FOOD. India feeds a population of over 1.4 billion, and does so from her own fields; self-sufficiency in food grains is among the most important achievements of independent India.

It supplies RAW MATERIALS to industry — cotton to the textile mills, sugarcane to the sugar factories, jute to the mills of Bengal, oilseeds to the vanaspati industry, and tea, coffee and rubber to processing industries. The agro-based industries could not exist without it.

It earns FOREIGN EXCHANGE through the export of tea, coffee, spices, cotton, rice and marine products; and it provides the MARKET for the goods of Indian industry, since the buying power of the rural population determines the demand for everything from fertilizer and tractors to bicycles and cloth.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q6. PROBLEMS.
(i) DEPENDENCE ON THE MONSOON. Much of the cultivated area is still unirrigated, so a late or failed monsoon means a failed crop. Reform: the extension of irrigation through canals, tube wells and multipurpose river valley projects, and the promotion of drip and sprinkler irrigation.

(ii) SMALL AND FRAGMENTED HOLDINGS. Inheritance has divided land into plots too small and too scattered to farm efficiently or to justify machinery. Reform: the CONSOLIDATION OF HOLDINGS, by which a farmer's scattered plots are exchanged for one compact block; and the encouragement of cooperative farming.

(iii) LOW PRODUCTIVITY AND OUTDATED METHODS. Yields per hectare have been low, with poor seed, little fertilizer and traditional implements. Reform: the GREEN REVOLUTION — high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice, chemical fertilizers, pesticides, tractors and assured irrigation — together with agricultural research, extension services and soil testing.

(Also acceptable, with their reforms: the exploitation of the cultivator by moneylenders and middlemen, met by cooperative credit societies, regulated markets and minimum support prices; the absence of storage and transport, met by warehousing and rural roads; and the abolition of the zamindari system to give the tiller the land.)

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q7. SUBSISTENCE FARMING — the farmer grows food for himself and his family, with little or nothing left to sell. Holdings are small, methods traditional, yields low. It is still the commonest form in India.

COMMERCIAL FARMING — crops are grown for sale in the market, on larger holdings, with machinery, fertilizer and high-yielding seed, and often specialising in one crop. Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh are the chief examples.

SHIFTING CULTIVATION — a primitive subsistence method in which forest is cleared by cutting and burning, cropped for two or three years until the soil is exhausted, then abandoned for a new patch. Practised by tribal communities in the north-east (jhum), Odisha and Andhra Pradesh (podu). It is destructive of forest and gives poor yields.

INTENSIVE FARMING — a great deal of labour, manure and irrigation applied to a small holding to obtain the maximum yield, often with two or three crops a year from the same field. Characteristic of the densely populated river valleys and deltas.

EXTENSIVE FARMING — large farms worked with machinery and comparatively little labour, giving a high total output but a low yield per hectare. Found in India only on a limited scale, in parts of Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan.

PLANTATION FARMING — a single cash crop grown on a large estate, with heavy capital investment, a large labour force, scientific management and processing on the spot, the produce being grown wholly for the market and largely for export. Tea in Assam and the Nilgiris, coffee in Karnataka and Kerala, rubber in Kerala, and spices.

MIXED FARMING — the growing of crops combined with the rearing of livestock on the same farm, each supporting the other: the animals supply manure and draught power, the crops supply fodder. It spreads the farmer's risk and gives him an income through the year.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q8. KHARIF — sown with the onset of the south-west monsoon in June and July, harvested September to October. It depends on the MONSOON RAINS. Crops: rice and jowar (also bajra, maize, cotton, jute, groundnut, sugarcane).

RABI — sown October to December, harvested April to June. It depends on IRRIGATION and on the winter rain brought by the WESTERN DISTURBANCES, and needs a cool growing season with a warm ripening period. Crops: wheat and gram (also barley, mustard, peas, linseed).

ZAYAD — the short summer season between the rabi harvest and the kharif sowing, roughly March to June. It depends ENTIRELY ON IRRIGATION, since it falls in the driest months. Crops: watermelon and cucumber (also muskmelon, vegetables, fodder crops and, in some regions, a short-duration rice).

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q9. (i) It is the LIVELIHOOD of about half the working population — nearly 55 per cent of Indians depend on farming and allied activities for their income.

(ii) It FEEDS the country — it must produce the food grains, pulses, oilseeds, sugar, fruit and vegetables for over 1.4 billion people; it supplies RAW MATERIALS to industry (cotton, jute, sugarcane, oilseeds); it earns FOREIGN EXCHANGE through exports of tea, coffee, spices, rice and cotton; and it provides the demand that keeps rural trade, transport and industry going.

Q10. (a) MIXED FARMING is the practice of raising CROPS and rearing LIVESTOCK (cattle, poultry, sheep) together on the same farm as one enterprise.

(b) It gives the farmer a STEADIER INCOME and greater SECURITY — if the crop fails, the animals still bring in money from milk, eggs, wool or meat; the animals provide manure for the fields and draught power, and the crop residues feed the animals, so nothing is wasted and the land stays fertile.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q11. INTENSIVE farming uses a SMALL holding but a large amount of labour, manure, irrigation and high-yielding seed on it, to get the maximum output PER HECTARE. It is the pattern in the densely peopled river plains and deltas of India (rice in the Ganga and Kaveri deltas).

EXTENSIVE farming uses a LARGE holding with machinery and comparatively little labour per hectare; the yield per hectare is lower but the total output and the output per worker are high. It is practised where land is plentiful and people few (wheat farms of Punjab on the large scale, and the wheat belts of North America and Australia).

Q12. PROBLEMS (any two): total dependence on an uncertain monsoon, with no irrigation on most land; tiny, SCATTERED and FRAGMENTED holdings that cannot be farmed efficiently; old traditional methods, poor seed and too little manure, giving low yields; lack of capital and dependence on the money-lender; and inadequate storage, marketing and fair prices.

REFORMS (any two): the GREEN REVOLUTION package — HYV seeds, chemical fertilisers and assured irrigation; the CONSOLIDATION of holdings and abolition of intermediaries (land reform); rural CREDIT through cooperative and regional rural banks and the Kisan Credit Card; MINIMUM SUPPORT PRICES and a network of regulated markets; and crop insurance, subsidised power and fertiliser, and farm-extension services.

Q13. • PURPOSE: in SUBSISTENCE farming the crop is grown mainly to FEED THE FARMER’S OWN FAMILY, with little left to sell; in COMMERCIAL farming the crop is grown for SALE in the market and for export.
• SCALE AND INPUTS: subsistence farming is on a SMALL holding with family labour, simple tools and little capital; commercial farming is on a LARGER holding with machinery, hired labour, fertilisers and high-yielding seed.
• OUTPUT: subsistence farming gives low yields and a small surplus; commercial farming (including plantation farming) gives large, marketable surpluses.
