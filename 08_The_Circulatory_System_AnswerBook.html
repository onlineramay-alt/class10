D. Descriptive Type Questions

Q50. (a) Circulatory system: the network consisting of the heart, blood and blood vessels that transports oxygen, nutrients and hormones to body cells and removes wastes. (b) Blood: a fluid connective tissue circulating in blood vessels, made up of plasma and blood cells. (c) Heart: a muscular, four-chambered pumping organ that drives the circulation of blood through the body. (d) Diapedesis: the process by which white blood cells squeeze out through the walls of capillaries into surrounding tissues. (e) Phagocytosis: the process by which certain white blood cells (e.g. neutrophils) engulf and destroy bacteria and foreign particles. (f) Rh factor: an inherited antigen present on the surface of RBCs; people who have it are Rh-positive, those who lack it are Rh-negative.

Official Answer

Q51. (a) Erythrocytes (mature mammalian RBCs) lack a nucleus; Leukocytes (WBCs) possess a nucleus. (b) Leukocytes live for a few days to a few weeks; Thrombocytes (platelets) live for only 3-5 days. (c) Arteries have thick, muscular and elastic walls with a narrow lumen; veins have thin walls with a wide lumen. (d) Pulmonary circulation carries deoxygenated blood from the heart to the lungs and returns oxygenated blood; systemic circulation carries oxygenated blood from the heart to the whole body and returns deoxygenated blood. (e) Mitral (bicuspid) valve is located between the left atrium and left ventricle; the Tricuspid valve is located between the right atrium and right ventricle.

Official Answer

Q52. (a) The left ventricle must pump blood at high pressure to the entire body (systemic circulation), whereas the right ventricle only pumps blood to the nearby lungs, so the left ventricle needs a thicker, more muscular wall. (b) The right ventricle actively pumps blood (needs muscular contraction), whereas the right auricle merely receives blood passively and pushes it a short distance into the ventricle, needing far less muscle. (c) Vitamin K is required by the liver to synthesise prothrombin, one of the essential clotting factors in the coagulation cascade; without it, blood cannot clot normally. (d) The absence of these organelles leaves more space for haemoglobin, maximising oxygen-carrying capacity, gives the cell its flexible biconcave shape for squeezing through narrow capillaries, and prevents the cell from consuming the oxygen it is meant to transport. (e) Although the heart is centrally located in the thoracic cavity, it is tilted with its broader base pointing right and its apex pointing towards the left, and the powerful beating of the more muscular left ventricle is felt more strongly on the left side of the chest.

Official Answer

Q53. (a) Tonsils: lymphatic organs that trap and destroy germs entering through the nose and mouth, localising infection. (b) Spleen: acts as a blood reservoir, filters and destroys old/worn-out RBCs, and produces lymphocytes. (c) Hepatic portal vein: carries nutrient-rich blood absorbed from the stomach and intestine directly to the liver for processing. (d) Basophils: release histamine and heparin, playing a role in inflammatory and allergic responses. (e) S.A.N. (sino-atrial node): acts as the natural pacemaker of the heart, initiating the electrical impulses that cause the atria to contract.

Q54. Double circulation means that in one complete cardiac cycle, blood passes through the heart twice — once via pulmonary circulation and once via systemic circulation. Pulmonary circulation carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs and returns oxygenated blood to the left atrium. Systemic circulation carries oxygenated blood from the left ventricle to all body tissues and returns deoxygenated blood to the right atrium.

Official Answer

Q55. 1. Injury damages tissue cells and platelets, which release the enzyme thrombokinase (thromboplastin) at the wound site.
2. In the presence of calcium ions and Vitamin K, thrombokinase converts the inactive plasma protein prothrombin into active thrombin.
3. Thrombin converts the soluble plasma protein fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin threads.
4. The fibrin threads form a mesh that traps blood cells, forming a clot; the clot shrinks, squeezing out clear serum and sealing the wound.
