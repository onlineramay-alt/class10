Constructions are done with RULER AND COMPASSES ONLY — a protractor may be used to check but not to construct, unless the question says otherwise.

THE GOLDEN RULE: NEVER RUB OUT THE CONSTRUCTION ARCS. They are the evidence that the figure was constructed rather than measured, and a clean-looking diagram without them scores almost nothing.

The syllabus requires: the circumcircle and incircle of a triangle, and tangents to a circle from an external point.

A. Worked Problems

Q1. CONSTRUCTION.

STEP 1 — draw the triangle. Draw AB = 6 cm. At B construct an angle of 60° using compasses (arc from B cutting AB, then the same radius from that intersection). Along that arm mark BC = 5 cm. Join AC.

STEP 2 — find the CIRCUMCENTRE. The circumcentre is equidistant from all three VERTICES, so it lies on the PERPENDICULAR BISECTOR of every side. Construct the perpendicular bisectors of any TWO sides — say AB and BC — using arcs of radius more than half the side from each end. They meet at O, the circumcentre.

STEP 3 — with centre O and radius OA, draw the circle. It should pass exactly through B and C as well; if it does not, the bisectors were inaccurate.

STEP 4 — measure OA with the ruler. That is the circumradius, and it should be about **3.2 cm** for these measurements.

WHY PERPENDICULAR BISECTORS: the locus of points equidistant from two points is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them — so the point equidistant from all three vertices must lie on all three bisectors. Two are enough to fix it; the third is a check.

CHECK BY CALCULATION: by the cosine rule AC² = 6² + 5² − 2(6)(5)cos60° = 36 + 25 − 30 = 31, so AC = 5.57 cm. Then R = AC/(2 sin B) = 5.57/(2 × 0.866) = 3.21 cm ✓

Q2. CONSTRUCTION.

STEP 1 — draw the triangle from the three sides. Draw QR = 6 cm. With centre Q and radius 5 cm draw an arc; with centre R and radius 7 cm draw another. They meet at P. Join PQ and PR.

STEP 2 — find the INCENTRE. The incentre is equidistant from all three SIDES, so it lies on the BISECTOR of every angle. Construct the bisectors of any TWO angles — say Q and R. They meet at I, the incentre.

STEP 3 — find the radius. The incircle touches the sides, so the radius is the PERPENDICULAR distance from I to a side. Drop a perpendicular from I to QR, meeting it at M. (Construct it properly with arcs; do not just drop it by eye.)

STEP 4 — with centre I and radius IM, draw the circle. It should touch all three sides exactly once.

STEP 5 — measure IM. It should be about **1.6 cm** for these measurements.

THE DISTINCTION TO KEEP CLEAR:
CIRCUMCIRCLE — passes THROUGH the vertices — found by PERPENDICULAR BISECTORS OF SIDES — radius measured to a VERTEX.
INCIRCLE — touches the SIDES — found by ANGLE BISECTORS — radius measured PERPENDICULARLY to a side.
Confusing the two constructions is the most frequent loss of marks in this chapter.

CHECK BY CALCULATION: s = (5+6+7)/2 = 9; area = √[9(9−5)(9−6)(9−7)] = √216 = 14.70 cm²; r = area/s = 14.70/9 = 1.63 cm ✓

Q3. CONSTRUCTION.

STEP 1 — draw the circle with centre O and radius 3 cm. Mark P with OP = 7 cm.

STEP 2 — bisect OP to find its mid-point M (perpendicular bisector construction).

STEP 3 — with centre M and radius MO (= MP = 3.5 cm), draw a second circle. It cuts the first circle at A and B.

STEP 4 — join PA and PB. These are the required tangents.

WHY IT WORKS: OP is a diameter of the second circle, so ∠OAP is the angle in a semicircle and is therefore 90°. That makes PA perpendicular to the radius OA at A — which is precisely the condition for PA to be a tangent.

STEP 5 — measure PA. It should be about **6.3 cm**.

CHECK BY CALCULATION: triangle OAP is right-angled at A, so by Pythagoras
PA² = OP² − OA² = 7² − 3² = 49 − 9 = 40
PA = √40 = 6.32 cm ✓

The two tangents from an external point are always equal, so PB = PA.
