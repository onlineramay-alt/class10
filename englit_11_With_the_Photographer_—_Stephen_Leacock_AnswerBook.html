B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q9. (i) He is in a photographer's studio, having gone in to have his picture taken. He has waited an hour in the anteroom, reading the National Geographic Magazine — long enough, he says, to have thought over his whole life.

(ii) It is a comic simile of contemptuous inspection. A man looking into an empty well looks with the flat disappointment of finding nothing where something ought to be; the photographer examines the narrator's face and finds it similarly void of anything he can use. The image does two things at once — it conveys the photographer's rudeness, and it reduces the narrator's face to a hole in the ground.

(iii) Any three of: the head is 'slightly oblique' and must be turned; the face is 'somewhat ovoid in shape'; the ears are wrong — 'droop them a little more'; the eyes must be rolled out under the eyelids; the mouth must be closed, and then partially opened; the expression must be altered; and finally the hands must be folded across the knee and the head thrown up so as to show the neck.

(iv) It comes from the narrator's meek agreement. A man told to his face that his face is wrong might reasonably object; instead he concurs, sadly and at once, as though this were an old grief he has lived with for years — and then, humbly, asks whether anything can be done. The comedy is in the mismatch between the outrage of the insult and the mildness of the response, and Leacock keeps that mismatch running until the very last page, when it finally snaps.

(v) By several devices, all working together.

The first is understatement and the deadpan. The narrator reports monstrous rudeness in a tone of patient reasonableness — 'I know… I have always known it' — and never raises his voice until the end.

The second is the comic simile, of which the story is full: the photographer looks into his face as into an empty well; he grinds the man's head as though sharpening a tool; he treats the face as raw material to be worked.

The third is the reduction of a person to an object. The photographer speaks of the head, the ears, the mouth and the eyes as separable defects, never addressing the man who owns them, and eventually retouches away everything that made the face a particular face at all.

The fourth is the reversal at the end. Having been told for pages that his face is wrong, the narrator finally rounds on the photographer and defends it — it is his face, he has had it for forty years, he knows its faults, it is his own and he paid for it. The joke turns into something with a point: the man is asserting his right to look like himself. Leacock's humour is nearly always of this kind — gentle, self-deprecating, and with a small serious observation left behind when the laugh has gone.

Q10. (i) The finished proof of his photograph, brought out to him a week after the sitting.

(ii) The ears have been removed altogether; the eyebrows have been taken out and replaced with better ones; the mouth has been altered; the eyes have been changed; and the face has been generally 'softened' and retouched until nothing of the original remains. The photographer names each change with pride.

(iii) He calls it a 'fine picture' and speaks of the retouching as an improvement — the ears were bad, so he has removed them; the eyebrows were poor, so he has substituted others; the expression has been corrected. He presents the destruction of a likeness as a professional service.

(iv) That he came to be photographed and has instead been replaced. A photograph is supposed to record a face; what he has been given is a face the photographer preferred, attached to his name. He paid for a likeness and has received an invention, and he objects to the impertinence of a stranger deciding what he ought to look like.

(v) Two targets, one obvious and one underneath it.

The obvious target is professional self-importance — the tradesman who has come to believe that his craft entitles him to overrule the customer, and who treats a human face as material for improvement. The photographer never once considers that the man might have a view about his own appearance.

The deeper target is the vanity that invites it, and Leacock is careful to implicate the narrator. He submits to every instruction for pages, agreeing that his face is wrong, obediently drooping his ears and rolling his eyes, because he too would rather look better than look like himself. He only rebels when the improvement is total.

What the story leaves behind is a serious little point in a comic frame: that a face is not a defect to be corrected but the thing a person actually is, and that surrendering it to somebody else's taste is a loss, however expertly done. 'It is my face. It is mine. I have had it for years' is the funniest and the truest sentence in the sketch.

Q11. (i) He was 'a DROOPING MAN in a gray suit, with the dim eye of a natural scientist' — listless, colourless, unsmiling. He looked at the author 'without enthusiasm' because he was already, silently, disapproving of the author's face as raw material. The impression the author formed was of an unfriendly, unfeeling, faintly contemptuous man.

(ii) He made him sit 'in a beam of sunlight filtered through a sheet of factory cotton hung against a frosted skylight'. For his first attempt the photographer rolled a large machine into the middle of the room, CRAWLED IN BEHIND IT under a black cloth, stayed there a moment as if praying, then came out again dissatisfied and began pulling at the cotton sheet to change the light.

(iii) A stream of contradictory commands: droop the ears, roll the eyes in under the lids, put the hands on the knees, turn the face upward, then 'expand the lungs', 'contract the waist', 'hump the neck', and 'twist the hip up toward the elbow' — until the sitter is knotted into an impossible posture.

(iv) The EARS had some resemblance to the real man. The photographer offered to fix even that — by a process he called 'the Sulphide', he could REMOVE THE EARS ENTIRELY and put in new ones.

(v) It is a portrait that erases the sitter: eyebrows gone, brow and hairline redrawn, mouth altered, ears to be replaced — a 'likeness' with nothing of the person left in it. The modern-day equivalent is heavy PHOTO-EDITING / FILTERS / airbrushing on phones and social media. It is insensitive because it amounts to telling a person, to his face and for profit, that his natural appearance is not good enough — a form of body-shaming that damages self-image and promotes an impossible standard of looks.

Q12. (i) He was at a PHOTOGRAPHY STUDIO, where he had gone to GET HIS PICTURE TAKEN. The photographer's pride was the pride of a craftsman in a finished job: he had disliked the narrator's face, but he had, by his own methods, 'improved' it into something he considered a success.

(ii) 'Proof' means a TRIAL PRINT — a first sample impression made so that it can be checked before the final photographs are produced. The narrator asked 'Is it me?' because he COULD NOT RECOGNISE HIMSELF: everything had been altered, and the picture bore no resemblance to his actual face.

(iii) The EYES, the EYEBROWS, the FOREHEAD and HAIRLINE, and the MOUTH had all been retouched or redrawn.

(iv) Only his EARS still looked like his own. The photographer proposed to remove even these — by 'the Sulphide' process he would take the ears out entirely and substitute new ones.

(v) He is furious because the photograph has NONE of his real features left in it — it is not a portrait of him at all, but a stranger's face imposed on him. The outburst is justified: his patience had been pushed past its limit by an hour of criticism and contortion; he felt HUMILIATED and OFFENDED by the needless 'corrections'; and the whole exercise was an attack on his sense of his own worth and identity — he leaves saying he will take the picture as it is, blotches and all, because at least it would be him.

C. Short Answer Type

Q13. He is a small man with a dome-like forehead and a wandering eye, and he speaks in the flat, absent manner of a man whose attention is on his equipment rather than on his customer.

His manner is one of complete professional authority. He inspects the narrator's face as a workman inspects unpromising material, announces its defects one by one without embarrassment, and issues instructions — turn the head, droop the ears, roll the eyes, close the mouth, alter the expression — as though the man were a piece of apparatus to be adjusted.

He is also entirely without malice, which is what makes him funny rather than unpleasant. He is not insulting the narrator; he simply does not think of him as a person with feelings about his own face. And he is proud of his retouching, presenting the removal of the ears and the substitution of new eyebrows as an achievement he expects to be thanked for.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q14. He begins nervous and eager to please. He has come voluntarily to be photographed, waits an hour without complaint, and when told his face is quite wrong agrees at once — 'I know; I have always known it' — and humbly asks whether anything can be done.

Through the sitting he is submissive to the point of absurdity. He turns his head, droops his ears, rolls his eyes under the lids, opens and closes his mouth to order, and asks anxious little questions about whether he has got it right. His only resistance is a mild attempt at conversation, which the photographer ignores.

The change comes with the proof. Seeing a picture in which the ears have been removed, the eyebrows replaced and the mouth altered, he finally speaks for himself, and the meekness turns into something close to dignity: this is his face, he has had it for years, he knows its faults better than anyone, and he will not have it made over. He refuses the photograph, tells the photographer to keep it, and goes out.

The arc is the joke and also the point. A man is comic while he apologises for his own face and stops being comic the moment he defends it.

D. Long Answer / Essay Type

Q15. The surface is pure farce. A man waits an hour, is told his face is quite wrong, is instructed to droop his ears and roll his eyes, and receives at last a photograph of somebody else. The comic machinery is Leacock's usual apparatus: understatement, absurd simile, a deadpan narrator, and an escalating sequence in which each new indignity is accepted more meekly than the last.

But the story is built to arrive somewhere. What the photographer actually does is remove, one by one, every feature that made the face recognisable — the ears, the eyebrows, the mouth, the expression — and then present the result as an improvement. He is not being cruel; he genuinely believes he has made something better, and he expects gratitude.

The narrator's outburst at the end is where the seriousness surfaces: 'It is my face. It is mine.' What he is defending is not his looks, which he has cheerfully admitted are poor, but his right to be the particular imperfect person he is. Leacock has quietly set up a small argument about individuality and the pressure to be improved into conformity, and he lets a joke carry it.

He is also honest about the narrator's complicity. The man submits for pages, because he too would rather look better than look like himself, and he objects only when nothing of him is left. The satire cuts both ways — at the expert who thinks he knows best, and at the vanity in all of us that lets him.

That is characteristic of Leacock. He is a humorist rather than a satirist proper; he does not attack, and nobody in the story is villainous. He simply arranges an absurd situation until a real observation falls out of it, and then stops.

E. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q16. Four features carry the story.

The deadpan first-person voice. Everything outrageous is reported calmly, as though it were reasonable, and the gap between what happens and the tone in which it is told is where the laughter comes from.

The comic simile. The photographer looks into the narrator's face 'as a man looks into an empty well'; the narrator waits an hour reading a magazine and reviewing his whole life. Each image is exact, unexpected and slightly too large for its occasion.

The technical vocabulary applied to a person. The face is 'ovoid', the head 'oblique'; the ears must be drooped, the eyes rolled, the expression altered. Treating a man in the language of apparatus is the story's central joke and also its argument.

Short sentences and dialogue that runs on rails. The exchanges are clipped, with the photographer issuing instructions and the narrator supplying small anxious questions — 'Is my mouth to be open or shut?' — so that the rhythm of the prose is itself comic.

The closing paragraph changes register completely, into short declarative sentences of protest, and that change of style is how Leacock signals that the joke has turned into a point.
