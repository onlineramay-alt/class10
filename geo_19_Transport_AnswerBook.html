The syllabus asks for the importance of transport and for the advantages and disadvantages of each of the four modes — roadways, railways, airways and waterways. Questions are almost always comparative, so learn the four as a set of trade-offs rather than as four separate lists.

A. Multiple Choice Questions

Q1. (d) Inland waterways — water transport is by far the cheapest per tonne-kilometre, because a vessel needs no track to be built or maintained and very little fuel for the load it carries. Its drawback is that it is the slowest and can only serve places on a navigable river or canal.

B. Short Answer Type

Q2. Transport is what turns a collection of regions into an economy. It carries RAW MATERIALS to the factory and FINISHED GOODS to the market, so no industry can exist without it; it moves food grain from the surplus regions to the deficit ones, which is what prevents local scarcity from becoming famine.

It makes AGRICULTURE commercial rather than subsistence, because a farmer will only grow a surplus if he can sell it. It gives access to EDUCATION, HEALTH CARE, employment and administration, and so binds remote regions into national life. It is essential to DEFENCE, allowing troops and supplies to be moved to the frontiers. It develops backward areas, since industry follows the road and the railway.

And in a country of India's size and diversity it is a force for NATIONAL INTEGRATION, allowing people of different regions and languages to travel, trade and mix, which is why the railways in particular are said to have helped make India one country.

Q3. ROADWAYS. Advantages — they provide DOOR-TO-DOOR service, which no other mode can, so they are indispensable for the first and last stage of every journey; they are cheaper to build and maintain than a railway; they can be laid in hilly, broken and remote country where a railway cannot go; they suit short distances, perishable goods and small consignments; and they are flexible, with no fixed timetable. Disadvantages — unsuited to very heavy or bulky loads over long distances; costly per tonne-kilometre for long hauls; subject to congestion, accidents and breakdown; badly affected by rain, flood and fog; and a major source of air pollution.

RAILWAYS. Advantages — much cheaper for HEAVY AND BULKY goods over LONG distances, such as coal, ore, cement, food grain and steel; can carry very large volumes of passengers at low fares; faster and safer than road over long hauls; less affected by weather; and more fuel-efficient and less polluting per tonne carried. Disadvantages — enormously expensive to construct and maintain; cannot provide door-to-door service, so goods must be transhipped at both ends; cannot easily be built in hilly, marshy or desert country; inflexible, running only on fixed routes and timetables; and uneconomic for short distances and small loads.

The two are complementary rather than competing: the railway does the long haul, the road does the collection and distribution at each end.

Q4. AIRWAYS. Advantages — by far the FASTEST mode, which makes it indispensable for long distances, for urgent freight, for perishables and for mail; it is the only practicable mode over difficult terrain — mountains, deserts, dense forest — and to remote regions such as the north-east and the island territories; it needs no continuous surface route, only two airports; and it is invaluable in disaster relief and defence. Disadvantages — much the most EXPENSIVE, in fare and in freight; can carry only small quantities and light goods; badly affected by bad weather, and accidents are usually fatal; requires very large capital investment in aircraft and airports; and is a heavy consumer of fuel and a source of pollution.

INLAND WATERWAYS. Advantages — the CHEAPEST mode of all, since no track has to be built or maintained and fuel consumption per tonne is very low; ideal for heavy, bulky, non-perishable goods such as coal, ore, timber, cement and food grain; and the most environmentally friendly, causing little pollution and no land acquisition. Disadvantages — the SLOWEST; confined to the course of navigable rivers and canals, so it can serve only places on the bank; in India many rivers are seasonal, silted or shallow, and water is drawn off for irrigation, so navigability is limited; and it needs terminal transport at both ends.

India's National Waterways — the Ganga (NW-1), the Brahmaputra (NW-2) and the West Coast Canal (NW-3) among others — remain greatly under-used compared with those of Europe and China.

Q5. Because the railway, however extensive, can never reach the village. India has more than six hundred thousand villages and only a few thousand railway stations, so the road is the only means by which the great majority of people and produce actually move; the railway carries goods between stations, but a road must carry them to and from the station at each end.

Roads also go where railways cannot. In the Himalayas, in the desert of Rajasthan, in the forests of central India and the hills of the north-east, the gradients, the cost and the terrain make a railway impossible, and a road is the only link.

Roads are besides far cheaper and quicker to build, so they can be extended rapidly — which matters for a developing country — and they suit the short hauls, small consignments and perishable goods that make up most of India's internal trade. Programmes such as the Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana and the Golden Quadrilingual highway network reflect this: India's road network is now among the largest in the world and carries the bulk of both passenger and freight traffic.
