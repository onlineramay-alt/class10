B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q5. (i) He is speaking to Brutus, whom he has drawn aside from the Lupercal procession. He has been working to persuade Brutus to join a conspiracy against Caesar, by convincing him that Caesar has grown too powerful for Rome's liberty and that Brutus is the man Rome looks to.

(ii) The Colossus was the enormous bronze statue at Rhodes, said to have stood astride the harbour so that ships passed between its legs. Cassius means that Caesar has grown so great that other Romans are dwarfed by him and must creep about beneath him. The image grants Caesar's greatness while resenting it: what the speaker feels is not admiration but humiliation at being one of the 'petty men'.

(iii) That their subjection is not fated or written in the heavens but is their own doing — they are underlings because they have consented to be. It is an appeal to Brutus's will: if the condition is self-made, it can be self-remedied, and by action rather than complaint.

(iv) Any two of: the swimming match in the Tiber, when Caesar challenged Cassius and then cried 'Help me, Cassius, or I sink!' so that Cassius had to carry him out, as Aeneas carried Anchises; and Caesar's fever in Spain, when he shook, his lips lost colour, and he groaned and cried 'Give me some drink, Titinius' like a sick girl.

(v) It reveals a man of great persuasive skill and considerable envy. He is a shrewd judge of character — he flatters Brutus's name and his ancestry, and appeals to honour rather than to interest, because he knows that is the only lever that will move him. But the stories he chooses betray him: they prove only that Caesar is physically frail, which has nothing to do with whether he would be a tyrant. The argument is really personal — 'I was born free as Caesar; so were you' — and it is the resentment of an equal who cannot bear to be surpassed. He is honest with himself about this: in the soliloquy that closes the scene he admits he is seducing Brutus, and that Brutus's noble metal may be wrought from that it is disposed.

Q6. (i) He is returning from the Lupercal games, where Antony has three times offered him a crown and he has three times refused it, fainting afterwards before the crowd. He is passing through the street with his train when he notices Cassius and draws Antony aside to speak of him.

(ii) He distrusts leanness, wakefulness and thought. Cassius reads much, observes others closely, sees through the deeds of men, and — the detail Caesar fixes on — seldom smiles, and smiles in such a sort as if he mocked himself for being moved to smile at anything. Such a man can never be at ease while another is greater than himself, and is therefore dangerous.

(iii) It is half a joke and wholly serious. The wish is absurd — Caesar cannot fatten Cassius — and the humour is his way of holding at arm's length a fear he has just admitted. That is exactly the tone of the whole speech: shrewdness dressed as banter.

(iv) That he is more afraid than he will allow. A man who is genuinely unafraid does not say so twice, nor add the elaborate condition 'if my name were liable to fear'. He speaks of himself in the third person, as 'Caesar', as though the public figure could not admit what the man privately feels. His judgement of Cassius is entirely correct; his refusal to act on it is what destroys him.

(v) It shows a Caesar of penetrating judgement who is nonetheless imprisoned by his own image. He sees Cassius exactly as we have just seen him, yet does nothing, because to take precautions would be to admit fear. The same conflict destroys him twice more. In Act II Scene ii he is persuaded by Calpurnia's dream to stay at home, and then reverses himself the moment Decius suggests the Senate will mock him — 'How foolish do your fears seem now, Calpurnia!' And in Act III Scene i, moments before his death, he boasts that he is 'constant as the northern star', unmoved by prayer — the very inflexibility that keeps him from hearing Artemidorus. The tragedy lies in the gap between what Caesar perceives and what Caesar permits himself to do.

Q7. Because the commoners have left their work and put on their best clothes to line the streets and cheer Caesar's triumph. The tribunes are angry on two counts. First, the triumph is not over a foreign enemy but over the sons of Pompey — Romans — so it celebrates a victory in a civil war. Second, and more bitterly, these are the same crowds who once climbed walls and chimney-tops to cheer Pompey himself; Marullus rebukes them as 'you blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things' for their fickleness. Their political fear is that Caesar's growing popularity will 'soar above the view of men / And keep us all in servile fearfulness'.

Q8. Casca tells it with heavy sarcasm. Mark Antony offered Caesar a crown — 'one of these coronets', as Casca dismissively calls it — and Caesar put it by with the back of his hand; but Casca thought he was very loath to lay his fingers off it. The offer was made three times, and each time the rabble shouted and clapped their chapped hands and threw up their sweaty night-caps, and Caesar refused more reluctantly than before.

At the third refusal Caesar swooned and fell down in the market-place, foaming at the mouth and speechless. When he came to himself he offered the crowd his throat to cut, and — Casca notes — if he had been a common man they would have taken him at his word. The crowd forgave him because they loved him, 'as they use to do the players in the theatre'.

Casca adds two further pieces of news: Cicero spoke, but in Greek, so those who understood smiled at one another and shook their heads and 'it was Greek to me'; and Marullus and Flavius have been 'put to silence' for pulling scarves off Caesar's images.

Q9. It performs three functions at once. As atmosphere, it fills the night before the assassination with violence and unnaturalness — fire dropping from heaven, a slave whose hand burned without being scorched, a lion in the Capitol, an owl hooting at noon in the market-place.

As characterisation, it separates the men. Casca is terrified and reads the portents as the gods' anger. Cicero, sceptical, observes that men construe things after their fashion, clean from the purpose of the things themselves. Cassius walks bare-chested through it, offering his breast to the thunder-stone — partly courage, partly theatre — and turns the whole storm into an argument, claiming heaven has sent it as a warning against 'a man no mightier than thyself or me' who has grown prodigious and fearful.

As structure, it is where the conspiracy is made. Cassius wins Casca in the storm, sends Cinna with forged letters to Brutus's window, and by the end of the scene has all but secured Brutus — 'him and all the rest' by morning.

Q10. By working on Brutus's honour and his name rather than on his interests. In conversation he flatters him, reminds him of his ancestor who drove the Tarquin king from Rome, and insists that Rome looks to Brutus for its liberty.

He then adds a deception. In his soliloquy he resolves to throw in at Brutus's windows, in several hands, writings 'as if they came from several citizens', all praising Brutus's reputation and hinting obscurely at Caesar's ambition. In Act I Scene iii he gives these papers to Cinna, with instructions to set one on the praetor's chair, throw one in at Brutus's window, and fix a third with wax on old Brutus's statue.

The method reveals both men. Cassius knows that Brutus will not be moved by envy or by argument, but only by the belief that Rome herself is calling him — and he is willing to manufacture that call. He admits as much: 'Well, Brutus, thou art noble; yet I see / Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed.'

D. Long Answer / Essay Type

Q11. Shakespeare gives us Caesar almost entirely at second hand in this act, and the reports contradict one another.

The case for greatness is real. He has conquered; the crowds fill the streets for him; Antony says simply, 'When Caesar says "Do this," it is perform'd.' He publicly refuses a crown three times, which is at least the outward form of republican modesty. And his judgement is formidable — his reading of Cassius in Scene ii is the shrewdest piece of character analysis in the act, and entirely accurate.

The case against is built up as carefully. Marullus and Flavius, the only voices from outside the great world, fear that he will 'keep us all in servile fearfulness'. He is superstitious enough to have Calpurnia stand in Antony's way during the race. He is deaf in one ear, and asks Antony to come to his other side — a small, real frailty in a man being called a Colossus. And Casca's account suggests that the refusal of the crown was a performance, with Caesar loath to lay his fingers off it.

But the audience is also taught to distrust the witnesses. Casca is a sour man who despises the crowd. Cassius is openly envious, and his two anecdotes — the swimming match, the fever in Spain — prove only that Caesar is mortal, which was never in question. Cicero's line about men construing things after their fashion is placed in this act as a warning about all the testimony in it.

The effect is deliberate. By the end of Act I we have heard Caesar called a Colossus, a sick girl, a tyrant in the making and the saviour of Rome, and we have seen too little of him to settle the question. That uncertainty is what makes the assassination in Act III a genuine moral problem rather than either a murder or a rescue — and it is why Brutus's decision, taken on what 'he may' become rather than on what he has done, is so uneasy.

Q12. The contrast is established in their first long conversation and holds for the rest of the play.

Cassius is a man of action, of grievance, and of penetrating practical intelligence. He begins the conversation, steers it, and closes it having got what he wanted. His motive is personal: he was born as free as Caesar, fed as well, can endure the winter's cold as well, and cannot bear that such a man should have become a god while Cassius is a wretched creature who must bend his body if Caesar carelessly but nod. He is a shrewd reader of others — Caesar's judgement of him is exactly matched by his own use of Brutus — and he is not scrupulous, since he is willing to forge letters to secure his end.

Brutus is reflective, honourable, and slow. He is 'with himself at war' before Cassius says a word, and neglects the shows of love he was wont to have. He is moved not by grievance but by principle — 'If it be aught toward the general good, / Set honour in one eye and death i' the other, / And I will look on both indifferently.' He loves Caesar personally, and says so; his difficulty is entirely public.

The crucial difference is in self-knowledge. Cassius sees himself clearly and admits, alone on stage, that he is seducing a better man than himself. Brutus does not see that he is being worked upon at all: he takes the forged letters for the voice of Rome. Cassius's flaw is envy, and he knows it; Brutus's flaw is a confidence in his own judgement so complete that he cannot recognise manipulation.

That pattern decides everything later. Cassius will be right about sparing Antony, right about letting Antony speak, and right about Philippi; Brutus will overrule him every time, and every time will be wrong. The seeds of the tragedy are all in this first conversation.

E. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q13. Act I sets the two views against each other and refuses to decide between them.

The case for free will is put by Cassius: 'The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings.' Men at some time are masters of their fates, and Rome's condition is the work of Romans. On this view the conspiracy is a free act of political will.

Against it stands almost everything else in the act. The soothsayer's 'Beware the ides of March' will be exactly fulfilled. The storm of Scene iii is full of portents that Casca reads as the gods' warning. And Cicero's sceptical reply — that men construe things after their fashion, clean from the purpose of the things themselves — does not dismiss the omens so much as warn that we cannot read them.

Shakespeare's arrangement is pointed. Cassius denies the stars in Scene ii and spends Scene iii interpreting portents to suit his argument; the man who says fate is nothing uses fate as a debating instrument. And the audience knows the ending: the soothsayer is right, and every character who dismisses him is wrong.

What the play finally suggests, and what Act I plants, is that the two are not opposites. The prophecy comes true because Caesar chooses to ignore it. Men act freely, and their free acts accomplish exactly what was foretold.

Q14. The crowd is introduced in the first thirty lines of the play, and its character is fixed there for good.

We meet them as individuals first — a carpenter, and a cobbler who parries Marullus's questions with a string of puns about mending soles and being a surgeon to old shoes. The wit is genuine and likeable, and it establishes that these are people, not a mob.

Then Marullus turns on them and the other side appears. These same men once climbed walls and towers and chimney-tops with their infants in their arms to sit all day waiting to see Pompey pass; now they strew flowers in the way of the man who triumphs over Pompey's blood. Rebuked, they go home at once — 'they vanish tongue-tied in their guiltiness' — which shows how easily they are turned.

Casca's report completes the picture and adds contempt: the rabble clapped their chapped hands and threw up their sweaty night-caps and uttered such a deal of stinking breath that it almost choked Caesar; and they forgave his fainting 'as they use to do the players in the theatre'.

The purpose is preparation. Everything the audience needs in order to believe Act III Scene ii — where Antony turns the same crowd against Brutus in a few hundred lines, and where they tear Cinna the poet to pieces for his name — has been established here. Shakespeare has shown us a crowd that is warm, quick, funny and utterly changeable, so that when it changes, we are not surprised but appalled.
