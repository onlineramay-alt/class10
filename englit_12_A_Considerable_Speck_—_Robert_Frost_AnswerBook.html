B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q3. (i) He is writing — the poem is set at his desk, with a manuscript sheet in front of him — and he notices something moving across the page. He first takes it for a speck of dust or a smudge of dry ink, until it begins to move with evident purpose.

(ii) It runs, then stops; it seems to hesitate, to consider, and to change direction. It appears to be repelled by the wet ink and to shrink from the danger of the pen. It pauses as if to think, then sets off again, and finally settles down, exhausted, in an attitude the poet reads as resignation or trust. Every one of these is a movement Frost interprets as showing will and self-preservation rather than blind instinct — the mite plainly wants to live, and takes steps about it.

(iii) Because he recognises mind in it, and mind is the one thing he values. He is careful to say that he has no love for the creature and no sentimental scruple about killing small things; his reason is intellectual, not tender. Having spent his life looking for intelligence, he will not destroy an instance of it merely because it is very small.

(iv) The obvious meaning is the sheet of paper on which the mite is crawling. The second meaning is the sheet of writing — the manuscript itself — so that the poet is also saying, wryly, how rare it is to find any display of mind on a written page. The joke turns the compliment: the mite has shown more intelligence on that sheet than most of what is written on such sheets.

(v) Two targets, and the second is the sharper.

The first is the modern preference for collective thinking over individual thought. Frost says explicitly that the mite is no 'tenant' of the collective, and elsewhere in the poem sets himself against 'the tenets of collectivistic regimenting love' — a dig at ideologies, fashionable in the 1930s and 1940s, that subordinate the individual mind to the mass.

The second is the general scarcity of thought. The whole poem is built so that its climax — a declaration of delight at finding intelligence — is provoked by a mite on a page. To be that pleased by that little is a comment on how seldom the poet meets mind anywhere else, including in what people write. Frost keeps the tone light and slightly self-mocking throughout, so the satire lands without ever becoming a complaint.

C. Short Answer Type

Q4. The title is an oxymoron and a joke. A speck is by definition negligible — the smallest thing that can be seen — and 'considerable' means large or important. Putting the two together announces the poem's argument before it begins.

The word also carries its root sense: this is a speck worth *considering*, one that repays attention. Frost's subtitle in some printings — 'Microscopic' — reinforces the play.

And the paradox is the poem's conclusion: something physically negligible turns out to be significant, because it displays mind, and mind is what makes anything considerable. The title states the case that the twenty-odd lines then argue.

Q5. Chiefly through disproportion. An entire poem, with a philosophical conclusion, is devoted to a mite on a piece of paper; the grandeur of the reflection and the smallness of its object are comically mismatched, and Frost knows it.

He also uses mock-solemnity. The mite is described in the language of drama and moral choice — it hesitates, it seems terrified, it takes a stand, it lies down in resignation — as though the poet were narrating a crisis rather than watching an insect.

There is self-mockery too. The declaration 'No one can know how glad I am to find / On any sheet the least display of mind' is funny at the speaker's own expense: a man so starved of intelligent company that a mite makes his day.

And the punning double sense of 'sheet' delivers the joke in one word, without underlining it.

D. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q6. SUBJECT. A poet at his desk notices a mite crawling across his page, watches it behave as though it were thinking, and spares it — because he honours mind wherever he finds it. From that trivial incident Frost builds a reflection on intelligence, individuality and the value of the small.

FORM. Rhyming couplets in a conversational iambic line. The couplet is traditionally the form of wit and epigram, and Frost uses it accordingly: the rhymes click shut on the jokes ('recognize / guise', 'find / mind'), and the closing quatrain has the finality of an epigram.

TONE. This is the poem's real distinction. It is light, dry, self-aware and never earnest, and it holds that tone while making a serious claim. Frost is careful to disclaim sentiment — he does not spare the mite out of pity, and says so — which prevents the poem from becoming a homily about not hurting living things.

THEME. Two ideas are joined. The first is that mind is what confers value, regardless of size or station: a thinking speck deserves more respect than an unthinking bulk. The second, made explicit in Frost's dismissal of 'collectivistic regimenting love', is a defence of the individual mind against ideologies that would submerge it in the mass — a live subject in 1942, when the poem was published.

IMAGERY AND OBSERVATION. The poem's success rests on the accuracy of the watching. The mite's hesitations, its recoil from the wet ink, its pause as if to consider, its final collapse — these are precisely observed and are what make the fanciful interpretation plausible.

CRITICAL COMMENT. It is a minor Frost poem and knows it, but the craft is complete: the title states the paradox, the observation earns the claim, and the last couplet lands the point with a pun. Its risk is whimsy — the reader must be willing to accept a great deal of interpretation laid on a very small insect — and Frost disarms that objection by making the disproportion part of the joke.
