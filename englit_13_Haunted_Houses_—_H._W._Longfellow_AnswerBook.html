Longfellow uses it to say that the dead still own the estates we think are ours: 'The spirit-world around this world of sense… / The dead hold the estates in mortmain still.'

B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q3. (i) Memory and association. The stranger sees only what is physically present — 'he but perceives what is' — because he has no past in that house. The poet, who has lived there, sees everything that has been: the people who once sat by that fire, the events that took place in those rooms. The difference is not that the poet has supernatural sight but that he has a history in the place.

(ii) Simply the fact that people have lived and died in it. The poem's opening states the whole thesis: 'All houses wherein men have lived and died / Are haunted houses.' There is no need for a violent death or an unquiet spirit. Any house with a past has its phantoms, and by that definition every house is haunted.

(iii) Everywhere in the ordinary spaces of the house, going about ordinary business. They glide through the open doors with feet that make no sound upon the floors; they are met along the passages and on the stairs; and at table there are 'more guests… than the hosts / Invited', an illuminated hall thronged with quiet, inoffensive ghosts as silent as the pictures on the wall. Longfellow deliberately puts them in the dining room and the corridor rather than the attic or the vault.

(iv) That ownership is an illusion. We hold our title-deeds by will and testament, but a stronger and older claim overrides them: the dead hold the estates in mortmain still, and the owner of a house is only its present occupant. What we think we possess we merely borrow from those who lived there before us, and shall pass on to those who come after.

(v) Traditionally ghosts are frightening — vengeful, malevolent, appearing at night to terrify the living, associated with unfinished business and violent death. Longfellow's are the opposite in every respect: harmless, quiet, inoffensive, silent, going about their errands, no more alarming than the portraits on the wall. They do not haunt in order to disturb; they are simply still there.

The mood is meditative and gently melancholy rather than fearful. One reason is the poem's diction — 'harmless', 'quiet', 'inoffensive', 'silent', 'noiseless' — which systematically removes menace; another is the measured, unhurried movement of the quatrains, which reads like reflection by a fire rather than narration of an event. The feeling the poem leaves is not fear of the dead but a sober awareness of how thoroughly the past inhabits the present.

Official Answer

Q4. (i) As quiet, inoffensive and silent — as unobtrusive as the portraits hanging on the wall, and no more threatening. Traditionally ghosts are objects of terror: they appear to frighten, they seek revenge, they are associated with darkness, violence and unfinished business. Longfellow's are simply present, harmless, and going about their own errands with feet that make no sound.

(ii) It brings to mind a lit dining hall with a table of invited guests, and, filling the same room unseen, a far larger company of the dead who once sat there — so that the living are always outnumbered in their own house. The effect is domestic rather than eerie.

Strangers cannot see them because seeing them depends on having a past in the house. The phantoms are made of memory and association; a visitor 'but perceives what is', while the person who has lived there sees all that has been.

(iii) The poet compares the spirit world to an atmosphere — it 'floats like an atmosphere' around this world of sense, wherein our little lives are kept in equipoise. What connects the two is described as a bridge of light: 'a bridge of light, / Connecting it with the greater world beyond', over which our thoughts pass as over a floating bridge, a slender span thrown from the world we know to the world we do not.

(iv) Mortmain means 'the dead hand'. In law it is the perpetual holding of property by a body that never dies, so that the land can never be alienated. The dead hold the estates in mortmain — that is, those who lived and died in our houses still have the older and stronger title, and our deeds and testaments do not really make the place ours.

(v) The instinct that enjoys and the instinct that aspires — the earthly and the spiritual. 'Our little lives are kept in equipoise / By opposite attractions and desires; / The struggle of the instinct that enjoys, / And the more noble instinct that aspires.' The first draws us to the pleasures of the world of sense; the second reaches towards the world beyond. Longfellow does not condemn the first: the word is 'equipoise' — balance — and his argument is that a life is held steady by the tension between them, not by the victory of either.

C. Short Answer Type

Q5. That every house in which people have lived and died is haunted — not by frightening spirits, but by the past itself. The dead remain present as memory and association, moving through the rooms on their errands, silent and harmless, visible to those who have a history in the place and invisible to strangers.

From this Longfellow draws a wider claim. If the dead are always with us, then the living never really own anything: 'the dead hold the estates in mortmain still.' The spirit world surrounds the world of the senses like an atmosphere, connected to it by a bridge of light along which our thoughts pass; and our lives are held in balance between the instinct that enjoys the present world and the nobler instinct that aspires beyond it.

The poem is therefore not a ghost story but a meditation on memory, inheritance and mortality.

Q6. It is the poem's central image for the relation between the seen and the unseen. Longfellow imagines a slender span thrown across from this world to 'the greater world beyond' — a floating bridge of light, over which our thoughts, our aspirations and our sense of the dead continually pass.

Three things make it effective. It is made of light, which is insubstantial, so it suggests a connection that is real but cannot be walked on with the body — only thought crosses it. It floats, and so has no foundations on either side, which conveys how uncertain and unanchored our knowledge of that world is. And it is a bridge, not a wall, which is the whole argument of the poem: the two worlds are not sealed off from each other, and the traffic between them is constant.

The image also gives the poem its resolution. Having spent six stanzas among the domestic dead, Longfellow lifts the poem outward at the end, from the dining hall and the staircase to the atmosphere and the span of light — from memory to metaphysics.

D. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q7. SUBJECT. Longfellow takes a familiar word — 'haunted' — and quietly redefines it. Every house where people have lived and died is haunted; the ghosts are memory and association; and from that domestic starting point the poem moves outward to inheritance, mortality and the relation of this world to the next.

STRUCTURE. It falls into two movements. The first four or five stanzas are concrete and domestic: the open doors, the noiseless feet, the crowded dining hall, the phantoms met upon the stairs. The last stanzas become abstract and metaphysical: mortmain, the spirit-world floating like an atmosphere, the bridge of light, the equipoise of the two instincts. The movement from the fireside to the cosmos is the poem's shape.

FORM. Quatrains rhyming ABAB, in a measured iambic line. The regularity is part of the meaning: the metre is calm, unhurried and slightly formal, the movement of a mind reflecting rather than of a man startled. Nothing in the rhythm ever quickens.

DICTION AND IMAGERY. The vocabulary is chosen to remove fear — harmless, quiet, inoffensive, silent, noiseless. The similes are domestic ('as silent as the pictures on the wall') until the closing stanzas, where they become expansive (the atmosphere, the bridge of light). The legal term 'mortmain' is the one hard word in the poem, and it is placed exactly where the argument turns from description to claim.

TONE AND MOOD. Meditative, melancholy, and finally consoling. There is loss in the poem — we do not own our houses, our lives are little — but no dread, and the closing image of the bridge is a hopeful one.

CRITICAL COMMENT. The poem's achievement is that it earns a large abstract statement out of a small domestic observation, and never raises its voice to do it. Its weakness, some readers feel, is the same thing: the last stanzas explain what the first stanzas had already shown, and the poem is arguably at its best in the dining hall rather than on the bridge.
