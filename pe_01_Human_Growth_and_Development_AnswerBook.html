B. Very Short Answer

Q2. GROWTH is the QUANTITATIVE increase in the body — an increase in height, weight, size and the number of cells. It can be measured and expressed in figures, and it STOPS at a certain age, usually around maturity.

DEVELOPMENT is the QUALITATIVE change in the body and mind — the improvement in the functioning, coordination, skill and maturity of the organs and of the personality. It cannot be measured in figures, and it CONTINUES throughout life.

The short form: growth is a change in SIZE, development is a change in CAPACITY. A child grows taller (growth) and also learns to run, balance and judge distance (development).

C. Short Answer Type

Q4. (i) HEREDITARY FACTORS. The characteristics a child inherits from the parents through the genes — height, body build, bone structure, colour, and to a considerable extent the potential for strength, speed and stamina. A child of tall parents is likely to be tall. Heredity fixes the POTENTIAL; it does not by itself decide the outcome.

(ii) ENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS. The surroundings in which the child grows — climate, housing, sanitation, pollution, family atmosphere, schooling and the opportunity to play. A child with space and encouragement to be active develops motor skills that a confined child does not, whatever the inherited potential.

(iii) NUTRITION. The single most important controllable factor. A balanced diet supplying adequate carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals and water is essential for the building of tissue and bone; undernutrition stunts growth and delays maturity, and overnutrition brings obesity and its associated problems.

(iv) GENDER. Boys and girls follow different patterns and different timings. Girls generally begin their growth spurt about two years earlier than boys and mature sooner; boys eventually attain greater height, muscle mass and strength. The differences become marked at adolescence.

(A fifth factor in the syllabus is NATIONALITY or race, which influences average body build and physical characteristics; and exercise, rest and illness may be added.)

Q5. CHARACTERISTICS. Adolescence, from about 12 to 19 years, is the period of transition from childhood to adulthood, and it is marked by rapid change on every side.

PHYSICAL — a sudden growth spurt in height and weight; the appearance of secondary sexual characteristics; the maturing of the reproductive organs; increases in strength, endurance and coordination, though limbs may grow faster than muscle so that the adolescent is temporarily clumsy.

MENTAL — the capacity for abstract reasoning, imagination and independent judgement develops; curiosity and the desire to question authority increase.

EMOTIONAL — feelings are intense and unstable; there is self-consciousness about appearance, and mood changes are frequent.

SOCIAL — the peer group becomes more important than the family; there is a strong desire for acceptance, for independence, and for hero-worship of admired figures.

WHY IT MATTERS TO A TEACHER. Because this is when lifelong habits of activity are formed, and when they are most easily lost. Training loads must be matched to a body that is still growing, since bones and growth plates are vulnerable to overuse injury. The self-consciousness of the age means an adolescent humiliated in front of peers may avoid sport for life, while one given responsibility and success may keep it. And the pull of the peer group can be used constructively, through team games that channel the need for belonging into cooperation and discipline.
