B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q4. (i) Cassius has proposed that they should not go to Philippi but wait where they are and let the enemy come to them. Brutus argues for marching at once to meet Octavius and Antony at Philippi.

(ii) Human affairs, like the sea, have a rising and a falling. There comes a moment when the tide is full and a ship can be floated off and carried out; if that moment is let slip, the water falls and the ship is left stranded in shallow places for the rest of its voyage. Brutus means that opportunity comes once, and that this is the moment.

(iii) Two practical arguments beneath the imagery. First, the people between Philippi and their present position are only friends by compulsion, having grudgingly given contributions; the enemy marching through them will be refreshed with new-added men and better spirits. Second, their own forces are at their peak — 'Our legions are brim-full, our cause is ripe' — and from now on they can only decline. To wait, therefore, is to grow weaker while the enemy grows stronger.

(iv) Cassius proves right, as he does every time. At Philippi the battle is lost; Cassius kills himself on a false report, and Brutus follows two scenes later. Cassius's own last words, over his sword, tell us so: 'Caesar, thou art revenged, / Even with the sword that kill'd thee.' Brutus's argument was persuasive and wrong.

(v) It completes a pattern established in Act II and repeats it for the fourth and last time. Cassius has been right about the oath, right about killing Antony, right about the funeral oration, and is now right about Philippi; and each time Brutus has overruled him and each time Cassius has yielded.

The reason he yields is what makes the relationship moving rather than merely unequal. This scene contains their quarrel and their reconciliation: Cassius has offered Brutus his dagger and his bare breast — 'Strike, as thou didst at Caesar' — and Brutus has just told him of Portia's death, which silences Cassius entirely. Their affection is real, and it is precisely that affection which prevents the shrewder man from insisting on the better judgement. Brutus commands because he is the better man; Cassius obeys because he loves him; and the combination destroys them both.

Q5. (i) He is in his tent in the camp near Sardis, late at night before the march to Philippi. Varro and Claudius are asleep on cushions in the tent, and the boy Lucius has fallen asleep over his instrument with the music half played.

(ii) Everything in the scene has stripped him of company. He has quarrelled bitterly with Cassius and been reconciled; he has told Messala of Portia's death and then, with terrible self-command, pretended to hear it for the first time; he has overruled Cassius on Philippi; and now the last of his companions — even the boy playing to him — has fallen asleep. He is left awake and alone with a guttering candle, and it is at exactly that moment that the Ghost comes.

(iii) The Ghost says only that it is his evil spirit, and that Brutus shall see it at Philippi. Brutus replies steadily — 'Why, I will see thee at Philippi, then' — and when it vanishes calls at once for Lucius, Varro and Claudius, and asks whether they saw anything. Nobody has.

(iv) Because the play is more interested in what the assassination has done to Brutus than in the supernatural. The words make the apparition ambiguous: it may be Caesar's spirit walking abroad, as Cassius will say when he dies, or it may be Brutus's own conscience given a shape by exhaustion and a bad candle — he himself first supposes it is the weakness of his eyes. That no one else in the tent sees it supports the second reading. The phrase allows both at once, and refuses to settle whether Brutus is haunted by Caesar or by himself.

(v) Three functions. Structurally it points forward, and its single prophecy is fulfilled precisely, so it works as the last of the play's warnings. Thematically it closes the circle: Antony had predicted that Caesar's spirit would range for revenge, and here it is. And dramatically it marks the moment the tide turns. Up to this point Brutus has been active; from here he is a man moving towards a meeting already fixed. When Cassius dies he says 'O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet!', and when Brutus dies he says 'Caesar, now be still' — both looking back to this tent.

C. Short Answer Type

Q6. It begins with a specific charge and widens into a difference of principle.

Brutus accuses Cassius of having condemned and noted Lucius Pella for taking bribes from the Sardians, and of having ignored Brutus's letters written in his defence — and, more damagingly, of having 'an itching palm', of selling and marting offices for gold to undeservers. Cassius denies it furiously and says that had any other man spoken so, it would have been his last.

Brutus then turns the charge into an argument about the whole enterprise: they struck down the foremost man of all this world for justice' sake, and shall they now contaminate their fingers with base bribes? He would rather be a dog and bay the moon than such a Roman.

The second grievance is the reverse of the first, and shows the trap Brutus is in. He has refused to raise money by 'vile means' — wringing it from the hard hands of peasants — and has then sent to Cassius for gold to pay his legions, which Cassius denied him. Brutus condemns the means and demands the proceeds.

The quarrel is settled when Cassius bares his breast and offers his dagger, and Brutus relents. The real cause of Brutus's temper appears only afterwards: Portia is dead, and he has been carrying it alone.

Q7. He tells Cassius of it quietly, in the middle of their reconciliation, as the explanation of his ill temper: Portia is dead, having fallen distract with grief at his absence and at the growing strength of Octavius and Antony, and having swallowed fire. Cassius is appalled — 'How scaped I killing when I cross'd you so?' — and Brutus refuses to speak more of her, and calls for wine.

Then, when Messala arrives with letters and asks whether he has heard anything of his wife, Brutus says nothing of what he already knows, and makes Messala tell him — 'Why ask you? hear you aught of her in mine?' — and receives the news a second time as though it were new, answering: 'Why, farewell, Portia. We must die, Messala. / With meditating that she must die once, / I have the patience to endure it now.' Messala calls it the art of enduring great things greatly.

The conduct shows the Roman stoicism Brutus lives by, and its cost. He will not let private grief weaken the army's confidence in its general, so he performs composure for his officers. It is admirable and it is also a kind of self-erasure — the same suppression of feeling that let him kill a man he loved for a principle.

Q8. A chilling one, and it is only about fifty lines long.

They are pricking names on a list of those who shall die. Antony agrees to his own nephew Publius's death in exchange for Lepidus consenting to his brother's — 'He shall not live; look, with a spot I damn him.' The casualness is the point: the men who avenged a murder are now arranging murders by arithmetic.

We also see Antony's contempt for his own colleague. The moment Lepidus is sent off to fetch Caesar's will — so that they may cut off some charge in legacies, which is to say cheat the citizens of the bequest Antony had used so movingly at the funeral — he calls him a slight unmeritable man, meet to be sent on errands, and compares him to a horse to be trained, fed and turned off to graze when his usefulness ends.

Octavius, though young, is not deferential; he answers Antony levelly and observes that Lepidus is a tried and valiant soldier. The friction between them, glimpsed here and again in Act V Scene i, points beyond the play to the war that will follow.

The scene's purpose is comparative. It is placed immediately before the quarrel between Brutus and Cassius, so that we see two pairs of allies in the same act: one pair quarrelling bitterly and forgiving each other with real love, the other perfectly polite and wholly ruthless. Shakespeare leaves the judgement to us.

D. Long Answer / Essay Type

Q9. It is the longest scene in the play after the forum, and it has often been thought the finest.

Its first importance is in what it reveals about the two men. Cassius's anger is quick, loud and self-pitying — he is 'aweary of the world', hated by one he loves, braved by his brother, checked like a bondman. Brutus's is cold and moral, and he uses it to wound: 'Away, slight man!' The quarrel exposes what has been implicit since Act I — that Cassius needs Brutus's good opinion far more than Brutus needs his — and Cassius's surrender, offering his naked breast and his dagger, is the proof of it.

Its second importance is structural. Shakespeare has placed it immediately after the proscription scene, so that two partnerships are set side by side: Antony and Octavius, courteous and cold-blooded, calmly marking down relatives for death; Brutus and Cassius, tearing at each other and then weeping and drinking together. The comparison is entirely to the conspirators' advantage as men, and entirely to their disadvantage as politicians. Antony and Octavius will win.

Its third importance is that it prepares the end. The reconciliation is followed at once by Portia's death, by the decision for Philippi, and by the Ghost — so the scene moves from noisy anger through grief into silence and an omen. Brutus is progressively stripped: of his temper, of his wife, of Cassius's counsel, and finally of company altogether as everyone in the tent falls asleep.

Finally it deepens the play's central irony. The two men quarrel about bribery and honour — about whether the cause has stayed clean. That they are having this argument at all shows that it has not: they killed Caesar for justice' sake and are now discussing itching palms and undeservers. Brutus's own position is untenable, since he condemns the means and asks for the money. The revolution has begun to resemble what it replaced, and the scene lets the audience see that without a word of comment.

E. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q10. Friendship in this play is repeatedly the instrument of destruction, and Act IV is where that is seen most clearly.

Caesar dies of betrayed friendship. He resists his murderers until he sees Brutus, and 'Et tu, Brute?' turns the assassination from politics into personal grief; Antony afterwards calls the wound Brutus made 'the most unkindest cut of all', and it is that phrase, not any argument, which finally moves the crowd.

Brutus is brought into the conspiracy through friendship: Cassius works on him as a friend, and manufactures the letters that make Rome seem to be asking. Antony survives by counterfeiting friendship with the conspirators, shaking each bloody hand in turn while intending their ruin.

Act IV sets the two kinds of alliance against each other. Antony and Octavius are allies without affection — Antony disposes of his nephew in half a line and describes Lepidus, in his absence, as a horse to be turned off when done with. Brutus and Cassius are friends without policy: they quarrel savagely over money and honour, and are reconciled because Cassius cannot bear Brutus's contempt and Brutus cannot maintain his anger once he has admitted that Portia is dead.

The play's judgement is bleak and unsentimental. Affection is the finest thing in it and also the most disabling: it is because Cassius loves Brutus that he yields on Philippi against his own better judgement, and so loses the battle and his life. The men who feel nothing win. Yet the last word belongs to Antony over Brutus's body — 'This was the noblest Roman of them all' — which grants, from the victor's mouth, that winning was not the same as being right.
