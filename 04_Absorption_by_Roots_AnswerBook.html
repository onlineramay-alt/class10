Q41. (a) Imbibition: absorption of water by solid particles/colloids (e.g. dry seeds), causing them to swell, without forming a true solution. (b) Diffusion: movement of molecules of a substance from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration until evenly distributed. (c) Osmosis: movement of water molecules from a region of lower solute concentration to higher solute concentration through a semi-permeable membrane. (d) Osmotic Pressure: the pressure that must be applied to a solution to just prevent the inward flow of water across a semi-permeable membrane; it measures a solution's tendency to draw in water by osmosis. (e) Active transport: movement of ions/molecules across a cell membrane from a region of lower to higher concentration, using metabolic energy (ATP). (f) Tonicity: the relative solute concentration of two solutions, which determines the direction of osmotic water flow between them (hypotonic, hypertonic, isotonic). (g) Root pressure: the pressure developed in root xylem due to the active absorption of water and minerals by root cells, which pushes water a short way up the stem.

Long Answer Questions

Q66. The cell sap of a root hair is more concentrated than the soil water, so the root hair is hypertonic to it. Water therefore passes from the soil through the cell wall and the semipermeable cell membrane into the vacuole of the root hair by endosmosis, and the root hair becomes turgid. Its sap is now more dilute than that of the cell next to it, so water passes on into that cell by osmosis, and in this way it moves from cell to cell across the cortex, each cell in turn becoming a little more dilute than its inner neighbour. Finally the water reaches the xylem vessels, into which it is pushed partly by root pressure, and from there it is carried up the stem.

AI-generated answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q67. Endosmosis is the passage of water into a cell when it is surrounded by a solution more dilute than its cell sap, and the cell becomes turgid. Exosmosis is the passage of water out of a cell when it is surrounded by a solution more concentrated than its cell sap. Plasmolysis is the shrinking of the protoplast away from the cell wall that results from continued exosmosis. If a plasmolysed cell is placed in pure water, water enters by endosmosis, the protoplast swells and gradually returns to the cell wall, and the cell becomes turgid again; this recovery is called deplasmolysis and it can happen only if the cell has not been left plasmolysed so long that it has died.
