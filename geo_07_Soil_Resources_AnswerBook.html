Q3. It is called self-ploughing because of its high clay content. In the dry season the clay contracts and the soil develops wide, deep cracks; air and moisture penetrate through these cracks and the soil is aerated and turned over naturally, as ploughing would do. In the rains the clay swells and the cracks close.

Two minerals present: any two of iron, lime (calcium carbonate), magnesium, potash and alumina. Black soil is rich in these but deficient in nitrogen, phosphorus and humus.

Official Answer

Q4. (a) Alluvial soil is transported soil, formed of the silt, sand and clay brought down by rivers from the mountains and deposited in their valleys, flood plains and deltas. It is therefore not formed in place from the underlying rock, and it covers the whole of the northern plains and the coastal deltas.

(b) KHADAR is the new alluvium, deposited afresh by the river every year in the low-lying flood plain. It is fine in texture, light in colour, renewed annually and therefore highly fertile.

BHANGAR is the old alluvium, lying above the flood level on higher terraces away from the present course of the river. It is coarser, darker, often contains lime nodules called kankar, is not renewed by fresh deposits, and is consequently less fertile than khadar.

Official Answer

Q5. (a) LATERITE soil — heavy rain in a hot climate washes the silica and bases out of the topsoil, leaving the insoluble iron and aluminium oxides behind.
(b) RED soil — it is poor in nitrogen, phosphorus and humus and dries out easily, but it responds well to irrigation and to added fertilizer and then supports good crops of millets, groundnut, pulses and tobacco.
(c) BLACK soil — weathered from the basaltic lava of the Deccan Trap.

Q6. CAUSES. Deforestation — when the tree cover is cut, there are no roots to bind the soil and no canopy to break the force of the rain, so the topsoil is washed away. Overgrazing — cattle, sheep and goats crop the grass to the roots and trample the surface, leaving it bare and loose. (Also acceptable: faulty methods of cultivation such as ploughing up and down a slope; shifting cultivation; and the action of wind in arid regions.)

METHODS. CONTOUR PLOUGHING — ploughing along the contour rather than up and down the slope, so that each furrow holds the water instead of channelling it downhill. TERRACE FARMING — cutting steps into a hillside so that the slope is broken into level strips. (Also acceptable: afforestation and shelter belts; strip cropping; check dams and gully plugging; crop rotation; and the regulation of grazing.)

Official Answer

Q7. (a) Such rows form SHELTER BELTS or wind-breaks. Planted at right angles to the prevailing wind, they stand across its path and reduce its velocity near the ground, so that it can no longer lift and carry away the loose dry topsoil. Rows planted parallel to the wind would simply channel it.

(b) Because leaching requires heavy rainfall to wash the soluble matter downwards, and black soil occurs in the Deccan, a region of moderate and seasonal rain. Its very high clay content also makes it retentive and comparatively impermeable, so water does not percolate freely through it. Its lime and other bases therefore remain in the soil, which is why it stays fertile without manuring.

(c) Because rice needs standing water and a great deal of it, and red soil is porous, coarse and low in humus, so it drains rapidly and cannot retain moisture. It is also deficient in nitrogen and phosphorus. Without irrigation to supply water continuously it cannot hold a rice crop, and red soil regions therefore grow the drought-resistant millets instead.

Official Answer

Q8. ALLUVIAL. Formation — transported and deposited by rivers. Colour — light grey to ash grey. Composition — rich in potash, lime and phosphoric acid, deficient in nitrogen and humus; khadar (new) is finer and more fertile than bhangar (old). Distribution — the northern plains, and the coastal deltas. Crops — rice, wheat, sugarcane, jute, oilseeds.

BLACK (regur). Formation — weathering of basaltic lava of the Deccan Trap. Colour — deep black to chestnut brown. Composition — rich in iron, lime, magnesium, potash and alumina, poor in nitrogen, phosphorus and humus; very high clay content, highly moisture-retentive, self-ploughing. Distribution — Maharashtra, western Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, parts of Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu. Crops — cotton above all, also sugarcane, wheat, jowar, oilseeds.

RED. Formation — weathering in place of ancient crystalline and metamorphic rocks. Colour — red, from iron oxide; yellow when hydrated. Composition — porous and friable, poor in nitrogen, phosphorus, lime and humus. Distribution — much of the Deccan plateau, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Odisha, Chhattisgarh. Crops — millets, groundnut, pulses, tobacco, potato; needs irrigation and fertilizer.

LATERITE. Formation — intense leaching under heavy rainfall and high temperature. Colour — red to reddish-brown. Composition — rich in iron and aluminium oxides, very poor in nitrogen, potash, lime and organic matter; acidic; hardens on exposure and is cut into bricks for building. Distribution — the summits of the Western Ghats, parts of the Eastern Ghats, Kerala, Karnataka, Odisha, Assam and Meghalaya. Crops — tea, coffee, rubber, cashew, coconut and tapioca, chiefly on the plantation crops that tolerate poor acidic soils.
