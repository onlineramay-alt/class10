Q7. It is called self-ploughing because of its high clay content. In the dry season the clay contracts and the soil develops wide, deep cracks; air and moisture penetrate through these cracks and the soil is aerated and turned over naturally, as ploughing would do. In the rains the clay swells and the cracks close.

Two minerals present: any two of iron, lime (calcium carbonate), magnesium, potash and alumina. Black soil is rich in these but deficient in nitrogen, phosphorus and humus.

Official Answer

Q8. (a) Alluvial soil is transported soil, formed of the silt, sand and clay brought down by rivers from the mountains and deposited in their valleys, flood plains and deltas. It is therefore not formed in place from the underlying rock, and it covers the whole of the northern plains and the coastal deltas.

(b) KHADAR is the new alluvium, deposited afresh by the river every year in the low-lying flood plain. It is fine in texture, light in colour, renewed annually and therefore highly fertile.

BHANGAR is the old alluvium, lying above the flood level on higher terraces away from the present course of the river. It is coarser, darker, often contains lime nodules called kankar, is not renewed by fresh deposits, and is consequently less fertile than khadar.

Official Answer

Q9. (a) LATERITE soil — heavy rain in a hot climate washes the silica and bases out of the topsoil, leaving the insoluble iron and aluminium oxides behind.
(b) RED soil — it is poor in nitrogen, phosphorus and humus and dries out easily, but it responds well to irrigation and to added fertilizer and then supports good crops of millets, groundnut, pulses and tobacco.
(c) BLACK soil — weathered from the basaltic lava of the Deccan Trap.

Official Answer

Q10. CAUSES. Deforestation — when the tree cover is cut, there are no roots to bind the soil and no canopy to break the force of the rain, so the topsoil is washed away. Overgrazing — cattle, sheep and goats crop the grass to the roots and trample the surface, leaving it bare and loose. (Also acceptable: faulty methods of cultivation such as ploughing up and down a slope; shifting cultivation; and the action of wind in arid regions.)

METHODS. CONTOUR PLOUGHING — ploughing along the contour rather than up and down the slope, so that each furrow holds the water instead of channelling it downhill. TERRACE FARMING — cutting steps into a hillside so that the slope is broken into level strips. (Also acceptable: afforestation and shelter belts; strip cropping; check dams and gully plugging; crop rotation; and the regulation of grazing.)

Official Answer

Q11. (a) Such rows form SHELTER BELTS or wind-breaks. Planted at right angles to the prevailing wind, they stand across its path and reduce its velocity near the ground, so that it can no longer lift and carry away the loose dry topsoil. Rows planted parallel to the wind would simply channel it.

(b) Because leaching requires heavy rainfall to wash the soluble matter downwards, and black soil occurs in the Deccan, a region of moderate and seasonal rain. Its very high clay content also makes it retentive and comparatively impermeable, so water does not percolate freely through it. Its lime and other bases therefore remain in the soil, which is why it stays fertile without manuring.

(c) Because rice needs standing water and a great deal of it, and red soil is porous, coarse and low in humus, so it drains rapidly and cannot retain moisture. It is also deficient in nitrogen and phosphorus. Without irrigation to supply water continuously it cannot hold a rice crop, and red soil regions therefore grow the drought-resistant millets instead.

Official Answer

Q12. ALLUVIAL. Formation — transported and deposited by rivers. Colour — light grey to ash grey. Composition — rich in potash, lime and phosphoric acid, deficient in nitrogen and humus; khadar (new) is finer and more fertile than bhangar (old). Distribution — the northern plains, and the coastal deltas. Crops — rice, wheat, sugarcane, jute, oilseeds.

BLACK (regur). Formation — weathering of basaltic lava of the Deccan Trap. Colour — deep black to chestnut brown. Composition — rich in iron, lime, magnesium, potash and alumina, poor in nitrogen, phosphorus and humus; very high clay content, highly moisture-retentive, self-ploughing. Distribution — Maharashtra, western Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, parts of Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu. Crops — cotton above all, also sugarcane, wheat, jowar, oilseeds.

RED. Formation — weathering in place of ancient crystalline and metamorphic rocks. Colour — red, from iron oxide; yellow when hydrated. Composition — porous and friable, poor in nitrogen, phosphorus, lime and humus. Distribution — much of the Deccan plateau, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Odisha, Chhattisgarh. Crops — millets, groundnut, pulses, tobacco, potato; needs irrigation and fertilizer.

LATERITE. Formation — intense leaching under heavy rainfall and high temperature. Colour — red to reddish-brown. Composition — rich in iron and aluminium oxides, very poor in nitrogen, potash, lime and organic matter; acidic; hardens on exposure and is cut into bricks for building. Distribution — the summits of the Western Ghats, parts of the Eastern Ghats, Kerala, Karnataka, Odisha, Assam and Meghalaya. Crops — tea, coffee, rubber, cashew, coconut and tapioca, chiefly on the plantation crops that tolerate poor acidic soils.

Q14. (a) The alluvial soil of the foothills (Bhabar/upper courses) is COARSE, gravelly and porous, because the fast upland rivers can carry only heavy material that far; the alluvial soil of the West Bengal delta is FINE, silty and clayey, because by the delta the sluggish river drops only its lightest particles. (b) A TRANSPORTED (ex-situ) soil has been carried away from its parent rock and deposited elsewhere by rivers, wind or ice — e.g. alluvial soil. A RESIDUAL (in-situ) soil has formed where it lies, directly from the weathering of the rock beneath it — e.g. black, red and laterite soils.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q15. (a) It is formed by INTENSE LEACHING. In areas of high temperature and heavy seasonal rainfall, the rain water dissolves and washes down the lime and silica from the topsoil, leaving behind a soil rich in insoluble oxides of IRON and ALUMINIUM — which give it its brick-red colour. (‘Later’ = brick.)

(b) By adding MANURE and FERTILISERS (especially nitrogen and potash) to make up for the leached-out nutrients, and by providing IRRIGATION; on the hill slopes it must also be protected from further erosion. It then supports tea, coffee, cashew, rubber and tapioca.

(c) The summits of the Western Ghats and Eastern Ghats; the Malabar coast of Kerala; the hills of Karnataka, Maharashtra, Odisha and Jharkhand; parts of Assam and Meghalaya.

Q16. (a) LATERITE SOIL — it caps the higher, wetter summits of both the Ghats, where leaching is most intense.

(b) RED SOIL — it is porous and cannot hold moisture, so it is farmed by dry-farming methods (drought-resistant crops, no standing water); it also becomes productive with irrigation.

(c) ALLUVIAL SOIL — coarse and sandy in the upper river courses and near the foothills, fine and clayey in the lower courses and deltas; its texture changes with the speed of the river that laid it down.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q17. (a) On a steep slope, rain water runs off fast and carries the soil with it. Cutting the slope into level STEPS (terraces) breaks the long slope into many short flat ones, so the water collects on each step instead of rushing downhill; it soaks in and the soil stays put.

(b) KHADAR is the new alluvium laid down by the rivers in flood every year, so it is constantly RENEWED and is fine, moist and very fertile. BHANGAR is the old alluvium of the higher ground, above flood level; it is not replenished, is coarser, often has lime nodules (kankar) and is less fertile.

(c) The foothills receive very heavy monsoon rain; the slopes are steep; and the natural forest that once held the soil has been cleared for farming, timber and grazing. Fast run-off down bare steep slopes strips the soil rapidly.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q18. (a) It forms from the weathering of old CRYSTALLINE and METAMORPHIC rocks — granites, gneisses and schists — of the peninsular plateau.

(b) From the presence of IRON OXIDE (ferric oxide) formed by the weathering of iron-rich minerals in those rocks. The soil is red when this iron is oxidised and looks yellow or brown where it is hydrated. (It is not rich in iron as plant food — only coloured by it.)

(c) Red soil is POROUS, coarse-grained and cannot retain moisture; it is also poor in nitrogen, phosphorus and humus. Without irrigation only hardy dry crops can be grown; with irrigation and manuring it produces good crops of rice, ragi, groundnut and tobacco.

In ARID/SEMI-ARID regions: planting SHELTER BELTS of trees across the wind, and strip cropping. C. Structured / Part II Questions

Q21. (i) It is fine and highly MOISTURE-RETENTIVE, keeping the deep-rooted cotton plant watered through the dry season; and it is naturally rich in LIME, IRON, MAGNESIA and potash, so it needs little manure.

(ii) Laterite forms by INTENSE LEACHING under high temperature and heavy seasonal rain, which washes out the lime and silica and leaves insoluble oxides of iron and aluminium. It is unsuitable for cultivation because it is coarse, porous, acidic and very poor in nitrogen, phosphorus, potash and humus; it hardens on exposure. With heavy manuring and irrigation it grows tea, coffee, cashew and tapioca.

(iii) (a) Khadar is fresh alluvium renewed by the floods each year and is fine and fertile; bhangar is old, un-renewed alluvium, coarser and often full of kankar. (b) Red soil is porous and cannot hold water, and is low in nutrients, so it yields well only when irrigated and manured. (c) Terracing turns a long steep slope into a series of level steps, so rain water collects and soaks in on each step instead of running off and carrying the soil away.

(iv) SOIL EROSION is the removal of the fertile topsoil by running water and wind, especially where the land has been laid bare. Three conservation methods: afforestation / planting shelter belts; contour ploughing and bench terracing on slopes; building check dams and bunds across gullies; crop rotation and strip cropping to keep the land covered.
