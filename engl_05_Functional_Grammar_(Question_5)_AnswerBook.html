A. Correct Form of the Verb

Q1. (1) was gliding (2) was holding (3) stopped (4) to run (5) frightened (6) went (7) kept (8) disappeared

REASONING
(1) and (2) — the snake was in the middle of these actions when the writer saw it, so the past continuous. Note that both must be continuous for the sentence to hang together.
(3) — a single completed action: simple past.
(4) — after 'so as not', the infinitive: 'so as not to run over it'.
(5) — after 'was so', a past participle used as an adjective: frightened. 'Frightening' would mean the writer alarmed other people.
(6) — simple past.
(7) — simple past, parallel with 'went'. 'Kept backing away' is the pattern keep + -ing.
(8) — simple past, the final completed action.

METHOD: read the whole passage before filling a single blank. The tense is set by the passage as a whole — here, a narrative in the past — and the commonest error is to change tense midway because a particular sentence sounds like the present.

Q2. (1) found (2) lying (3) was (4) waved (5) cried (6) shouted (7) hesitated (8) looked

REASONING
(2) is the only item here that is not a simple past. After 'found a dead mouse ___ among our treasures' the verb describes the state the mouse was in when found, so the present participle: 'lying'. Note the spelling — lie becomes lying, not 'laying' (which is a different verb, meaning to put something down) and not 'lieing'.

Everything else is straightforward narrative past. The examiner's real target in this passage is (2), and the trap is that a candidate who has settled into filling every blank with a simple past will write 'lay' without pausing.

Q3. (1) selected (2) gave (3) groomed (4) followed (5) uttering (6) letting (7) hits (8) to look

REASONING — this passage is a good deal harder than it appears, because four of the eight are not simple pasts.
(5) 'followed me about uttering several pieces of advice' — the present participle, describing what he was doing as he followed.
(6) after the preposition 'before', a gerund: 'before letting me out'.
(7) inside the quoted speech, which is in the present. 'Even if the sun hits you in the eyes' — a general truth, so the simple present.
(8) after 'try', the infinitive: 'try to look pleasant'.

METHOD: the blanks that follow a preposition (before, after, without, by) almost always take the -ing form; those that follow 'try', 'so as not', 'must' and similar take an infinitive. Identify the word immediately before the blank — it usually decides the form.

B. Prepositions and Appropriate Words

Q4. (a) to (b) by (c) for (d) on (e) since (f) through (g) beyond (h) before

NOTES ON THE HARDER ONES
(d) 'patted him ON his back' — English uses 'on' for a pat and 'on the back' for the location; 'at' and 'in' are both wrong here.
(e) 'since last May' — since is used with a point in time, for with a length of time. 'I have not seen her since May' but 'I have not seen her for eight months'.
(g) 'beyond her means' is the fixed expression for something one cannot afford. 'Above her means' is a common wrong answer.
(h) 'before he was born' — the sentence needs a conjunction of time, not a preposition, and the clue is that a clause follows, not a noun.

These items are largely fixed collocations rather than rules, so they are learnt by reading. Keep a page of them: according to, by means of, a passion for, beyond one's means, in accordance with, in spite of, on behalf of.

Official Answer

Q5. (a) after (b) over (c) out (d) of (e) under (f) across (g) out of (h) since

NOTES
(a) 'named after' — the fixed expression for being given someone's name.
(c) 'spoke out against' — the adverb 'out' is needed to give the sense of speaking publicly and boldly.
(d) 'accused OF' — compare 'charged WITH', 'blamed FOR', 'suspected OF'. These four are regularly confused and regularly tested.
(e) 'under control'.
(g) 'jumped out of the car' — 'out' alone is not enough before a noun.
(h) 'since last summer', as in the 2026 paper. This exact item, in nearly identical wording, has appeared in the 2024, 2025 and 2026 papers — 'I have not slept since yesterday', 'I have not seen my uncle since last summer', 'I have not seen Sonia since last May'. It is worth knowing cold.

Q6. (a) on (b) over (c) to (d) in (e) with (f) at (g) by (h) since

NOTES
(d) 'in recent years' — 'in' is used with a period of time considered as a whole.
(f) 'at home' — never 'in home'. Compare 'at school', 'at work', 'in hospital'.
(g) 'prove your worth BY doing something' — 'by' introduces the means. Compare (b) in the 2026 paper: 'by means of a rope'.
(h) 'since yesterday'.

GENERAL METHOD FOR THIS ITEM: read the sentence aloud in your head with each candidate preposition. Where more than one sounds possible, ask what the preposition is doing — position (on, at, in), direction (to, into, across, over), means (by, with), or time (since, for, in, during) — and choose from that group.

C. Joining and Transforming Sentences

Q7. 1. (c) Picking up the bundle, he walked away.
(a) uses 'and', which the instruction forbids. (b) changes the meaning — 'packing' is not 'picking'. (d) is nonsense: he did not walk away by means of picking up the bundle.

2. (b) Ravi is neither a technician nor a musician.
Two negatives are joined by neither…nor. (a) and (d) reverse the meaning; (c) makes him a musician, which contradicts the original.

3. (d) We like Tanya because she is extremely polite.
The two statements are in a cause-and-effect relation, so 'because'. (a) uses 'but'. (b) and (c) both survive the ban on and/but/so, but (b) says we like her IN SPITE of her politeness, which reverses the sense, and (c) is clumsy though not strictly wrong — (d) is the cleanest.

4. (a) Although the construction started on time, it was delayed due to the bad weather.
The two facts are in contrast, so a concessive conjunction. (b) 'since' asserts a cause that makes no sense; (c) uses 'so'; (d) is ungrammatical.

METHOD: first identify the LOGICAL RELATION between the two sentences — sequence, contrast, cause, condition, or two things of the same kind. Then pick the connector that expresses it: sequence takes a participle ('-ing') or 'after'; contrast takes 'although' or 'though'; cause takes 'because', 'since' or 'as'; two negatives take 'neither…nor'; two positives take 'both…and' or 'not only…but also'.

Q8. 1. (a) These are fresh mangoes which I bought yesterday.
A relative clause keeping the original meaning and the original agent (I). (b) changes the meaning; (c) drops the agent unnecessarily; (d) uses 'brought' for 'bought', which is a different word.

2. (c) The debating teams were very happy as they had been declared joint champions.
The declaring happened BEFORE the happiness, and the main clause is in the past, so the earlier event takes the past perfect: had been declared. This is the sequence-of-tenses rule, and it is the whole point of the item.

3. (a) When the rain came down, all the people ran away.
(b) changes 'the rain came down' to 'it rained' and drops 'all'. (c) has no conjunction at all and is two sentences run together. (d) is grammatical but stilted, and alters the emphasis.

4. (d) Although the disciple was wise, the teacher did not trust him.
The only option that preserves the meaning: (a) and (b) and (c) all state that the teacher DID trust him, reversing the original. (a) also commits the common error of using 'although' and 'yet' together — use one or the other, never both.

WATCH in this item type: check the meaning before the grammar. Three of the four options in question 4 are grammatical English; they are wrong because they say something the original did not.

Q9. 1. (d) Taking pity on the mouse the magician turned it into a cat.
(b) says the same thing but begins the participial phrase with 'Taking pity' and then uses the pronoun 'he' before the noun 'magician' has been introduced — a dangling reference. (d) puts the noun in the main clause, which is correct. 'Showing pity ON' in (a) is also wrong; one takes pity on, or shows pity to.

2. (b) This work must be completed because there is a deadline.
The deadline is the reason for the requirement, so 'because'. (a) reverses cause and effect.

3. (a) He fled where his pursuers could not follow him.
'Where' replaces 'somewhere' and joins the clauses. (b) is nonsense; (c) misuses 'when'; (d) breaks the sequence of tenses by putting 'cannot' in a past narrative.

4. (d) Though he began late, he finished first.
The contrast is between beginning late and finishing first. (a) uses 'and'; (b) and (c) both reverse the facts.

NOTE how consistently the wrong options work: one uses the forbidden conjunction, one reverses the meaning, one breaks the tense sequence, and one is simply ungrammatical. Checking for those four faults will find the answer even when the grammar is unfamiliar.

Q10. 1. The teacher told us to come early the next day as the examination began at eight o'clock.

This is direct into indirect speech, and four things change together: 'come' becomes 'to come' after 'told us'; 'tomorrow' becomes 'the next day'; the present 'begins' shifts back to 'began'; and the inverted commas and the comma splice both go. Candidates commonly change one or two of these and forget the rest.

2. Arun and Nikhil had climbed the mountain, hadn't they?

The rules for a question tag: (i) a positive statement takes a negative tag and a negative statement a positive one; (ii) the tag repeats the AUXILIARY of the main verb — here 'had' — and not 'did' or 'do'; (iii) the subject becomes a pronoun — two people, so 'they'; (iv) the tag is contracted and ends in a question mark.

So: 'had' + n't + they. 'Didn't they' is wrong because the auxiliary in the statement is 'had', not 'did'.

Official Answer

Q11. 1. DIRECT TO INDIRECT SPEECH. Remove the inverted commas; change the tense back one step (present to past, past to past perfect); change pronouns to suit the new speaker; change words of time and place (now→then, today→that day, tomorrow→the next day, here→there, this→that). A command becomes 'told him to…', a request 'requested him to…', a question 'asked whether/why…' with the word order of a statement.

2. ACTIVE TO PASSIVE. The object becomes the subject; the verb becomes the appropriate form of 'be' plus the past participle; the original subject follows 'by' — and is dropped when it is obvious or unimportant. 'The children have broken the window' → 'The window has been broken by the children'.

3. QUESTION TAGS. Positive statement, negative tag and vice versa; repeat the auxiliary; pronoun subject; contracted; question mark.

4. DEGREES OF COMPARISON. 'Samudragupta was one of the greatest Indian kings' → superlative to comparative: 'Samudragupta was greater than most other Indian kings'; to positive: 'Very few Indian kings were as great as Samudragupta'. Note 'most other' and 'very few' — these exact phrasings are what the mark scheme expects.

5. BEGIN WITH A GIVEN WORD. The instruction supplies the opening, which usually forces a passive, an inversion or a subordinate clause. Write the given opening down first, then complete it; do not compose a sentence and try to bend it to the opening afterwards.

6. SIMPLE / COMPOUND / COMPLEX. Simple has one finite verb; compound joins two independent clauses with and/but/or/so; complex has a main clause and a subordinate clause introduced by because, although, when, if, who, which, that.

7. SYNONYM OR PHRASE SUBSTITUTION. 'Rewrite using the word given' — the whole sentence usually has to be restructured around it, and the meaning must not shift.

IN EVERY CASE: the transformed sentence must mean exactly what the original meant. A grammatically perfect sentence that has changed the meaning scores nothing, and that — not the grammar — is what most candidates get wrong.

D. Conjunctions and Sentence Joining (written form)

Q12. Both 1 and 3 join the sentences correctly and preserve the meaning: 'not only cricket but also tennis' and 'cricket as well as tennis' both assert that he plays both games.

Option 3 is the safer answer for this paper, because option 1 contains the word 'but', and the instruction forbids 'and', 'but' or 'so'. Although 'not only… but also' is a single correlative construction rather than the simple conjunction 'but', a strict reading of the instruction rules it out — so choose 3, 'He plays cricket as well as tennis.'

2 is wrong (either/or means one of the two, not both) and 4 reverses the meaning entirely.

NOTE — this is a genuine ambiguity in the paper as set, not an error in the answer. Where two options are defensible, choose the one that cannot be faulted under the instruction given.

Q13. (i) 2. behind — one hides behind a cupboard, or in it, but 'into' expresses motion towards an inside and does not fit 'hid'.
(ii) 1. across — a bridge spans a stream from one side to the other.
(iii) 1. through — 'carry him through his difficulties' is the fixed expression, meaning to bring him safely out the other side.
(iv) 1. in — 'to call in' a person means to summon them for help or advice, which is exactly what the sentence describes. 'Call to' means to shout at; 'call up' means to telephone; 'call over' means to summon across a distance.

(iv) is the item worth studying: 'call' takes at least six different particles with six different meanings — call in, call up, call on, call off, call out, call for. Phrasal verbs of this kind are the hardest part of this question and cannot be reasoned out; they must be collected and learnt.

Q14. (i) get (ii) gone (iii) lived (iv) known

REASONING
(i) after 'hesitate to', the plain infinitive: 'to get into it'.
(ii), (iii) and (iv) all follow 'had', so all three take the past participle — gone, lived, known. The past perfect is required because these events happened before the moment of seeing the pool.

The trap is (ii): the past tense of 'go' is 'went' but the past participle is 'gone', and 'had went' is the error the item is designed to catch. Likewise 'had knew' for 'had known'.

RULE TO CARRY AWAY: after any form of 'have' (has, have, had), the verb must be the PAST PARTICIPLE, never the simple past. See/saw/seen, go/went/gone, know/knew/known, do/did/done, write/wrote/written — learn the third column, because it is the third column this question tests.
