Q2. (c) polar covalent compound. The H–Cl bond is polar because chlorine is far more electronegative than hydrogen, giving the molecule a positive and a negative end. The polar water molecules attract these ends strongly enough to pull the molecule apart into ions, so HCl is extremely soluble — about 450 volumes in one volume of water.

Q4. (a) NaCl. Concentrated sulphuric acid, being a non-volatile acid, displaces the volatile hydrogen chloride from sodium chloride. Na₂SO₄ has no chloride; Na₂CO₃ would give carbon dioxide; NaNO₃ would give nitric acid.

Q5. (d) A greenish-yellow gas that turns moist starch-iodide paper blue-black. Manganese dioxide oxidises concentrated hydrochloric acid to CHLORINE: MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + 2H₂O + Cl₂. Chlorine displaces iodine from potassium iodide, and the iodine turns the starch blue-black.

Q6. (a) upward displacement of air — that is, downward delivery into an upright gas jar. Hydrogen chloride is denser than air (RMM 36.5 against 29), so it sinks to the bottom of the jar and pushes the air up and out.

Q7. SODIUM CHLORIDE (common salt) and CONCENTRATED SULPHURIC ACID, heated to BELOW 200 °C.

NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl↑ (below 200 °C)

The temperature is kept below 200 °C deliberately. Above that, the sodium hydrogen sulphate reacts with more sodium chloride to give sodium sulphate — 2NaCl + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2HCl — but that reaction needs a much higher temperature, at which the glass apparatus would be attacked and the sodium sulphate formed would set as a hard crust in the flask, difficult to remove.

Q10. (a) Quicklime (CaO) is a basic drying agent, and hydrogen chloride is an acidic gas — they react (CaO + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O), so the gas would be absorbed instead of dried. Only the acidic drying agent concentrated sulphuric acid can be used.

(b) The gas is extremely soluble; in moist air it at once dissolves in the water vapour to form tiny droplets of hydrochloric acid, which appear as dense white fumes.

(c) Because hydrogen chloride is SO soluble that it would rush back up the delivery tube into the flask ('back suction' or sucking back), drawing water into the hot apparatus and cracking it. A funnel arrangement is used instead so that any sucked-back water is held in the wide funnel and breaks contact with the gas.

C. Short Answer Type

Q13. A dry round-bottomed flask is filled with hydrogen chloride gas and fitted with a stopper carrying a jet tube and a dropper. The jet is dipped into a trough of water coloured blue with litmus. A few drops of water are introduced from the dropper.

The gas dissolves almost instantly in those few drops, creating a partial VACUUM in the flask. The atmospheric pressure on the trough then forces the water up the jet, and it enters the flask as a FOUNTAIN, turning RED as it goes because the solution is acidic.

THE TWO PROPERTIES DEMONSTRATED:
(i) that hydrogen chloride is EXTREMELY SOLUBLE in water — the fountain only forms because the gas dissolves fast enough to create the vacuum;
(ii) that its solution is ACIDIC — shown by the blue litmus turning red.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q14. It is collected by UPWARD DISPLACEMENT OF AIR — that is, downward delivery into a gas jar, the gas being led to the bottom — because hydrogen chloride is DENSER THAN AIR (relative molecular mass 36.5 against air's 29), so it sinks and displaces the air upwards out of the jar.

It cannot be collected OVER WATER because it is EXTREMELY SOLUBLE in water — about 450 volumes dissolve in one volume of water — so it would simply dissolve instead of being collected.

Q15. AQUA REGIA — 'royal water' — is a mixture of CONCENTRATED HYDROCHLORIC ACID and CONCENTRATED NITRIC ACID in the ratio 3 : 1 by volume.

Its importance is that it dissolves the NOBLE METALS, GOLD and PLATINUM, which neither acid can attack alone. The nitric acid oxidises the metal while the hydrochloric acid supplies chloride ions that form a stable soluble complex with it, and the two together achieve what neither can separately.

It is used in the refining and testing of gold and platinum, and in analytical chemistry to dissolve otherwise resistant samples.

Q17. (a) 2NaCl + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2HCl↑ (above 200 °C)
Below 200 °C only the acid salt forms (NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl); the higher temperature drives the second stage, NaHSO₄ + NaCl → Na₂SO₄ + HCl.

(b) Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂↑ (iron gives the iron(II) salt with a dilute acid)

(c) MgSO₃ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O + SO₂↑ (a dilute acid displaces sulphur dioxide from a sulphite)

(d) MnO₂ + 4HCl (conc.) → MnCl₂ + 2H₂O + Cl₂↑ (here the concentrated acid is oxidised to chlorine)
