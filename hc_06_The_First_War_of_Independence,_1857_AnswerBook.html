C. Short Answer Type

Q14. (i) The Doctrine of Lapse — Lord Dalhousie refused to recognise the adopted sons of Hindu rulers who died without a natural heir, and annexed Satara, Jhansi and Nagpur, alarming every princely house in India. (ii) The annexation of Awadh in 1856 on the ground of misgovernment, though its Nawab had always been loyal, showed that even faithful allies were not safe; it also embittered the many sepoys recruited from Awadh. (iii) The humiliation of the Mughal emperor — Nana Sahib's pension was stopped, and it was announced that Bahadur Shah's successors would have to give up the title of emperor and leave the Red Fort — which offended both Hindu and Muslim sentiment attached to the imperial house.

Official Answer

Q15. (i) The destruction of Indian handicrafts — British machine-made goods entered India free of duty while Indian goods faced heavy duties in England, so weavers and artisans lost their livelihood and were thrown back on an already crowded land. (ii) Heavy and rigidly collected land revenue ruined the peasantry, driving them into the hands of moneylenders and off their holdings; the Inam Commission's resumption of rent-free lands dispossessed many landholders and religious institutions. (iii) The drain of wealth — India's revenues were used to pay for British administration, an army employed in British wars, and dividends to Company shareholders, so wealth flowed steadily out of the country and impoverished it.

Official Answer

Q16. (i) Discrimination in service — the Indian sepoy was poorly paid, could rise no higher than the rank of subedar however able he was, and saw every senior post reserved for Europeans. (ii) The General Service Enlistment Act, 1856, obliged recruits to serve overseas if ordered, which meant loss of caste for high-caste Hindus, and the foreign service allowance for duty in Sindh and the Punjab was withdrawn, cutting their pay. (iii) The sepoys outnumbered European troops several times over, which gave them the confidence to revolt; and many of them, coming from Awadh, shared their families' anger at its annexation. The greased cartridges then provided the spark.

Official Answer

Q17. (i) Indians had come to believe that the British intended to convert the whole country to Christianity — missionaries were active and were thought to enjoy official support, and the Religious Disabilities Act of 1850 allowed a convert to Christianity to inherit his ancestral property. (ii) Social reforms such as the abolition of sati in 1829 and the Widow Remarriage Act of 1856, however humane, were seen by orthodox opinion as interference by a foreign government in religion and custom. (iii) The introduction of Western education, the railway and the telegraph was distrusted as an attack on tradition and caste, and the taxing of lands held rent-free by temples and mosques struck directly at religious establishments.

(i) It was limited in extent — Bengal, the Punjab, Sindh, Rajputana and the whole of southern and western India remained quiet, so the British could draw men and supplies from the greater part of the country. Q19. On the Mughals it was final: Bahadur Shah II, whom the rebels had proclaimed emperor, was tried for treason, deposed and exiled to Rangoon, where he died in 1862, and two of his sons and a grandson were shot at Delhi. The Mughal dynasty, which had ruled for more than three centuries, was extinguished, and the Red Fort ceased to be an imperial residence.

On the Peshwas it was equally final. Nana Sahib, the adopted son of the last Peshwa Baji Rao II, had led the revolt at Kanpur after the British refused to continue his father's pension; when the revolt failed he fled towards Nepal and was never heard of again. The office of Peshwa, already abolished in name, now disappeared altogether as a rallying point, and the Maratha claim to a revived confederacy died with it.

Official Answer

Q20. (i) The British Crown assumed the government of India directly, and the rule of the East India Company came to an end. (ii) There would be no further annexation of Indian territory: the treaties made with the Indian princes would be respected, and their right to adopt heirs was conceded, so the Doctrine of Lapse was abandoned. (iii) Religious toleration was promised — the government would not interfere with the religious beliefs or worship of any of its Indian subjects. (iv) Indians would be admitted freely and impartially to offices under the government, without distinction of race or creed, according to their education and ability; and a general amnesty was granted to all who had taken part in the revolt except those directly guilty of murdering British subjects.

Official Answer

Q21. (i) The proportion of European to Indian soldiers was greatly increased, so that a European force was always at hand. (ii) The artillery — the decisive arm — was placed exclusively in European hands, and Indians were kept out of it and out of the higher ranks. (iii) Recruitment was reorganised on the principle of divide and rule: the so-called 'martial races' were favoured, men from the regions that had rebelled were distrusted, and regiments were deliberately mixed by caste, community and region so that the soldiers should not combine again as they had in 1857.

D. Structured / Eight-Mark Questions

Q22. (i) The Doctrine of Lapse and the annexations of Satara, Jhansi and Nagpur; the annexation of Awadh in 1856 on the plea of misgovernment; and the humiliation of the Mughal house and the stopping of Nana Sahib's pension.

(ii) The ruin of Indian handicrafts by the free entry of British machine-made goods; a heavy and rigidly collected land revenue, together with the resumption of rent-free lands, which impoverished peasants and landholders; and the steady drain of India's wealth to England.

(iii) The rule of the East India Company ended and the Crown assumed the government of India under the Government of India Act, 1858, the Governor-General becoming also the Viceroy. Queen Victoria's Proclamation promised no further annexation, religious toleration, and the admission of Indians to office without distinction of race or creed. The Mughal dynasty and the Peshwaship were extinguished. And the army was reorganised, with more European troops, the artillery reserved to Europeans, and recruitment deliberately arranged on lines of caste and community to prevent another combination.

Official Answer

Q23. (i) The destruction of Indian handicrafts by duty-free British imports, which threw artisans onto the land; heavy land revenue and the resumption of rent-free holdings, which ruined peasants and dispossessed landholders and religious institutions; and the drain of wealth, by which India's revenues paid for British administration, British wars and Company dividends.

(ii) Low pay, and no promotion for an Indian above the rank of subedar; the General Service Enlistment Act of 1856, which required service overseas and so threatened loss of caste, together with the withdrawal of the foreign service allowance; and the great numerical preponderance of sepoys over European troops, reinforced by the anger of the many sepoys drawn from newly annexed Awadh.

(iii) The greased cartridges of the new Enfield rifle. The cartridge had to be bitten open before loading, and the sepoys believed the grease was made from the fat of cows and pigs — sacred to Hindus and unclean to Muslims. Both communities therefore saw it as a deliberate attempt to destroy their religion, and the refusal to use the cartridges at Meerut turned into open mutiny on 10 May 1857.

Official Answer

Q24. (i) It went far beyond the army: peasants, artisans, dispossessed landholders and religious leaders joined it, and in Awadh it took the character of a popular rising. It had political leadership of its own — Bahadur Shah II was proclaimed emperor, and Nana Sahib, Rani Lakshmibai, Begum Hazrat Mahal and Kunwar Singh led whole regions. And its causes were national, not merely military: annexation, economic ruin and the fear for religion affected the whole of northern India, not only the sepoys.

(ii) Discrimination in pay and promotion; the General Service Enlistment Act of 1856 and the loss of the foreign service allowance; and the sepoys' numerical preponderance combined with their anger over the annexation of Awadh, from which so many of them came.

(iii) It was confined to the north — the south, the west, Bengal and the Punjab stayed quiet. It had no unity of leadership, no common plan and no agreed aim, and several powerful Indian rulers helped the British against it. And the British were far stronger in discipline, weapons and resources, and used the telegraph and railway to move faster than the rebels could.

E. Source-Based / Picture-Based Questions

Q25. (i) That its character has been disputed from the beginning. 'Mutiny' and 'Sepoy Mutiny' are the British names, presenting it as a limited breakdown of military discipline that reflected no wider discontent and so required no change of policy. 'First War of Indian Independence' is the nationalist name, asserting that the soldiers were only the spark and that the rising expressed the grievances of a whole people against foreign rule. The neutral 'Indian Rebellion' avoids taking either side. The event is the same; the name chosen is an argument about its meaning.
(ii) Any two of: Delhi, under Bahadur Shah II with General Bakht Khan; Kanpur, under Nana Sahib with Tantia Tope; Lucknow, under Begum Hazrat Mahal; Jhansi, under Rani Lakshmibai; Bihar, under Kunwar Singh of Jagdishpur.

Official Answer

Q26. (i) Rani Lakshmibai, associated with Jhansi.
(ii) The Doctrine of Lapse. When her husband died without a natural heir, Lord Dalhousie refused to recognise their adopted son as the successor, and Jhansi was annexed to the Company's dominions in 1853. Her protest was rejected, and when the revolt broke out she took up the cause of her state.
(iii) Because she fought and died in the field — she led her troops in person at Jhansi and afterwards at Gwalior, where she was killed in June 1858 — and so became the enduring image of Indian resistance to British rule.
