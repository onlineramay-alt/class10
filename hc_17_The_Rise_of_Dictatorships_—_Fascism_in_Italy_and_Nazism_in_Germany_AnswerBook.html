C. Short Answer Type

Q9. (i) The 'mutilated victory'. Italy had joined the Allies in 1915 on the promise of substantial territory under the Treaty of London, and had lost 600,000 men; at the peace conference she was given only two small islands and part of Dalmatia, and Fiume was denied her. The sense of having been cheated after so great a sacrifice created a nationalist grievance the Fascists exploited relentlessly.

(ii) Economic distress and the fear of communism. The war left Italy with a ruined currency, heavy debt and mass unemployment among demobilised soldiers. In 1919-20 came a wave of strikes, factory occupations and seizures of land by peasants, which convinced industrialists, landowners and the middle class that a communist revolution was at hand. They turned to Mussolini's Blackshirts, who broke the strikes by force, and financed them.

(iii) The weakness of the government. Ministry followed ministry, none commanding a stable majority, and none able to restore order or prosperity. Parliamentary democracy appeared discredited, and Mussolini offered instead strength, discipline and national pride. When his Blackshirts marched on Rome in October 1922, the King, rather than resist, invited him to form a government.

Official Answer

Q10. (i) The humiliation of the Treaty of Versailles. The war-guilt clause, the crushing reparations, the loss of territory and colonies and the reduction of the army to a police force were felt by Germans as a national degradation. Hitler's central promise was to tear the Treaty up, and it was the most powerful appeal he possessed.

(ii) The weakness of the Weimar Republic. It had been born of defeat and had signed the Treaty, so its opponents called its founders the 'November criminals'; its system of proportional representation produced a splintered Reichstag and a succession of unstable coalitions, so that democracy came to be identified in German minds with surrender and paralysis.

(iii) Economic catastrophe and the fear of communism. The hyperinflation of 1923 destroyed the savings of the whole middle class, and the Depression from 1929 threw six million out of work. In that ruin the Nazi vote rose from 12 seats in 1928 to 230 in 1932. Industrialists and the propertied classes, alarmed by the growth of the Communist vote as well, were willing to see Hitler in office as a bulwark; he was appointed Chancellor on 30 January 1933. To these must be added his own gifts as an orator and organiser, and the party's mastery of propaganda and street violence.

Q11. (i) One party and one leader. Both were totalitarian systems in which all authority was gathered into the hands of a single leader — Il Duce, Der Führer — whose word was law; every other political party was banned, and the state controlled the press, the schools, the courts and the youth movements.

(ii) Hostility to democracy and to communism alike. Both dismissed parliamentary government as weak, corrupt and incapable of decision, and both destroyed socialists, communists and free trade unions, using secret police, censorship and concentration camps against all opposition.

(iii) Extreme nationalism and the glorification of war. Both held that the individual exists for the state and has no rights against it, both preached the superiority of their own nation — the Nazis in explicitly racial terms — and both regarded war and territorial expansion as legitimate and indeed noble instruments of policy, building up armed forces in defiance of treaty limits.

Q12. (i) Totalitarian rule under a single leader and a single party, with all opposition parties abolished and the state controlling the press, education and every organisation of public life.

(ii) Contempt for democracy. Parliamentary government was condemned as slow, divided and corrupt, and was replaced by rule from above, with elections reduced to acclamation.

(iii) Militant anti-communism and the destruction of the labour movement. Both came to power partly as the answer of the propertied classes to the fear of revolution, and both suppressed communists, socialists and free trade unions.

(iv) Aggressive nationalism, imperialism and the glorification of war. Each preached the greatness and superiority of his own people, demanded expansion — Mussolini a new Roman Empire, Hitler Lebensraum in the east — regarded war as an instrument of policy and a test of national worth, and rearmed in defiance of the treaties. To these may be added the denial of individual rights, the use of secret police and concentration camps, and the systematic use of propaganda and mass spectacle.

Official Answer

Q13. (i) Dissatisfaction with the peace settlement of 1919. Germany found the Treaty of Versailles a humiliation, and Italy — though on the winning side — believed she had been cheated of the territory promised her. In both countries the settlement gave the coming dictator a national grievance to exploit, and a promise of restored greatness to offer.

(ii) Economic ruin and the failure of the parliamentary system to deal with it. Both countries came out of the war with wrecked finances, inflation and mass unemployment among demobilised soldiers, and in both the succession of weak coalition governments proved unable to restore order or prosperity. When democracy appeared powerless and communism appeared imminent, the propertied classes and much of the middle class were prepared to accept a strong man who promised discipline, work and national pride — and to overlook the cost.

D. Structured / Eight-Mark Questions

Q14. (i) The 'mutilated victory' — Italy's disappointment at receiving almost nothing at Versailles after entering the war on the promise of large gains; economic ruin and the fear of communism after the strikes and factory occupations of 1919-20, which drove industrialists and the middle class to finance Mussolini's Blackshirts; and the succession of weak coalition ministries that discredited parliamentary government, so that when the Blackshirts marched on Rome in 1922 the King invited Mussolini to form a ministry rather than resist.

(ii) One party and one leader, with all opposition abolished and the state controlling press, schools and courts; hostility both to parliamentary democracy and to communism, with secret police and concentration camps used against all dissent; and extreme nationalism with the glorification of war and expansion, the individual existing wholly for the state.

(iii) Appeasement was the policy by which Britain and France conceded Hitler's demands one after another in the hope of avoiding war. They accepted his reintroduction of conscription, the remilitarisation of the Rhineland in 1936, the annexation of Austria in March 1938 and, at Munich in September 1938, the surrender of the Sudetenland — Chamberlain returning to announce 'peace for our time'. Its consequence was the opposite of its intention. Each concession persuaded Hitler that the western powers would not fight; he seized the rest of Czechoslovakia in March 1939, and when he invaded Poland that September he did so in the confident expectation that Britain and France would again do nothing. Appeasement also destroyed what remained of the League's authority and drove the Soviet Union, excluded from Munich, towards the pact with Germany that made the attack on Poland possible.

The rally is not a meeting for debate but a spectacle, designed so that the individual feels himself absorbed into a disciplined mass with a single leader and a single will — which is precisely the Fascist and Nazi doctrine that the individual exists for the state. Propaganda, uniforms, symbols and mass ceremony were instruments of rule as deliberate as the secret police, and were meant to make opposition seem not merely dangerous but unthinkable.
