B. Short Answer Type

Q2. Because India has very few sites where the earth's internal heat reaches the surface at a temperature and pressure high enough to generate power on a commercial scale. The known hot springs — Manikaran in Himachal Pradesh, Puga in Ladakh, and a few in Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh — are small, and most lie in remote mountain areas far from the demand for electricity. The technology is besides expensive to install, the drilling is difficult and costly, and the water at these sites often carries dissolved minerals that corrode the plant. Given that solar and wind power are cheaper and far more widely available in India, geothermal has not been developed.

Official Answer

Q3. SUITABILITY. India lies largely within the tropics and receives strong sunshine for about 300 days in the year, with the highest insolation in Rajasthan, Gujarat and the Deccan — precisely the dry regions where other resources are scarce. The country also has a great many villages remote from the electricity grid, which solar panels can serve without any transmission line.

TWO ADVANTAGES. It is INEXHAUSTIBLE AND FREE — sunlight will not run out and costs nothing, so once the equipment is installed the running cost is very low. And it is CLEAN — it produces no smoke, no ash and no carbon dioxide, so it neither pollutes the air nor contributes to global warming, unlike coal and oil.

(Also acceptable: it can be installed anywhere, including on a roof; it needs little maintenance and has no moving parts; and it is well suited to decentralised village use — solar cookers, lanterns, water heaters and street lights.)

Q4. GENERATION. Bio-gas is produced by the decomposition of organic waste — cattle dung, crop residues, human waste and vegetable refuse — in the ABSENCE OF AIR. The waste is fed into a closed digester or gobar gas plant, where bacteria break it down anaerobically, releasing a gas that is chiefly methane with some carbon dioxide. The gas is drawn off through a pipe and burned for cooking and lighting; the residue left in the digester is removed as slurry.

ADVANTAGES. The raw material is waste, freely available in every village, so the fuel costs nothing. It burns cleanly, without the smoke of dung cakes and firewood, which removes a serious cause of respiratory illness among village women. It reduces the cutting of trees for firewood and the burning of dung, and the spent slurry is a better manure than the dung would have been, since it retains the nitrogen. It also disposes of waste hygienically, reducing flies and disease. And it is decentralised — a household or a village can run its own plant.

Q5. WIND ENERGY. The wind turns the blades of a windmill or wind turbine, which drives a generator to produce electricity. A large number of turbines placed together forms a wind farm. It requires steady winds of adequate speed, so the best sites are open coastal plains, hilltops and passes. In India, TAMIL NADU is much the largest producer, with the great wind farms in the Muppandal and Aralvaimozhi passes near Kanyakumari, where the wind funnels through the gap in the Western Ghats; Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka and Rajasthan follow.

TIDAL ENERGY. A barrage or dam is built across the mouth of a narrow inlet or estuary. As the tide rises, water flows in through gates and is trapped; as the tide falls, the water is released through turbines, generating electricity. It requires a large tidal range — a big difference between high and low water — and a narrow funnel-shaped inlet. In India the GULF OF KUTCH and the GULF OF KHAMBHAT in Gujarat have the greatest tidal range and the best potential, and the Sundarbans delta in West Bengal is a further site. Very little has yet been developed, because the capital cost is very high.

Q6. CONVENTIONAL sources are those that have been in use for a long time and on which industry has historically depended: coal, petroleum, natural gas, firewood and hydel power. They are, with the exception of hydel, EXHAUSTIBLE and non-renewable — once burnt they are gone. They are POLLUTING, releasing carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide and particulates, and are the chief cause of global warming. They are UNEVENLY DISTRIBUTED, concentrated in a few regions, so they must be transported at cost. But the technology is well established and the supply is reliable and available on demand.

NON-CONVENTIONAL sources are those developed more recently as alternatives: solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, nuclear and bio-gas. Most are RENEWABLE and inexhaustible. They are largely CLEAN and non-polluting. They are WIDELY DISTRIBUTED — sunlight and wind fall everywhere — so they can be used in a decentralised way in remote villages without a grid. Against this, the initial cost of installation is high; many of them are INTERMITTENT, since the sun does not shine at night nor the wind blow steadily; and the technology in some cases is still developing. Nuclear power is the exception to several of these: it is not renewable and it produces dangerous waste, though it emits no carbon dioxide.

The importance of the non-conventional sources is that the conventional ones are finite and are damaging the atmosphere, so the transition is not optional.
