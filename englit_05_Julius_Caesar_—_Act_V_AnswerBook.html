B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q4. (i) Over the body of Cassius, who has killed himself. He ran on his own sword — the same sword with which he had stabbed Caesar — held for him by his bondman Pindarus, whose freedom he had long ago promised and now claims payment for.

(ii) That Caesar, though dead, is destroying his killers by their own hands rather than by his. Cassius has died on his own sword, and Brutus will shortly do the same; the conspirators are not being executed by an enemy but are turning their weapons inward. 'Proper' here means 'own', intensifying the point.

(iii) Cassius, seeing troops surround his camp, sent Titinius to find out whether they were friends or enemies, and set Pindarus on a hill to watch. Pindarus reported that Titinius was enclosed by horsemen who shouted for joy, and that some lighted down — he took it for a capture. In fact they were Brutus's men, and the shouting was their welcome; Titinius was being crowned with a garland of victory sent by Brutus. Believing his best friend taken because he had sent him, and that his own side had lost, Cassius asked Pindarus to hold his sword and ran upon it, saying, 'Caesar, thou art revenged, / Even with the sword that kill'd thee.' Titinius returned with the garland, found him dead, and killed himself with the same sword.

(iv) He calls him 'The last of all the Romans' and says that Rome will never breed his equal. He tells Cassius's friends that he owes more tears to this dead man than they shall see him pay, and defers the funeral — 'I shall find time' — sending the body to Thasos rather than holding funerals in the camp, lest it comfort the soldiers and weaken their spirits. Then he turns at once to the second battle.

(v) In three senses, and Brutus means at least the first two.

Literally, the deaths are Caesar's revenge: Cassius dies on the sword that killed him, and says so; Brutus will die on his own. Antony's prophecy over the body — that Caesar's spirit would range for revenge — is exactly fulfilled.

Politically, Caesar has won. The conspirators killed him to prevent one-man rule, and the result is a triumvirate and, beyond the play, an empire under his heir Octavius. Caesar is more powerful dead than living, because his name has become a cause.

Morally, and this Brutus half-perceives, the conspirators are destroyed by what the murder did to them rather than by any enemy: by the quarrel over bribes, by isolation, by misjudgement, by the haunted conscience that produced the Ghost. Nobody defeats Brutus and Cassius. They defeat themselves, which is what the line says.

Q5. (i) Of Brutus, whose body has just been found. Brutus, having lost the second battle at Philippi, asked several of his followers in turn to hold his sword — Clitus, Dardanius, Volumnius all refused — and finally persuaded Strato to hold it while he ran upon it. Antony and Octavius arrive with their victorious army and stand over the body.

(ii) On motive alone. Every other conspirator, Antony says, acted out of envy of Caesar's greatness — a personal, unworthy reason. Brutus alone joined them out of a genuine and disinterested concern for the general good. Antony is granting that Brutus was wrong about the deed and right in what moved him to it.

(iii) It draws on the old idea that a man's temperament is a compound of the four elements — earth, water, air and fire — and that character depends on their proportion. In most men one predominates and unbalances them; in Brutus they were combined so evenly that no quality overpowered the rest. Antony means that he was a complete and balanced human being, and 'This was a man!' is the conclusion of that thought: not merely a good man, but the thing itself, as Nature would have wished to make it.

(iv) Octavius orders that Brutus be treated with all respect and rites of burial: he shall lie in Octavius's tent, 'ordered honourably', with his bones. He takes into his service all who served Brutus, at Strato's request and on Messala's recommendation. He then calls the field to rest and, in the play's last line, invites them to 'part the glories of this happy day'.

(v) Because it comes from the enemy, and from the one man who had every reason to withhold it. Antony loved Caesar, was nearly murdered by these men, deliberately destroyed them, and has just won. In the forum scene he called them traitors and butchers, and stirred a mob to burn their houses — and he did it knowingly, with 'Now let it work'.

For that man, at the end, to separate Brutus from the rest and place him above them gives the tribute an authority that no friend's eulogy could have. It also settles the play's central question in the only way it can honestly be settled. Brutus was mistaken in every practical judgement he made, and the assassination brought about the opposite of what he intended; but his motive was clean, and the play will not let him be dismissed as a murderer. That the verdict is delivered by the victor, over the body, in the last speech but one, is Shakespeare's way of insisting that being defeated and being contemptible are not the same thing.

C. Short Answer Type

Q6. It is a meeting of insults rather than negotiation, and it settles nothing.

Octavius and Antony come down, and the four commanders exchange words. Brutus observes that they stand and talk before they strike; Octavius answers that he does not, like Antony, love words better than blows. Antony sneers that in blows Brutus gives good words — witness the 'Long live! hail, Caesar!' with which he struck. Cassius retorts that the quality of Antony's blows is yet unknown, but that as for his words, they rob the Hybla bees and leave them honeyless. Antony throws back the bitterest charge: the conspirators showed their teeth like apes, fawned like hounds, and kissed Caesar's feet while Casca, like a cur, struck him on the neck behind — 'O you flatterers!'

Octavius ends it by drawing his sword and swearing it shall not be sheathed till Caesar's three-and-thirty wounds are well avenged, or till another Caesar has added the slaughter to the sword of traitors. The two sides withdraw to fight.

The scene's function is to show that no reconciliation is possible and that the argument has degenerated from politics into personal hatred — and, in Octavius's cold refusal to bandy words, to mark the arrival of a harder kind of man than any yet seen in the play.

Q7. Brutus, having lost the second battle, gathers his few remaining followers and asks each in turn to hold his sword so that he may run upon it. Clitus, Dardanius and Volumnius all refuse — Volumnius saying that is not an office for a friend. Hearing the enemy approach, he asks Strato, who consents and turns away his face; Brutus runs on the sword with 'Caesar, now be still: / I kill'd not thee with half so good a will.'

The contrast with Cassius is deliberate and complete. Cassius dies on a factual mistake, in despair, believing a defeat that has not happened and a friend captured who is in fact being crowned; his last words blame the sword and are addressed to Caesar as a victim of revenge. Brutus dies knowing exactly what has happened, having said farewell to each of his friends by name, in full possession of himself, and his last words are a release rather than a defeat — he goes to death more willingly than he went to the killing.

Cassius's end is pathetic; Brutus's is composed. Shakespeare gives Brutus, alone of the two, the dignity of choosing, and gives him companions who love him enough to refuse to help — which is why Antony's eulogy follows so naturally.

Q8. Both are minor figures whose loyalty measures Brutus's worth, which is their dramatic purpose.

Lucilius, in Scene iv, impersonates Brutus in order to draw the enemy off — 'And I am Brutus, Marcus Brutus, I' — and offers them money to kill him at once. When the deception is discovered, Antony, far from punishing him, orders that he be kept safe and well used: 'I had rather have such men my friends than enemies.' The moment does double duty, showing what Brutus inspires in his followers and what Antony is capable of recognising.

Strato, in Scene v, is the man who finally holds the sword. He does it out of love, turning his face away, and afterwards tells Messala that Brutus 'only overcame himself, / And no man else hath honour by his death'. Octavius takes him into his own service on Messala's word.

Between them they establish the note on which the play ends: that Brutus was beaten by no one, and that even his enemies could see what he was.

D. Long Answer / Essay Type

Q9. The title names Caesar, but the play belongs to Brutus by every measure that matters.

Brutus has by far the largest part; he is on stage in most scenes and Caesar is killed at the beginning of Act III, less than halfway through. Caesar appears in only three scenes alive, and much of what we know of him comes from other men's reports. The soliloquies — the form in which Shakespeare gives a character an interior life — belong to Brutus and Cassius, not to Caesar.

More importantly, the play has the shape of Brutus's tragedy. He is a good man with a flaw; the flaw is not ambition or cruelty but an idealism so complete that it cannot recognise how other men actually work. Every disaster follows from it: he refuses the oath, spares Antony, permits the funeral oration, and insists on Philippi, and each time he overrules the shrewder Cassius on a point of principle and is wrong. The catastrophe is not something done to him but something he does, which is the classical requirement.

He also has what Caesar lacks — a divided mind, shown to us from inside. 'It must be by his death' is the moral centre of the play precisely because we watch a man reason himself into an act his own reasoning will not support, admitting that the quarrel 'will bear no colour for the thing he is'. And the play ends on him: his death, Strato's testimony that he overcame only himself, Antony's eulogy, Octavius's orders for his burial.

The case for Caesar is not empty. His name opens and closes the action; his spirit dominates the second half more powerfully than his person dominated the first, as Brutus himself acknowledges over Cassius's body — 'O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet!' The play could be read as the tragedy of a great man destroyed by his own inflexibility, with the last two acts as a long demonstration of what Rome lost.

But a tragedy requires an interior, and Shakespeare gives Caesar almost none. What he gives Caesar is a function: to be the fixed point around which Brutus's conscience turns. The title is Caesar's; the tragedy is Brutus's.

E. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q10. Honour is the value everyone in the play appeals to, and the play spends five acts testing what the word is worth.

For Brutus it is the governing principle and is entirely sincere. He tells Cassius at the outset that if it be for the general good he will look on honour and death indifferently; he refuses an oath because honour needs no binding; he refuses to raise money by vile means. His honour is real, and it is also what makes him usable: Cassius sees that Brutus's honourable mettle may be wrought from that it is disposed, and forges letters accordingly. A man who acts only for honour can be moved by anyone who can dress a proposal in it.

For Cassius honour is a language rather than a motive. He appeals to it constantly while his real grievance is that he cannot bear to be a lesser man than Caesar — 'I was born free as Caesar; so were you.' He is at least honest about this in soliloquy.

Antony makes the word do the opposite of its meaning. 'Brutus is an honourable man', repeated after each item of contrary evidence, hollows it out until the crowd hears it as an accusation. It is the play's most brilliant demonstration that a moral term can be turned into a weapon by tone alone.

What the play concludes is neither cynical nor comfortable. Brutus's honour leads directly to a murder, a civil war and his own death, and it does so because he mistakes his own integrity for a sufficient guide to political judgement. And yet the last speech in the play insists that his motive was clean and different in kind from everyone else's, and it is spoken by the enemy who destroyed him. Honour is shown to be both genuinely admirable and completely insufficient — and Shakespeare declines to resolve that into a lesson.
