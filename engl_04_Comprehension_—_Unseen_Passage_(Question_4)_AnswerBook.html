Q3. The three forms are:

1. MEANING AS USED IN THE PASSAGE — a word is given with a line reference and four options. Substitute each option into the sentence in the passage and read it aloud in your head. Reject options that are true meanings of the word but not the meaning here; that is precisely the trap.

2. FIND THE WORD — you are given a meaning ('which word in the passage means…') or an opposite ('which word is the opposite of…') and must locate it. Fix the sense you want in your own words first, then scan; if a line reference is given, search the lines around it and not the whole passage.

3. WORD IN A NEW SENTENCE — occasionally you are asked to use the word in a sentence of your own. The sentence must make the meaning evident: 'The house was dilapidated' proves nothing, while 'The dilapidated house had a broken door and no glass in its windows' proves you know the word.

In every form the marks are for reading, not for vocabulary you happen to possess: the passage always contains the evidence.

Q6. Because the farmer had denied being a teacher at all — he waved his hand and said he was 'just a farmer' who had merely been asked to help. The headmaster corrected him because he did not agree: in his view a man who can teach children to plant a field IS their teacher for that lesson, whatever his occupation and whatever qualifications he lacks. By insisting on the title 'farming teacher' he was giving the man the standing he deserved in front of the class, and making sure the children treated the lesson as a real one.

NOTE 'why do you think' — the question invites inference, so a good answer goes beyond restating what happened to explain the headmaster's motive, using the passage's own statement that he thought it important for children to learn by seeing things done.

Q7. Because the farmer could teach the children what no qualified teacher in a classroom could. He knew the subject from a lifetime of doing it, and he taught it while doing it — his hands never stopped pulling weeds as he talked, and he showed the children how to dig, make furrows and spread fertiliser by demonstrating each step. He also taught far beyond the lesson set, telling them about insects, birds, butterflies and the weather, and he was able to reassure a frightened boy about the snake because he genuinely knew that the snakes there were harmless. The headmaster's belief that children should 'learn by actually seeing things done' was fully justified by the result.

Q9. APPROACH
1. Read the whole passage once before looking at the questions, then read the questions, then read the passage again. The second reading is directed and much faster.
2. Answer in the order set — the questions almost always follow the order of the passage, so each answer points you to where the next one is.
3. Match the length of the answer to the marks: one mark means one sentence, two marks usually means two points or a point with its evidence.
4. Answer the question actually asked. 'Why do you think' wants a reason; 'which sentence' wants a quotation; 'what did he say' wants his words.

COMMONEST LOSSES OF MARKS
- Copying whole sentences when the instruction says 'in your own words'.
- Paraphrasing when the question asks which sentence or which word — there, exactness is required.
- Writing everything known about the topic in the hope that the answer is somewhere inside it. Examiners mark the answer given, and an answer containing a contradiction scores nothing even if one half is right.
- Importing knowledge from outside the passage. Every answer must be supported by the text.
- Leaving the inference questions ('why do you think', 'what does this suggest') to be answered by restating the events. These carry the discriminating marks.

C. Summary Writing

Q10. MODEL SUMMARY (49 words)

The farmer taught the children to weed, explaining that weeds are hardy, outgrow crops, block their sunlight, shelter harmful insects and drain the soil's nourishment. He then demonstrated digging, making furrows and spreading fertiliser. He also reassured them about snakes and spoke of insects, birds, butterflies and the weather.

HOW IT WAS BUILT
1. Underline only what answers the question set. The question asks what he TAUGHT, so his clothes, Totto-chan's recognition of him and the headmaster's argument about qualifications are all irrelevant, however interesting.
2. List the teaching points from the passage: weeding; the five facts about weeds; digging; furrows; fertiliser; the snake; insects, birds, butterflies, weather.
3. Join them into continuous prose, not a list of bullet points.
4. Count the words and cut. Cutting is where the marks are won — 'he explained to them that weeds are very hardy plants' becomes 'weeds are hardy'.

SUMMARY RULES THAT APPLY EVERY YEAR
- Obey the word limit; examiners stop reading at it, so anything beyond is wasted.
- Write one continuous paragraph in your own words, in the third person and the past tense.
- Include no examples, no direct speech, no quotation, no comment of your own.
- Summarise only what the question asks for, which is never the whole passage.
- Write a rough version first, count, then write the fair copy — that is what the reading time and the working space are for.

Q11. PROCEDURE

1. READ THE INSTRUCTION FIRST, before the passage if possible. It always names a specific topic — 'what the farmer taught', 'how the writer escaped', 'the difficulties faced by the family' — and never asks for the passage as a whole. Half the marks lost on summary are lost by candidates who summarise everything.

2. UNDERLINE, on the question paper, only the points that answer that instruction. Ignore everything else however striking.

3. NUMBER the underlined points. You will usually find eight to twelve; the mark scheme normally awards a mark for each of a set of expected points, which is why finding them all matters more than elegant phrasing.

4. DRAFT in continuous prose, in your own words, third person, past tense. Do not begin 'This passage tells us that…' — begin with the content itself.

5. COUNT. If you are over, cut adjectives, examples and repetition first; cut a whole point only as a last resort.

6. FAIR-COPY it, and write the word count at the end. Some boards deduct for its absence and none penalise its presence.

WHAT IS NEVER INCLUDED: your own opinion; direct speech; quotations; figures of speech from the original; illustrations and examples; and anything not present in the passage.
