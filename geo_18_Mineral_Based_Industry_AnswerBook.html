B. Short Answer Type

Q6. BASIC, because its product is the raw material of almost every other industry. Machinery and machine tools, railways and rolling stock, ships, motor vehicles, bridges, buildings, pipelines and agricultural implements are all made of steel, so no other industry can develop until this one exists. The per capita consumption of steel is taken as the index of a country's industrial development.

HEAVY, because both the raw materials and the finished product are bulky and weighty. Iron ore, coking coal, limestone, dolomite and manganese must be brought in by the trainload, and the pig iron, ingots, plates and rails produced are equally heavy. This weight is what dictates where the plants are put: transport costs are so large a part of the total that the plant must be near its raw materials.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q7. FACTORS. Nearness to IRON ORE; nearness to COKING COAL; nearness to LIMESTONE, DOLOMITE and MANGANESE, which are needed as flux and alloy; a plentiful supply of WATER for cooling; cheap POWER; adequate TRANSPORT, by rail and preferably by port; abundant LABOUR; and a MARKET for the product. Since ore and coal are the heaviest inputs, the plant is normally placed between the two.

TISCO, JAMSHEDPUR (Jharkhand). Iron ore comes from Noamundi and the Singhbhum belt close by; coking coal from Jharia and Raniganj, both within easy reach; limestone and dolomite from Odisha and Chhattisgarh; manganese from Odisha. Water comes from the Subarnarekha and Kharkai rivers, which meet there. It is on the Kolkata–Mumbai railway, with the port of Kolkata for export, and labour is plentiful from the surrounding densely peopled districts. Every requirement lies within a short radius, which is why Tata chose the site.

VISAKHAPATNAM (Andhra Pradesh). The one shore-based plant among the four, and its location is decided by the PORT. Iron ore comes from Bailadila in Chhattisgarh by rail; coking coal is largely IMPORTED, and a coastal site allows it to be landed directly; the port also allows finished steel to be exported. Water comes from the Yeleru reservoir, and power from the regional grid. It is the most modern of the four, and it demonstrates the newer principle that a steel plant may be placed at a port rather than at a coalfield when coal must be imported.

(BHILAI in Chhattisgarh — ore from Dalli-Rajhara, coal from Korba and Jharia, limestone from Nandini, water from the Tandula reservoir; built with Soviet help. ROURKELA in Odisha — ore from Sundargarh and Keonjhar, coal from Jharia and Talcher, water from the Koel and Sankh; built with German help.)

Q8. The petrochemical industry manufactures, from crude petroleum and natural gas, a very wide range of products: synthetic fibres (nylon, polyester, acrylic), plastics, synthetic rubber, detergents, dyes, insecticides, fertilizers, paints and drugs. It is among the fastest-growing industries in India.

ITS ADVANTAGES are that its products are cheaper, lighter, more durable and often more versatile than the natural materials they replace, and that it reduces the pressure on natural resources — synthetic fibre relieves the demand for cotton and land, synthetic rubber for plantations.

LOCATION follows the refineries and the ports, since crude is the raw material and much of it is imported. The chief centres are MUMBAI and Thane in Maharashtra, VADODARA, Jamnagar and Hazira in Gujarat, and Chennai, Kochi, Barauni and Haldia. Mumbai is the largest, drawing on the Mumbai High offshore field.

PROBLEMS: dependence on imported crude, whose price fluctuates; heavy capital cost; and the pollution and the non-biodegradable plastic waste the industry generates, which connects it directly to the waste-management chapters of this syllabus.

Q9. WHY IT HAS GROWN. It is a LIGHT industry: its raw materials and its products are small and valuable, so transport costs hardly matter and the plant can be located wherever the skilled people are. India has a very large pool of SKILLED, ENGLISH-SPEAKING TECHNICAL LABOUR, trained in the engineering colleges and institutes of technology, and at wages far below those of the West. Government policy encouraged it through software technology parks, special economic zones and tax concessions. The domestic MARKET is enormous and growing, and the industry needs comparatively little raw material, power or water.

THREE CENTRES. BENGALURU, much the largest, called the Silicon Valley of India, with a pleasant climate, research institutions and a long-established base in aviation and electronics. HYDERABAD (Cyberabad), and CHENNAI. Also important: Pune, the National Capital Region including Noida and Gurugram, Mumbai and Kolkata.

The industry covers both hardware — computers, telecommunications equipment, television and consumer electronics — and software and IT services, in which India is a world leader and which earn a very large part of the country's foreign exchange.

Q11. (a) A mini-steel plant makes steel in an ELECTRIC ARC FURNACE from scrap and sponge iron, not from ore in a blast furnace. It therefore burns NO COKING COAL at all, so it saves the country’s limited good coking coal for the big integrated plants.

(b) Visakhapatnam is the only Indian steel plant on the COAST. It can import coking coal and export finished steel directly by sea through its own port, cheaply and quickly, while its iron ore comes by a short rail haul from the Bailadila mines — so both raw materials and market are easy to reach.

(c) Because India has a very large pool of skilled, English-speaking, comparatively low-cost ENGINEERS and technicians; a huge and fast-growing home MARKET for phones, computers and appliances; strong government support (Software Technology Parks, ‘Make in India’, PLI schemes); and established hubs (Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, Noida) with the needed infrastructure.

Q14. (a) A PUBLIC-SECTOR industry — it is owned and run by the Government of India (through Rashtriya Ispat Nigam Ltd).

(b) A CO-OPERATIVE sector industry is owned and managed jointly by the people who supply its raw material or use its services, who pool their resources and share the profit — for example the many SUGAR MILLS of Maharashtra owned by the cane-growers’ co-operatives, and co-operative dairies.

(c) An INTEGRATED steel plant is one that carries out ALL the stages under one roof — preparing the ore, making pig iron in the blast furnace, converting it to steel, and rolling the steel into finished shapes (sheets, rails, bars). Bhilai, Rourkela, Durgapur, Bokaro, Jamshedpur and Visakhapatnam are integrated plants; a mini-steel plant does only the last stages, from scrap.
