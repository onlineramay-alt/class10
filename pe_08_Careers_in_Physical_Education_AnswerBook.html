The syllabus names six career options and asks for 'a brief understanding' of each: coach, physical education teacher, professional sportsperson, sports management, commentator and official. For each, know what the work involves and what qualification it needs.

Q1. (a) B.P.Ed. — Bachelor of Physical Education, followed for many posts by M.P.Ed. (Master of Physical Education). Diploma courses and NIS certification from the National Institute of Sports, Patiala, are the corresponding qualifications on the coaching side.

B. Short Answer Type

Q2. (i) COACH. Trains sportspersons or teams to improve performance — planning the training programme, teaching and correcting technique, developing tactics, selecting the side, and preparing players mentally for competition. Qualification: a diploma or certificate from the National Institute of Sports (NIS), Patiala, or a degree in physical education, together with experience in the sport.

(ii) PHYSICAL EDUCATION TEACHER. Teaches physical education in a school or college — conducting classes, organising the games programme and annual sports, coaching school teams, and looking after the health and fitness of the pupils. Qualification: B.P.Ed., and often M.P.Ed. for senior posts.

(iii) PROFESSIONAL SPORTSPERSON. Plays a sport as a full-time occupation, earning through contracts, prize money, salary from an employer's team and endorsements. It demands exceptional talent, years of training and acceptance of a short career, but is the most visible of the options.

(iv) SPORTS MANAGEMENT. Handles the business and organisation of sport — running clubs, academies, stadiums and federations; organising tournaments; managing sponsorship, marketing, ticketing and finance; and acting as agent for players. Qualification: a degree or diploma in sports management or business administration.

(v) COMMENTATOR. Describes and analyses a match live on television, radio or the internet, explaining play and tactics to the audience. It requires deep knowledge of the game, fluency of speech, a good voice, quick thinking and impartiality; many commentators are former players or journalists.

(vi) OFFICIAL — umpire, referee or judge. Conducts the match, applies the rules, keeps the score and time, and takes decisions on the field, whose authority must be accepted. It requires a thorough knowledge of the laws, physical fitness to keep up with play, and above all impartiality and firmness under pressure. Officials are certified by the state and national federations of the sport.

(OTHER CAREERS worth mentioning if the question allows: sports physiotherapist, sports nutritionist, sports psychologist, fitness trainer, yoga instructor, sports journalist, and posts in the armed forces, police and public-sector undertakings under the sports quota.)

Q3. QUALITIES.
SOUND KNOWLEDGE of the sport — its technique, tactics, rules and the science of training — and the willingness to keep learning as the sport changes.
COMMUNICATION — the ability to explain and demonstrate clearly, and to say the same thing in a different way to a player who has not understood it the first time.
PATIENCE AND OBSERVATION — skill is built slowly, and the coach must see the fault before it can be corrected.
IMPARTIALITY — treating every player on merit, with no favouritism in selection.
DISCIPLINE AND CHARACTER — the coach sets the standard the team adopts, so punctuality, fair play and self-control must be modelled and not merely demanded.
MOTIVATION — the ability to encourage a player who has failed and to steady one who has succeeded.

RESPONSIBILITIES.
To plan and conduct scientific training suited to the individuals and to the season, applying the principles of training.
To teach and correct technique and to develop tactics.
To select the team fairly and to prepare it for competition.
To look after the players' SAFETY — warm-up, condition of ground and equipment, and the immediate management of injury — and to see that no player is pushed beyond what is safe for their age and stage of growth.
To care for the players' overall welfare, including their studies, diet, rest and conduct, since a school coach is dealing with children first and athletes second.
To keep records of performance and to maintain discipline within the squad.
