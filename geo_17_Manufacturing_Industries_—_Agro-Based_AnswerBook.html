B. Short Answer Type

Q2. IMPORTANCE. Manufacturing converts raw material into finished goods of far greater value, so it is the chief means of increasing national wealth. It provides EMPLOYMENT to millions and absorbs the surplus labour of agriculture, reducing the pressure on the land. It reduces DEPENDENCE ON IMPORTS and earns foreign exchange through exports of textiles, engineering goods, chemicals and software. It supplies agriculture itself with fertilizers, pesticides, pumps, tractors and implements, so that farming can modernise. And it develops backward regions, brings in transport, power and towns, and raises the general standard of living.

CLASSIFICATION, on four bases.
BY RAW MATERIAL — agro-based (sugar, cotton textiles, jute, vegetable oil) and mineral-based (iron and steel, cement, aluminium, petrochemicals).
BY SIZE AND CAPITAL — large-scale, small-scale and cottage industries.
BY OWNERSHIP — public sector (Bhilai, Rourkela), private sector (TISCO), joint sector and cooperative sector (the sugar mills of Maharashtra).
BY THE WEIGHT OF THE PRODUCT — heavy industries such as iron and steel, and light industries such as electronics.

Q3. WHY AT THE CANE FIELDS. Sugarcane is both WEIGHT-LOSING — only about a tenth of the weight of the cane becomes sugar — and PERISHABLE, losing sucrose from the moment it is cut, so it must be crushed within twenty-four hours. Carrying it any distance means paying freight on waste and losing sugar on the way. The mill therefore goes to the cane.

WHY THE INDUSTRY IS MOVING SOUTH. The southern states — Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh — have a longer crushing season, because the tropical climate allows cane to be grown and harvested over more months of the year, so the mills work longer and are more economical. The cane grown there has a HIGHER SUCROSE CONTENT, because the maritime climate is equable and there is no frost, so the yield of sugar per tonne is greater. The mills are NEWER AND LARGER, with better machinery, and many are run on the COOPERATIVE model, which has proved efficient in Maharashtra. And ports are near, which helps export.

The north — Uttar Pradesh and Bihar — still grows the largest area of cane, but its shorter season, lower sucrose content and older, smaller mills have cost it the lead in sugar production.

Q4. WHY MUMBAI FIRST. Every requirement met in one place. RAW MATERIAL — the black soil cotton tracts of Maharashtra and Gujarat lie immediately inland. CLIMATE — the humid maritime air keeps the yarn from breaking, which mattered greatly before artificial humidifiers. PORT — Mumbai could import machinery from Britain and export finished cloth. POWER — coal came by sea, and later hydel power from the Western Ghats. CAPITAL — Parsi and Gujarati merchants who had made money in trade were willing to invest. LABOUR — plentiful and cheap, from the densely populated Deccan. And TRANSPORT — the railways ran inland from the port.

WHY IT HAS DISPERSED. The industry is now spread through Ahmedabad, Surat, Coimbatore, Kanpur, Indore, Nagpur and Kolkata. The reasons are that electricity can now be transmitted anywhere, so a coastal coal supply no longer matters; artificial humidifiers remove the climatic advantage; land and labour in Mumbai became expensive and congested; states offered incentives to attract mills; and it is often cheaper to spin near the cotton fields and weave near the market. Coimbatore, drawing on hydel power from the Western Ghats, is now called the Manchester of South India.

Q5. India is the second largest producer of silk in the world and the largest consumer. SERICULTURE — the rearing of silkworms on mulberry leaves, the reeling of the cocoons and the weaving of the thread — is a cottage and small-scale industry, employing very large numbers in rural areas, particularly women, and requiring little capital.

FOUR VARIETIES are produced. MULBERRY silk, the finest and about nine-tenths of the output, from worms fed on mulberry leaves — chiefly Karnataka, which leads, with Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and West Bengal. And three wild or 'vanya' silks: TASAR, from worms fed on arjun and asan trees, in Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh and Odisha; ERI, in Assam; and MUGA, the golden silk unique to Assam.

CENTRES OF WEAVING: Bengaluru and Mysuru in Karnataka; Kanchipuram in Tamil Nadu; Varanasi in Uttar Pradesh; Murshidabad in West Bengal; and Sualkuchi in Assam.

PROBLEMS: competition from cheap imported Chinese silk and from synthetic substitutes, low productivity of some indigenous worm varieties, and the exploitation of weavers by middlemen.
