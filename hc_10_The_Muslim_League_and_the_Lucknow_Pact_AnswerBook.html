C. Short Answer Type

Q14. (i) To promote among the Muslims of India feelings of loyalty to the British Government, and to remove any misconception that might arise as to the intentions of that government towards them. (ii) To protect and advance the political rights and interests of the Muslims of India, and to represent their needs and aspirations to the government in temperate language. (iii) To prevent the rise among the Muslims of India of any feeling of hostility towards other communities, without prejudice to the other objects of the League.

In practice the League soon came to press for a fourth object, secured in 1909: separate electorates, in which only Muslims voted for Muslim representatives.

Official Answer

Q15. (i) The British policy of divide and rule. Having seen in the Congress the beginnings of an all-India movement, the government worked to prevent Muslims from joining it, and encouraged a separate Muslim organisation. The Simla Deputation of 1906, received sympathetically by Lord Minto, was the immediate prelude to the League's foundation a few months later.

(ii) The influence of Sir Syed Ahmad Khan and the Aligarh movement. He urged Muslims to take to Western education and to keep out of the Congress, arguing that their interests lay in loyal cooperation with the British rather than in a movement which, being based on numbers, must in his view end in Hindu rule.

(iii) Educational and economic backwardness, and the fear that flowed from it. Muslims had held back from English education after 1857 and so were thinly represented in government service, the professions and modern trade. In any representative system based on counting heads they would be a permanent minority, and the anti-Partition agitation of 1905 — which they were told was directed against a measure that benefited them — sharpened that fear into the demand for a separate political body.

Official Answer

Q16. (i) It united the two great Indian political organisations. For the first and, as it proved, the last time, the Congress and the Muslim League met in the same city, agreed a common scheme of constitutional reform and presented it jointly to the British — depriving the government of its standing argument that Indians could not agree among themselves and that British rule was therefore necessary to hold them apart.

(ii) It reunited the Congress itself. The Moderates and the Extremists, divided since Surat in 1907, came together again at this same Lucknow session, so that the national movement recovered both its unity and Tilak's leadership at the moment when the joint demand was made. The pressure the two Pacts created led directly to the Montagu Declaration of 1917 and the reforms of 1919.

Official Answer

Q17. It was the high water mark because nothing before or after brought the two organisations so close. They sat in the same city, agreed on a single scheme of reform, and put it to the government together; Jinnah, who belonged to both bodies, was called the ambassador of Hindu-Muslim unity; and the British lost, for a time, the argument that Indians were too divided to govern themselves.

It is called a costly mistake because of the price the Congress paid. By accepting separate electorates and weightage, it conceded the principle that Muslims were a distinct political community whose interests could be represented only by Muslims chosen by Muslims. That principle, once admitted by the national organisation itself, could not afterwards be denied; it entrenched communal representation in every later constitutional scheme, and gave the League a permanent institutional basis for the claim that it alone spoke for Muslims. The unity of 1916 lasted a few years; the concession outlasted it by thirty, and led where the Congress had not intended.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q18. The Congress, by 1906, had come to demand Swaraj: Dadabhai Naoroji named self-government as its goal at the Calcutta session of that year, and its Assertive wing was already preaching Swadeshi, boycott and passive resistance against the government.

The Muslim League began from the opposite premise. Its very first stated object was to promote among Muslims feelings of loyalty to the British Government and to remove misconceptions about its intentions. It sought not to end British rule but to secure, under it, a protected share of representation and office for Muslims — which is why it welcomed the separate electorates granted in 1909.

The Lucknow Pact of 1916 briefly bridged that difference, the League joining the Congress in demanding self-government; but the two organisations had started from different assumptions about whether British rule was the problem or the protection.

(ii) To promote among Indian Muslims feelings of loyalty to the British Government; and to protect and advance the political rights and interests of Indian Muslims and place their needs before the government.

Q21. (i) That the League was founded not to oppose British rule but to work within it and to secure its favour. Loyalty is placed first among its objects, before even the protection of Muslim interests, and the second half of the sentence — removing 'misconceptions' about government measures — casts the League as an interpreter of the government to its own community rather than a critic of it. The immediate reference was to the Partition of Bengal, which the League wished Muslims to see as beneficial.

(ii) The contrast is sharp. In that same year, 1906, Dadabhai Naoroji as Congress president declared Swaraj — self-government — to be the goal of the Congress, and its Assertive wing was already preaching Swadeshi, boycott and passive resistance. Where the Congress had come to regard British rule as the obstacle, the League regarded it as the protection.
