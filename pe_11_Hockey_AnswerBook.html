B. Terminology and Very Short Answer

Q3. (a) PENALTY CORNER (short corner) — awarded for an offence by a defender inside the shooting circle, or a deliberate offence within the 23 m area. The ball is pushed or hit from the goal line at least 10 m from the nearest goalpost; a maximum of five defenders including the goalkeeper stand behind the goal line, and the attackers stand outside the circle until the ball is played.

(b) PENALTY STROKE — the most severe award, for an offence in the circle that prevented a probable goal, or for a deliberate offence in the circle. One attacker takes a push or flick at goal from a spot 6.40 m in front of the goal, against the goalkeeper alone.

(c) OBSTRUCTION — using the body or the stick to prevent an opponent from playing the ball, or shielding the ball with the body or stick. It is a foul because in hockey every player must have a fair chance to play the ball.

(d) THE CARDS — a GREEN card is a warning and carries a temporary suspension of two minutes; a YELLOW card is a temporary suspension of at least five minutes; a RED card is permanent exclusion from the match, and the team plays on short.

(Also worth knowing: bully — the old method of restarting; long corner; free hit; advancing; sticks; and the 23 m area.)

Q5. WHAT THE DIAGRAM MUST SHOW, with these measurements labelled:

MARKINGS.
— The CENTRE LINE across the middle.
— The two 23 METRE LINES, each 22.90 m from the goal line, dividing the field into quarters.
— The SHOOTING CIRCLE ('D') at each end: a semicircle of radius 14.63 m drawn from each goalpost, joined by a straight line parallel to the goal line. A goal can be scored only from within it.
— The DOTTED LINE 5 m outside and parallel to the shooting circle, used at penalty corners.
— The PENALTY SPOT, 6.40 m in front of the centre of each goal.
— PENALTY CORNER MARKS on the goal line, 5 m and 10 m from each goalpost, and on the sideline 14.63 m from each goal line.

THE GOALS. 3.66 m wide (12 feet) by 2.14 m high (7 feet), with the net supported by backboards 460 mm high and sideboards 1.20 m long.

Q6. THE STICK. Traditionally of wood, now commonly of composite materials. It has a flat playing side on the LEFT only — the ball may not be played with the rounded back. The head is curved (the 'hook'). The whole stick, including any tape, must pass through a ring of 51 mm internal diameter. Maximum weight 737 g. The stick must have no sharp edges or dangerous projections.

THE BALL. Spherical and hard, traditionally of solid plastic with a cork core, usually white (or any colour contrasting with the pitch). CIRCUMFERENCE 224 to 235 mm; WEIGHT 156 to 163 g. The surface may be smooth or dimpled.

THE GOALKEEPER'S PROTECTIVE EQUIPMENT — extensive, and the goalkeeper must wear a differently coloured shirt from both teams.
— HEADGEAR: a full-face helmet with a throat protector, compulsory within the 23 m area.
— LEG GUARDS and KICKERS (foot protectors) over the boots.
— HAND PROTECTORS or gloves, one of which is the stopping glove.
— BODY ARMOUR: chest guard, abdominal protector and, commonly, arm, elbow and thigh protection.
All protective equipment must be worn for the goalkeeper's own safety and is regulated in size so that it protects without unfairly blocking the goal.

FIELD PLAYERS may wear shin guards, a mouthguard and gloves, and face masks are permitted when defending a penalty corner.

Q7. (i) DRIBBLING. Carrying the ball with the stick under control. The Indian dribble — moving the ball rapidly from side to side by turning the stick over the ball — and the loose dribble for speed in open space. The left hand controls the stick at the top, the right hand lower down, and the head stays up.

(ii) PASSING AND HITTING. The PUSH, in which the ball is already in contact with the stick and is pushed away with a sweeping action — quick and accurate, and the standard pass. The HIT, with the hands together at the top of the stick and a backswing, for distance. The FLICK, a raised push used to score and at penalty strokes. The SCOOP, lifting the ball over an opponent's stick.

(iii) STOPPING or RECEIVING. Presenting the flat face of the stick to the ball with the stick angled slightly forward so the ball does not bounce up, giving with the hands to deaden the pace, and controlling it into a position from which the next stroke can be played.

(iv) TACKLING AND DODGING. The JAB tackle, a quick one-handed poke at the ball; the block tackle, presenting the stick flat to stop the ball. And on the attacking side, DODGING — beating an opponent by pushing the ball to one side of him and running the other, or by the reverse-stick dodge.

(Also acceptable: GOALKEEPING — kicking and padding the ball clear, and narrowing the angle.)
