B. Short Answer Type

Q5. Segregation is the sorting of waste into separate categories at the place and moment it is generated — in the household, the office or the factory — rather than at the dump.

HOW IT IS DONE. Separate bins are used, and municipalities in India have standardised the colours: a GREEN bin for wet or biodegradable waste — kitchen scraps, food, garden trimmings; a BLUE bin for dry recyclable waste — paper, plastic, glass, metal; and a third, often BLACK or RED, for hazardous and domestic sanitary waste — batteries, medicines, syringes, paint, e-waste. Electronic waste and construction debris are collected separately again.

WHY IT MATTERS SO MUCH. Once wet and dry waste are mixed the damage cannot be undone: the food waste can no longer be composted because it is contaminated with glass and plastic, and the paper, cardboard and metal can no longer be recycled because they are soaked and soiled. A mixed load can only be dumped. Segregation at source is therefore the step on which composting, recycling and every other method depends, and it costs nothing but the habit.

Q6. OPEN DUMPING is simply the tipping of untreated, unsegregated waste onto low-lying or waste land in the open. It is cheap and needs no preparation, and it is what most Indian towns still do. Its consequences are those described in the previous chapter: it spoils the landscape, breeds flies, rats and disease, burns and smoulders releasing toxic smoke, releases methane, and allows leachate to percolate down into the ground water unchecked.

A SANITARY LANDFILL is an engineered site. The ground is first LINED with clay and impermeable plastic so that leachate cannot reach the ground water; the leachate is collected through a drainage system and treated; the waste is spread in layers, COMPACTED and COVERED with earth at the end of each day, which prevents smell, flies and rats and stops the waste blowing about; methane is collected through vents and may be burnt for power; and when the site is full it is sealed with a final cover and the land can be reclaimed as a park or playing field.

The difference is between abandoning waste and containing it. A landfill costs far more and requires careful siting away from settlements, rivers and the water table, but it is the only acceptable way of disposing of what cannot be composted or recycled.

Q7. COMPOSTING is the controlled decomposition of biodegradable waste — kitchen scraps, leaves, garden trimmings, cow dung — by micro-organisms in the presence of air and moisture, producing a dark, crumbly, odourless manure called compost. The waste is heaped in a pit or bin, kept damp and turned occasionally so that air reaches it, and in six to eight weeks it becomes manure.

VERMICOMPOSTING uses EARTHWORMS to accelerate and improve the process. Waste is placed in a pit or container with a bed of moist soil and cow dung, and earthworms — usually the red wriggler — are introduced. The worms eat the organic matter and excrete it as fine granular castings, and the pit is kept moist, shaded and undisturbed. The vermicompost is ready in about six to eight weeks.

ADVANTAGES. It converts a waste into a valuable product at almost no cost. Vermicompost is richer than ordinary compost in nitrogen, phosphorus, potash and beneficial micro-organisms, and it improves the texture, aeration and water-holding capacity of the soil. It is entirely organic, so it replaces chemical fertilizer, which is expensive and damages the soil over time. It removes the largest fraction of municipal waste — the wet fraction is over half of it in India — from the disposal problem altogether, and it can be done by a household, a school or a village without machinery.

Q8. REDUCE — generating less waste in the first place, which is the most effective of the three because waste not created needs no disposal. Examples: carrying a cloth bag to the market instead of taking plastic ones; refusing over-packaged goods, disposable cups, straws and cutlery; printing on both sides of the paper; and buying in bulk rather than in single-use sachets.

REUSE — using an article again, in its existing form, instead of throwing it away. Examples: refilling glass and plastic bottles and jars for storage; using old newspapers for wrapping and packing; passing on clothes, books and furniture rather than discarding them; and using rainwater or waste water from washing for the garden.

RECYCLE — processing waste material to make something new. Examples: sending paper, glass, metal and plastic for recycling into fresh paper, bottles, cans and fibre; composting kitchen waste into manure; and the recovery of copper, gold and other metals from electronic waste.

THE ORDER MATTERS, and examiners give credit for saying so: reducing is better than reusing, and reusing better than recycling, because recycling itself consumes energy and water and degrades the material a little each time. The three Rs are a hierarchy, not a list.
