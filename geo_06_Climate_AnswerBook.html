B. Short Answer Type

Q8. (i) LATITUDE. The Tropic of Cancer passes through the middle of the country, so the south lies in the tropics and has high temperatures all year, while the north lies in the sub-tropics and has a distinct cold season.

(ii) ALTITUDE. Temperature falls about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 metres of ascent, which is why hill stations are cool and why the Himalayas are snow-covered while the plains below them are hot.

(iii) DISTANCE FROM THE SEA. Coastal places such as Mumbai and Chennai have an equable climate — a small annual range of temperature — because water heats and cools slowly. Inland places such as Delhi and Kanpur have an extreme or continental climate, very hot in summer and cold in winter.

(iv) RELIEF. The Himalayas shut out the cold winds from Central Asia and force the monsoon to shed its rain on the plains; the Western Ghats give heavy rain on their windward western slopes and leave a dry rain-shadow on the Deccan behind them.

(Two more may be added: the direction of the prevailing winds — the monsoons themselves — and upper-air circulation such as the jet stream and the Western Disturbances.)

Official Answer

Q9. In winter a HIGH PRESSURE prevails over the land, because the land cools faster than the sea; the air over it becomes cold, dense and heavy and sinks. Winds therefore blow outwards, from the land to the sea — which is why most of India is dry in winter.

Two local winds: the LOO, the hot, dry, dust-laden wind that blows over the northern plains in May and June and can be fatal; and the KAAL BAISAKHI (or Norwesters), the violent evening thunderstorms of Bengal and Assam in April and May. Others acceptable: the MANGO SHOWERS of Kerala and Karnataka, which help ripen the mango crop, and the CHERRY BLOSSOM showers of Karnataka, which help the coffee.

Q11. KERALA. The Arabian Sea branch of the south-west monsoon strikes the Kerala coast about the first of June — the 'burst' of the monsoon — and, as the monsoon withdraws from north to south, Kerala is the last part of the mainland it leaves, about the end of November.

WEATHER DURING THE RETREATING MONSOON (October–November): the low pressure over the northern plains weakens and moves south, and the winds begin to blow from the land. Skies clear, and temperatures rise again — a spell of hot, oppressive weather known as 'October heat', made unpleasant by high humidity left behind by the monsoon. Rainfall over most of the country ceases, but the Coromandel coast of Tamil Nadu receives its main rain from the north-east monsoon crossing the Bay of Bengal. This is also the season of tropical cyclones in the Bay of Bengal, which strike the eastern coast, sometimes with great destruction.

METHOD FOR EVERY CLIMATE-DATA QUESTION. Calculate three things before answering anything: the ANNUAL RANGE of temperature (a large range means inland, a small range means coastal); the TOTAL and the DISTRIBUTION of rainfall (June–September means south-west monsoon; October–December means north-east monsoon and therefore the Coromandel coast; rain in winter means the far north-west and Western Disturbances); and the HOTTEST MONTH (if it is May, the station is inland or on the west coast; if the rain comes in the last quarter, it is on the east coast).

Q14. The monsoon is a seasonal reversal of wind direction, caused by the different rates at which land and sea heat and cool.

IN SUMMER the sun is overhead in the northern hemisphere. The landmass of Asia heats rapidly, and an intense LOW PRESSURE develops over north-western India and central Asia. The sea, heating far more slowly, remains comparatively cool and therefore under HIGH PRESSURE. Air moves from high to low, so moisture-laden winds blow from the Indian Ocean towards the land — the SOUTH-WEST MONSOON. Crossing the equator, they are deflected to the right by the Coriolis force, which gives them their south-westerly direction. They arrive in two branches: the ARABIAN SEA branch, which strikes the Western Ghats and gives heavy rain to the west coast, and the BAY OF BENGAL branch, which is deflected by the Himalayas up the Ganga valley and westwards across the plains.

IN WINTER the position is reversed. The land cools quickly and develops HIGH PRESSURE; the sea, retaining its heat, is relatively LOW. Winds therefore blow outwards from the land to the sea — the NORTH-EAST MONSOON — and being dry land winds they bring no rain to most of India. The exception is the Coromandel coast, because these winds cross the Bay of Bengal, pick up moisture, and shed it on Tamil Nadu.

The apparent northward movement of the sun, the shifting of the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone, and the behaviour of the upper-air jet stream are the further mechanisms behind this seasonal switch.

C. Structured / Part II Questions

Q24. (i) Any two of: latitude, altitude, distance from the sea, relief, and the direction of the prevailing winds. (See the four-mark answer above for the explanations.)

(ii) High pressure prevails over the land in winter, because the land cools faster than the sea and the cold dense air sinks; winds therefore blow outwards from land to sea. Two local winds: the Loo and the Kaal Baisakhi (or the Mango Showers).

(iii) (a) Temperature falls about 6.5 °C per 1,000 m of altitude, so hill stations remain cool while the plains are at 40 °C. (b) Cherrapunji is on the windward funnel-shaped slope of the Khasi hills where the monsoon is forced to rise and is wrung dry; Shillong lies behind those slopes in the rain-shadow. (c) Kanpur is inland with a continental climate and a large annual range, and lies further north; Mumbai is coastal and moderated by the sea.

(iv) The ARABIAN SEA BRANCH strikes the western coast head-on and is forced to rise over the Western Ghats, giving very heavy rain — over 200 cm — on the windward western slopes, and leaving a marked rain-shadow on the Deccan plateau behind them; part of it travels north over Gujarat and Rajasthan, where, running parallel to the Aravallis rather than across them, it gives little rain.

The BAY OF BENGAL BRANCH strikes the north-east, is deflected westwards by the Himalayas, and travels up the Ganga valley. Because it moves away from its source of moisture as it goes, rainfall decreases steadily from east to west — about 200 cm at the head of the Bay, 119 cm at Kolkata, 64 cm at Allahabad and 20 cm at Delhi. It is this branch that gives Cherrapunji its extraordinary totals.
