Building and repair is the function of proteins; protection against disease belongs to vitamins and minerals; and aiding digestion is the function of fibre and water.

B. Very Short Answer

Q3. HEALTH EDUCATION is the process of imparting knowledge, attitudes and practices concerning health, so that individuals and communities can take informed decisions and adopt habits that promote and maintain their own well-being and prevent disease.

A BALANCED DIET is a diet that contains all the essential food constituents — carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, water and fibre — in the CORRECT PROPORTIONS and in the QUANTITY needed for the individual's age, sex, body build and level of physical activity.

The words 'correct proportions' are essential to the definition: a diet containing everything in the wrong amounts is not balanced.

Q4. MALNUTRITION is the condition arising from a diet in which one or more nutrients are either deficient or present in excess, so that the body's requirements are not properly met.

UNDERNUTRITION — too little food, or food lacking in one or more essential nutrients. It causes loss of weight, stunted growth in children, weakness, anaemia, lowered resistance to infection and deficiency diseases such as kwashiorkor, marasmus, rickets and scurvy.

OVERNUTRITION — more food, particularly of fats and carbohydrates, than the body can use. It causes obesity, and with it a raised risk of diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease and joint problems.

Both are malnutrition, and a country may suffer from both at once.

C. Short Answer Type

Q5. (i) CARBOHYDRATES — the body's main source of ENERGY, and the fuel the muscles prefer during hard exercise. Sources: rice, wheat, bread, potato, sugar, banana.

(ii) PROTEINS — for the BUILDING and REPAIR of body tissue, growth, and the making of enzymes and hormones. Sources: milk, eggs, meat, fish, pulses, soya, nuts.

(iii) FATS — a CONCENTRATED store of energy, giving more than twice the energy of carbohydrate for the same weight; they insulate the body, cushion the organs and carry the fat-soluble vitamins. Sources: ghee, butter, oil, nuts, cheese.

(iv) VITAMINS — PROTECTIVE substances required in very small amounts to regulate body processes and prevent deficiency diseases. Sources: fruits and green vegetables (vitamin C), carrots and liver (vitamin A), sunlight and fish oil (vitamin D).

(v) MINERALS — for building BONES AND TEETH, for the formation of haemoglobin, and for the working of nerve and muscle. Sources: milk and cheese (calcium), green leafy vegetables and liver (iron), iodised salt (iodine).

(vi) WATER — the medium of every body process: it carries nutrients and waste, regulates body temperature through sweat, and lubricates the joints. It makes up about two-thirds of body weight. Sources: drinking water, milk, juices, fruits and vegetables.

(vii) FIBRE or ROUGHAGE — indigestible plant material that adds bulk to the food, stimulates the movement of the intestine and PREVENTS CONSTIPATION. Sources: whole grains, salads, fruits with skin, green vegetables.

Q6. CALORIES. A sportsperson expends far more energy than a sedentary person, so the total calorie intake must be raised to match — often to 3,000–5,000 kcal a day depending on the sport, the body weight and the training load. Too few calories means the body breaks down its own muscle for fuel.

CARBOHYDRATES. These should form the LARGEST share of the diet, around 60–70 per cent of calories, because they are the muscles' preferred fuel and are stored as glycogen. Glycogen stores are limited and are exhausted in prolonged effort, which is what athletes call 'hitting the wall'; endurance athletes therefore practise CARBOHYDRATE LOADING before a long event, and take carbohydrate immediately after training, when the muscles restock glycogen fastest.

PROTEINS. Requirements are higher than for a non-athlete, because training breaks muscle down and it must be rebuilt — roughly 1.2 to 1.7 g per kilogram of body weight a day, against about 0.8 for a sedentary person. Strength and power athletes need the upper end. Excess protein beyond requirement is not converted into extra muscle; it is simply burnt or stored.

FLUIDS. The most immediately important of all. Even a 2 per cent loss of body weight through sweat measurably reduces performance, and severe dehydration is dangerous. The athlete must drink BEFORE, DURING and AFTER activity, not merely when thirsty, since thirst lags behind the actual need.

SALTS (electrolytes). Sweat carries away sodium, potassium and chloride as well as water. In prolonged exercise, especially in heat, these must be replaced — through an isotonic sports drink or lightly salted fluid — because replacing water alone can dilute the blood's sodium dangerously.

TIMING also matters: a main meal three to four hours before competition, light and high in carbohydrate; nothing heavy or unfamiliar immediately before; and recovery food within the first hour afterwards.

Q7. GUIDELINES.
(i) Meet the individual's actual ENERGY REQUIREMENT — which depends on age, sex, body weight and the type and amount of activity. A hockey player in season needs more than a chess player.
(ii) Include ALL SEVEN CONSTITUENTS in the right proportions, so that the diet is genuinely balanced rather than merely plentiful.
(iii) Prefer FRESH, SEASONAL and LOCALLY AVAILABLE foods, which are cheaper, fresher and more likely to be eaten.
(iv) VARY the menu, so that the diet does not become monotonous and the family will actually eat it, and so that nutrients come from several sources.
(v) Plan the TIMING around training and competition — a substantial meal three to four hours before, a light snack an hour before if needed, and recovery food soon after.
(vi) Respect the individual's likes, habits, culture and budget; the best diet is worthless if it is not eaten.

SAMPLE DAY FOR A SCHOOL SPORTSPERSON.
EARLY MORNING — a glass of milk with a banana or a few soaked almonds.
BREAKFAST — two parathas or idlis with vegetable, one egg or a bowl of curd, and a fruit; water.
MID-MORNING — a fruit or a handful of nuts.
LUNCH — rice and chapatti, dal, a green vegetable, curd, salad.
BEFORE PRACTICE — a banana or a light snack, and water; fluids during the session.
AFTER PRACTICE — a glass of milk or a fruit shake within the first hour, for recovery.
DINNER — chapatti, a protein dish (paneer, chicken, fish or pulses), vegetables, salad; taken early enough to digest before sleep.

Throughout the day: 2.5 to 3 litres of water, more in hot weather or on heavy training days.
