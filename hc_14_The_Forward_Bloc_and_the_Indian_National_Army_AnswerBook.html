C. Short Answer Type

Q13. (i) To consolidate the left wing — to bring together all the radical and anti-imperialist forces inside the Congress into one organised bloc, so that the demand for complete independence should not be bargained away in negotiation. (ii) To establish a socialist state, in which the state would own and control the principal means of production and distribution, and to reconstruct agriculture and industry on socialist lines. (iii) To abolish the zamindari system and free the peasantry from landlordism, redistributing land to those who tilled it. Bose also wished to see Hindi, written in the Devanagari script, adopted as the national language, and full equality for all citizens regardless of caste or creed.

Official Answer

Q14. We would begin by ending landlordism: the zamindari system would be abolished, and the land redistributed among the peasants who actually cultivate it, so that the largest class in the country ceases to work for a rentier. We would bring the principal industries, the banks and the means of transport under state ownership and control, so that production is planned for the needs of the people rather than for private profit, and we would reorganise agriculture on cooperative lines. We would work for equality of opportunity — free education, the abolition of caste disabilities, and equal rights for women — since a socialist state must remove social as well as economic privilege. And we would do all this only in a country that is completely free, for which we would keep up an uncompromising struggle against British rule rather than accept any settlement short of full independence.

Official Answer

Q15. (i) He gave the national movement its radical wing. Twice elected president of the Congress — at Haripura in 1938 and at Tripuri in 1939, the second time defeating Gandhi's own candidate — he resigned when the Working Committee would not support him, and founded the Forward Bloc in 1939 to unite the left in an uncompromising demand for complete independence and a socialist state.

(ii) He organised an armed challenge to British rule from outside India. Escaping from house arrest in Calcutta in January 1941, he made his way through Afghanistan to Germany and then by submarine to Japan; in July 1943 he took command of the Indian National Army at Singapore, and in October proclaimed the Provisional Government of Free India, which nine governments recognised. Under him the INA fought at Imphal and Kohima and raised the Indian flag at Moirang in Manipur.

(iii) He gave the movement an ideal of unity and sacrifice that outlasted his defeat. The INA was deliberately built without distinction of caste or religion and included a women's regiment; his slogans — 'Jai Hind', 'Delhi Chalo', 'Give me blood and I will give you freedom' — passed into the language, and the trial of his officers at the Red Fort in 1945 roused the whole country.

Official Answer

Q16. It was founded in 1942 by Captain Mohan Singh, from among the Indian prisoners of war taken by the Japanese in Malaya and Singapore, with the help of Rash Behari Bose. Subhas Chandra Bose took over and reorganised it in July 1943 as its Supreme Commander.

Its objectives were: (i) to liberate India from British rule by armed force, marching into the country from the east; (ii) to establish and sustain a provisional government of free India, which would be recognised internationally and would administer the liberated territory; and (iii) to build a disciplined national army representing the whole of India, in which no distinction of caste, province or religion was recognised, and in which women too should serve — hence the Rani of Jhansi Regiment.

Official Answer

Q17. After the surrender of Japan the British put the captured officers of the INA on trial for treason at the Red Fort in Delhi — choosing as the first case a Hindu, a Muslim and a Sikh together: Prem Kumar Sahgal, Shah Nawaz Khan and Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon.

The effect was the opposite of what was intended. The country treated the accused as patriots, not traitors; the Congress formed a defence committee in which Nehru put on his barrister's gown after twenty-five years; there were demonstrations, strikes and student riots in every major city, and the men were released after conviction because their sentences could not safely be carried out.

Its real significance was what it revealed about the army. Indian soldiers and policemen had watched their countrymen tried for having fought the British, and the government could see that the loyalty of the Indian forces — the ultimate foundation of British rule — was no longer certain. The naval mutiny at Bombay in February 1946 confirmed it. That, more than any argument, convinced the British that India could not be held.

Official Answer

Q19. (i) He founded the Forward Bloc in 1939 to unite the left of the Congress behind an uncompromising demand for complete independence; and he organised the armed struggle from abroad, commanding the Indian National Army from 1943 and proclaiming the Provisional Government of Free India at Singapore.

(ii) To consolidate all radical anti-imperialist forces within the Congress for a struggle admitting of no compromise; and to establish a socialist state, with the abolition of zamindari following from it.

(iii) We fought to free India from British rule by force of arms, marching on Delhi from the east; and we served in an army that recognised no caste, no province and no religion among its soldiers, and in which women bore arms in the Rani of Jhansi Regiment — for the India we meant to free was to be one nation.

(iv) The trials at the Red Fort in 1945-46 turned the accused into national heroes: the Congress defended them, and the country struck and demonstrated on their behalf, so the sentences could not be enforced. Their true significance was that they showed the British that the Indian army and police could no longer be relied on to hold India down — a lesson the naval mutiny of February 1946 confirmed, and one that weighed heavily in the decision to leave.
