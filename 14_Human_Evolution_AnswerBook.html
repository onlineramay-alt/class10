In humans, it is a functionless (vestigial) organ, even though its corresponding organ in herbivorous mammals (e.g. sheep) is large and helps digest cellulose — making the human appendix a vestige of a once-functional ancestral structure.

Official Answer

Q42. Lamarck proposed that organs which are used continually and extensively become larger, stronger and more developed over an organism's lifetime, while organs that are not used gradually weaken and degenerate ("use and disuse"). He further proposed that such acquired changes are passed on to the offspring, so that over many generations, the used organ becomes progressively more developed in the species (illustrated by his classic giraffe's-neck example).

Q45. This is false. Bat wings and butterfly wings are ANALOGOUS organs, not homologous — they perform the same function (flight) but have completely different origins and structures: a bat's wing is a modified vertebrate forelimb, while a butterfly's wing is an outgrowth of its exoskeleton (cuticle). Since they do not share a common evolutionary/structural origin, they cannot be called homologous.

Official Answer

Q46. Speciation is the process by which one species gives rise to one or more new, distinct species over time. In geographical (allopatric) speciation, a large, widely-distributed population becomes divided by a physical barrier (river, mountain, ocean) into isolated sub-populations, which accumulate different variations and eventually become reproductively isolated, new species. In sympatric speciation, new species arise within the same geographical area, without physical separation, through other forms of reproductive isolation (e.g. differences in breeding behaviour or timing).

D. Descriptive Type

Q47. (a) Evolution — a gradual and continuous process by which simple ancestral life forms have given rise, over successive generations, to more complex and varied organisms.
(b) Vestigial organs — anatomical structures that were fully functional in ancestral organisms but have become reduced, non-functional or nearly functionless in their present-day descendants (e.g. the human vermiform appendix).
(c) Speciation — the process by which a new species arises from an existing one through gradual accumulation of genetic and morphological changes.
(d) Bipedalism — the mode of locomotion in which an organism walks upright on its two hind limbs, freeing the forelimbs (hands) for other uses such as tool-making.
(e) Natural selection — the process by which individuals with variations better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more successfully, passing those favourable traits on to the next generation.

Official Answer

Q48. (a) Chin — Australopithecus had no well-developed chin (a receding, chinless jaw); Cro-Magnon had a well-developed, prominent chin, much like modern humans.
(b) Body hair — Australopithecus had a body substantially covered with thick hair; modern man has greatly reduced, sparse body hair.

Official Answer

Q49. Lamarck's Theory (Inheritance of Acquired Characters): proposed by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck; based on the principle of "use and disuse" of organs; holds that characteristics acquired by an organism during its own lifetime (through use/disuse) are passed directly to its offspring; illustrated by the giraffe stretching its neck to reach higher leaves, and this longer neck being inherited by its young; this mechanism has since been scientifically discredited (acquired characters are not encoded in the germ cells/DNA and so cannot be inherited).
Darwin's Theory (Natural Selection): proposed by Charles Darwin; based on overproduction of offspring, struggle for existence, variation among individuals, and survival of the fittest; holds that naturally-occurring heritable variations already present in a population determine which individuals survive and reproduce, gradually shifting the population's characteristics over many generations; illustrated by industrial melanism in the peppered moth; this mechanism remains the scientifically accepted basis of evolution today.
