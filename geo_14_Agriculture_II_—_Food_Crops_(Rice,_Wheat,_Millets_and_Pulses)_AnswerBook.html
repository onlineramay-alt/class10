B. Short Answer Type

Q5. CLIMATE. Rice is a tropical crop of hot and wet conditions. It needs a temperature of 24 °C or above throughout the growing season, and it must not fall below about 20 °C. It needs heavy rainfall — above 150 cm, and ideally 200 cm — well distributed through the growing period; where the rainfall is less than 100 cm it can only be grown under irrigation, as it is in Punjab and Haryana. It requires bright sunshine and dry weather at harvest.

SOIL. Deep, heavy, fertile CLAYEY or LOAMY soil that retains water, since the field must be kept flooded. Alluvial soil is ideal, which is why the deltas and the river valleys are the great rice regions; the soil must be level and bunded to hold the water.

PRODUCING STATES. West Bengal — the largest producer; Uttar Pradesh; Punjab; also Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Odisha, Bihar, Tamil Nadu, Assam and Chhattisgarh.

METHODS. Broadcasting, drilling, transplantation and the Japanese method, the last two giving the highest yields.

Q6. CLIMATE. Wheat is a crop of the temperate and sub-tropical winter. It needs a COOL, MOIST growing season — about 10 to 15 °C — and a WARM, DRY, BRIGHT period for ripening and harvesting, about 20 to 25 °C. It requires only moderate rainfall, 50 to 75 cm, and too much rain or cloud at ripening spoils the grain. Frost at flowering and hail at harvest are both damaging.

SOIL. Well-drained fertile LOAM or CLAYEY LOAM; the alluvial soil of the northern plains is ideal, and the black soil of the Deccan also grows it.

WHY PUNJAB LEADS. Its winter climate is exactly right — cool moist growing months and a dry warm ripening season. Its soil is deep, level and fertile alluvium. Above all it has ASSURED IRRIGATION from the canals of the Bhakra Nangal system and from tube wells, so the crop does not depend on rainfall at all. And it was the centre of the GREEN REVOLUTION: high-yielding dwarf varieties, chemical fertilizer, pesticides, tractors and mechanised harvesting were adopted there first and most completely, supported by rural credit, minimum support prices and an efficient procurement system. Uttar Pradesh grows a larger total tonnage, but Punjab's yield per hectare is the highest in India.

Q7. IMPORTANCE. Millets grow where nothing else will. They are drought-resistant, need little water and a short growing season, and thrive on poor, sandy, shallow or infertile soil — so they can be cultivated in the semi-arid regions of the Deccan and the north-west without irrigation. They are therefore the food security of the driest parts of India and of its poorest farmers. Nutritionally they are rich in protein, minerals and fibre, and they serve as fodder for cattle as well as food for people; because they are hardy they are also being promoted now as a crop that will withstand a changing climate.

THE THREE MILLETS.
JOWAR (sorghum) — the most widely grown; a crop of the drier parts of the Deccan. Maharashtra above all, and Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh.
BAJRA (pearl millet) — the most drought-resistant of all, grown on sandy and shallow soils. Rajasthan is the chief producer, with Uttar Pradesh, Haryana, Gujarat and Maharashtra.
RAGI (finger millet) — grown in the drier uplands of the south, chiefly Karnataka, and also Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and the hill regions; it is very rich in calcium and iron.

Q8. IMPORTANCE. Pulses — gram, arhar (tur), moong, urad, masoor and peas — are the chief source of PROTEIN in a country where much of the population is vegetarian, so they are essential to nutrition. They are also LEGUMES, and their root nodules fix atmospheric nitrogen in the soil, so growing them restores fertility and they are valuable in rotation with cereals. They need little water and can be grown on comparatively poor soil.

WHY PRODUCTION LAGS. Pulses have been pushed onto marginal, unirrigated land because the Green Revolution concentrated its high-yielding varieties, fertilizer, irrigation and price support on wheat and rice, which were more profitable and more assured. Yields per hectare have consequently remained low and stagnant. The crop is besides vulnerable to pests and to erratic rain, and there has been comparatively little research investment in it. India therefore remains the world's largest producer of pulses and at the same time its largest importer.

CHIEF PRODUCING STATES: Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh and Karnataka.

Q10. (a) Rice is grown in fields that are flooded with 10–25 cm of standing water for much of its life. Only LEVEL land divided into small bunded plots can hold an even sheet of water; on a slope the water would run off and pond unevenly. (Hence terracing on hill slopes.)

(b) Millets need very little water and stand heat and poor soil, so they are grown WITHOUT IRRIGATION in the semi-arid areas of Rajasthan, Gujarat, the Deccan and Karnataka that get only 40–75 cm of rain — land where rice and wheat would fail.

(c) Pulses are the chief source of PROTEIN in the largely vegetarian Indian diet. They are also LEGUMES: their root nodules fix nitrogen and enrich the soil, so they are grown in ROTATION with cereals; and they are hardy, drought-resistant and can be grown on poor land as a second crop.
