Chemistry — Study of Compounds D — Sulphuric Acid

Sulphuric acid is examined under three heads that must be kept apart: as an ACID when dilute, and as a DEHYDRATING agent and an OXIDISING agent when concentrated. Nearly every question turns on which of the three is being asked about.

Q2. (c) Both are true and (R) explains (A). Ionisation requires water. Concentrated sulphuric acid contains very little water and exists largely as un-ionised molecules, so it conducts poorly; on dilution it ionises almost completely into H⁺ and SO₄²⁻, the concentration of mobile ions rises sharply, and it becomes a strong electrolyte.

Q3. (c) Oxidising agent. The concentrated acid gives up oxygen to the sulphur, oxidising it (to SO₂), and is itself reduced (also to SO₂). It is not acting as an acid here at all — no salt is formed.

Official Answer

Q4. (a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A. The reaction SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄ is violently exothermic and throws up a persistent acid MIST that is hard to condense and is largely lost. So the SO₃ is absorbed in concentrated sulphuric acid to give oleum, which is then diluted safely with water.

Q5. (c) Concentrated sulphuric acid. It is a powerful DEHYDRATING agent: it removes the elements of water (H and O in the ratio 2 : 1) from sucrose, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + conc. H₂SO₄ → 12C + 11H₂O, leaving a spongy black mass of carbon.

Official Answer

Q6. (c) dehydration. The concentrated acid removes water from the glucose molecule, leaving only carbon. The acid is not itself consumed and no oxygen is added to the sugar, so it is dehydration, not oxidation or combustion.

Official Answer

Q7. (c) concentrated HCl. Concentrated sulphuric acid and concentrated nitric acid are both oxidising agents (they oxidise copper, carbon and sulphur). Hydrochloric acid has no oxidising power of its own — it can only act as an acid, or, when acted on by a stronger oxidiser like MnO₂, be oxidised itself to chlorine.

B. Identify / Name / Very Short Answer

Q8. The CONTACT PROCESS.

Sulphur trioxide is NOT dissolved directly in water because the reaction SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄ is violently exothermic and produces a dense, persistent MIST of sulphuric acid droplets, which is difficult to condense and is largely lost.

Instead the SO₃ is absorbed in CONCENTRATED SULPHURIC ACID to give OLEUM (fuming sulphuric acid, H₂S₂O₇):
SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂S₂O₇
The oleum is then diluted with the calculated quantity of water to give sulphuric acid of any required strength:
H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄

C. Short Answer Type

Q12. (a) AS A DEHYDRATING AGENT it removes the elements of water — hydrogen and oxygen in the ratio 2 : 1 — from a compound that does not contain water as such.

With sugar it produces a spectacular black mass of carbon:
C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + conc. H₂SO₄ → 12C + 11H₂O
The acid takes all eleven molecules of water out of the sucrose, leaving only carbon. (It dehydrates oxalic acid to carbon monoxide and dioxide, and formic acid to carbon monoxide, in the same way.)

(b) AS AN OXIDISING AGENT it supplies oxygen, being itself reduced to sulphur dioxide:
H₂SO₄ → H₂O + SO₂ + [O]

With copper:
Cu + 2H₂SO₄ (conc.) → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O
With carbon:
C + 2H₂SO₄ (conc.) → CO₂ + 2SO₂ + 2H₂O

KEEP THE TWO APART. Dehydration REMOVES water from the substance; oxidation ADDS oxygen to it. The sugar reaction is dehydration, the copper reaction is oxidation, and confusing them is the commonest error in this chapter.

Q13. (a) SUGAR — the white sugar turns yellow, then brown, and finally swells up into a spongy BLACK MASS OF CARBON, with a hissing sound and a great deal of heat and steam. The acid has dehydrated the sucrose, removing all eleven molecules of water.

(b) BLUE COPPER SULPHATE CRYSTALS — the blue crystals turn WHITE. The acid removes the five molecules of water of crystallisation, converting CuSO₄·5H₂O to anhydrous CuSO₄, which is white.

(c) HYDRATED FERROUS SULPHATE — the green crystals turn WHITE, as the seven molecules of water of crystallisation are removed from FeSO₄·7H₂O.

All three are examples of DEHYDRATION, and (b) and (c) demonstrate specifically the removal of water of crystallisation.

D. Chemical Equations

Q16. (a) C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + conc. H₂SO₄ → 12C + 11H₂O
(dehydration — the acid acts as a dehydrating agent and is not itself consumed in the equation as written)

(b) Cu + 2H₂SO₄ (conc.) → CuSO₄ + SO₂↑ + 2H₂O
(oxidation — note that NO hydrogen is evolved, because the concentrated acid acts as an oxidising agent, and copper is below hydrogen in any case)

(c) Zn + H₂SO₄ (dilute) → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑
(ordinary acid behaviour — zinc is above hydrogen in the activity series)

(d) Na₂CO₃ + H₂SO₄ (dilute) → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O + CO₂↑
(ordinary acid behaviour with a carbonate)

Note how (b) and (c) differ: the SAME acid gives hydrogen when dilute and sulphur dioxide when concentrated, because in the first case it acts as an acid and in the second as an oxidising agent. Examiners test that distinction repeatedly.
