Official Answer

Q18. (i) Constitutional agitation versus direct action. The Early Nationalists confined themselves to petitions, memorials, resolutions and deputations, always within the law; the Radicals adopted Swadeshi, boycott, passive resistance and mass agitation, and were prepared to defy the government and suffer imprisonment.

(ii) Appeal to the rulers versus appeal to the people. The Moderates addressed the Government of India, the British Parliament and the British public, believing that a fair statement of facts would bring redress; the Radicals addressed the Indian people, using festivals, the vernacular press and public meetings to reach the masses whom the Congress had never touched.

(iii) Loyalty versus self-reliance. The Moderates professed loyalty to the Crown and sought reform within the Empire, trusting British good faith; the Radicals had no such faith, held that freedom must be won by Indians themselves through sacrifice, and set Swaraj as the goal.

Official Answer

Q19. (i) He gave the movement its goal and its slogan. 'Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it' turned self-rule from a distant hope into a demand claimed as a right, and he preached Swadeshi, Boycott, National Education and passive resistance as the means to it. For his writings he was tried for sedition and sentenced in 1908 to six years' transportation to Mandalay, where he wrote the Gita Rahasya; his willingness to suffer made him 'Lokmanya', revered by the people.

(ii) He took nationalism to the masses. Through his newspapers Kesari in Marathi and Mahratta in English, and by turning the Ganapati and Shivaji festivals into great public gatherings, he reached ordinary people in their own language and idiom, and made political feeling popular rather than the property of an English-educated few. In 1916 he founded the Home Rule League to organise that feeling into a nationwide demand.

Official Answer

Q20. The official reason given was administrative — that Bengal, with some 78 million people, was too large a province to govern efficiently. Indians did not believe it. The dividing line was drawn so as to make the new province of Eastern Bengal and Assam a Muslim-majority one, while Bengali Hindus became a minority in a western province dominated by Biharis and Oriyas.

The nationalist perspective was that the real purpose was political: to break the unity of the Bengali-speaking people, who were the most politically advanced in India, and to set Hindu against Muslim so that the national movement centred in Bengal would be weakened. It was seen as the plainest possible application of divide and rule. That it had been decided without any regard for Indian opinion, despite protest meetings and petitions, made the point the Radicals had been arguing — that petitioning was useless — and the Partition therefore produced the Swadeshi and Boycott movements and split the Congress within two years. It was annulled in 1911.

Q21. In 1907, at the Surat session of the Congress.

The result: the Congress broke into two. The Moderates retained control of the organisation and the Assertive Nationalists left it, so the Congress lost its most vigorous men and its hold on the younger generation, and became for some years a body of little influence. The government seized the opportunity to crush the Radical leaders — Tilak was transported to Mandalay in 1908 and Lala Lajpat Rai deported — and the national movement as a whole was gravely weakened. The two wings did not come together again until the Lucknow session of 1916.

Official Answer

Q22. (i) The failure of the Moderates. Twenty years of petitions had produced almost nothing, and the Indian Councils Act of 1892 was a plain disappointment. A younger generation concluded that the method itself was at fault.

(ii) The true character of British rule became clearer. The famines and plague of 1896-1900, in which millions died while the government's response was harsh and inadequate, and the steady evidence of the drain of wealth, destroyed the belief that British rule was benevolent.

(iii) Lord Curzon's measures. The Calcutta Corporation Act, the Indian Universities Act, the Official Secrets Act, and above all the Partition of Bengal in 1905, were carried through in open contempt of Indian opinion, and showed that the government would concede nothing to argument.

(iv) Events abroad and a revived pride at home. The defeat of Italy by Abyssinia in 1896, and of Russia by Japan in 1905, exploded the myth of European invincibility; and the work of Indian and Western scholars in recovering India's past gave Indians confidence that they had a civilisation worth governing themselves for.

Official Answer

Q25. (i) The official reason was administrative — that Bengal, with 78 million people, was too large to govern. The nationalists held the real reason to be political: the line was drawn to give the new eastern province a Muslim majority and leave Bengali Hindus a minority in the west, so as to break the unity of the most politically advanced people in India and set the communities against each other. It was divide and rule, and it was carried out in open disregard of Indian protest. It produced the Swadeshi and Boycott movements, and was annulled in 1911.

(ii) At the Surat session of 1907 the Congress divided over the presidency and over the resolutions on Swaraj, Swadeshi, Boycott and National Education. The Moderates kept the organisation; the Assertive Nationalists left it. Its significance is that the movement lost both its unity and its most vigorous leaders at once — the government took the chance to imprison Tilak and deport Lajpat Rai — and the Congress declined into insignificance for some years. The two wings reunited only at Lucknow in 1916.

(iii) Their objective was Swaraj — complete self-rule, claimed as a birthright. Tilak's contributions: he gave the movement that goal and slogan and suffered transportation to Mandalay for his writings; and he took nationalism to the masses through Kesari and Mahratta and the Ganapati and Shivaji festivals, founding the Home Rule League in 1916.

E. Source-Based / Picture-Based Questions

Q26. (i) Swadeshi. Its aim is self-reliance: by producing and buying goods made in one's own country, Indian industry is built up, employment is created at home, and the economic dependence on Britain — which was the root of the drain of wealth — is reduced. It was a positive, constructive programme, not merely a protest.
(ii) Boycott — the refusal to buy or use British goods. During the Swadeshi movement following the Partition of Bengal it was practised by public bonfires of imported cloth, by shopkeepers refusing to stock British goods, and by students and lawyers boycotting government schools and courts. Swadeshi and Boycott were two sides of the same policy: what was refused to Britain was given to India.

Official Answer

Q27. (i) Bal Gangadhar Tilak, known as 'Lokmanya' — revered by the people. He founded Kesari in Marathi and Mahratta in English.
(ii) 'Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it.' It marked the break because the Early Nationalists had asked the British for reforms, as favours to be granted by a just government to loyal subjects. Tilak asserted that self-rule was not a favour at all but a right which belonged to Indians already, and which they would take. Once that was said, petitioning became beside the point, and the movement's methods had to change with its premise.
