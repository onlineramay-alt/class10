C. Short Answer Type

Q13. (i) It rejected the demand for a separate sovereign Pakistan, and proposed instead a Union of India comprising British India and the Princely States, in which the central government would deal only with foreign affairs, defence and communications, all residuary powers resting with the provinces.

(ii) The provinces were to be grouped into three sections — Section A of the Hindu-majority provinces; Section B of the Punjab, Sindh and the North West Frontier Province; and Section C of Bengal and Assam — each group free to settle its own provincial subjects and to have its own executive and legislature.

(iii) A Constituent Assembly was to be elected by the provincial assemblies to frame the constitution, and an Interim Government representing the major parties was to carry on the administration meanwhile. A province might leave its group after the first general election under the new constitution, and the constitution itself could be reconsidered after ten years.

Official Answer

Q14. The Congress accepted the plan in part. It welcomed the rejection of Pakistan and the proposal for a Constituent Assembly, and agreed to join it; but it objected to the grouping of the provinces, holding that grouping must be optional and that a province could not be forced into a section against its will. Nehru's statement in July 1946 that the Congress would enter the Constituent Assembly unbound by any prior commitment made this plain.

The Muslim League accepted the plan in June 1946, because it saw in the compulsory grouping of Sections B and C the framework of a future Pakistan. When Nehru made his statement, the League concluded that the Congress would use its majority to undo the grouping altogether. It thereupon withdrew its acceptance, abandoned constitutional methods, and called for Direct Action Day on 16 August 1946 — which began the communal killings that ended in partition.

Q15. (i) British India was to be divided into two independent dominions, India and Pakistan, and power transferred on 15 August 1947.

(ii) The provinces of Punjab and Bengal were to be partitioned if the members of their legislative assemblies from the Hindu-majority and Muslim-majority districts, voting separately, decided in favour; a Boundary Commission under Sir Cyril Radcliffe was to demarcate the frontiers.

(iii) A referendum was to be held in the North West Frontier Province and in the Sylhet district of Assam to decide which dominion they would join, and the Sindh legislature was to decide for itself.

Official Answer

Q16. (i) From 15 August 1947 two independent dominions, to be known as India and Pakistan, were set up, and the government of British India came to an end.

(ii) The Constituent Assembly of each dominion was given full authority to frame a constitution for it and, until that was done, to act as its legislature with power to repeal any Act of the British Parliament, including this one. Until the new constitutions came into force each dominion was to be governed as nearly as possible under the Government of India Act, 1935.

(iii) The suzerainty of the Crown over the Princely States lapsed, together with all treaties made with them, and each state became free to join either dominion or to remain independent. The office of Secretary of State for India was abolished.

(iv) The King ceased to use the title 'Emperor of India'; each dominion was to have a Governor-General appointed by the King on the advice of its own ministers, acting as its constitutional head; and either dominion might leave the Commonwealth if it chose.

Official Answer

Q17. (i) Because any further delay in the transfer of power could lead to civil war. Communal rioting had been spreading since Direct Action Day in August 1946 — Calcutta, Noakhali, Bihar, the Punjab — and the leaders judged that a country already killing itself could not wait.

(ii) Because the alternative offered was worse. The only way to keep India united against the League's opposition was to concede a constitution with a weak Centre, in which the Muslim-majority groups could paralyse the Union; the Congress preferred a smaller India with a strong Centre to a larger one that could not govern itself. Partition also swept away separate electorates and the other communal safeguards, leaving the new Constitution free to be built on common citizenship.

Official Answer

Q18. (i) The communal violence. From the Great Calcutta Killings of August 1946 onward the country was in the grip of massacres it could not stop, and partition seemed the only means of ending them.

(ii) The fear of civil war. Any further delay in the transfer of power, the leaders judged, would mean not disorder but open war between the communities, and possibly the disintegration of the country into many pieces rather than two.

(iii) The alternative was an unworkable state. To hold India together against the League's will meant accepting a Union with a weak Centre in which the League could block everything; the League's conduct in the Interim Government had already shown that it would. A strong, effective, secular India of smaller extent was preferred to a larger one that could not act.

(iv) The prolonging of British rule was itself a calamity. Every additional year of it meant more bitterness and more violence; and partition, whatever else it cost, would rid the future Constitution of separate electorates and communal weightage, so that free India could be built on the principle of one common citizenship.
