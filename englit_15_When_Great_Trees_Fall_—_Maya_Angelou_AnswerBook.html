B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q3. (i) Rocks on distant hills shudder; lions hunker down in tall grasses; and even elephants lumber after safety. Angelou chooses a mineral, a predator and the largest land animal — so that the disturbance is shown to reach everything, from the inanimate to the most powerful living creature.

(ii) The comparison is apt because a great tree, like a great person, has grown slowly over a long time, has given shelter and shade to many who depended on it without thinking about it, and leaves a visible gap when it goes — the space it occupied is suddenly empty and the light falls differently. It is also apt because the fall of a large tree disturbs a wide area around it, as the death of a great soul disturbs everyone within its reach.

The mood of the extract is one of awe and unease. Nothing is grieving yet; everything is simply shaken. The verbs — shudder, hunker, lumber — convey instinctive alarm rather than sorrow.

(iii) The air is described as light, rare and sterile; and it 'becomes light, rare, sterile' so that we breathe with difficulty. 'We breathe, briefly' expresses the sensation of shock — the shallow, held, insufficient breathing of grief, when one cannot draw a full breath — and behind it the suggestion that our own lives are brief and that we have been reminded of it.

(iv) We remember, and regret, the words the great soul spoke that we did not fully attend to, and the promised walks that were never taken — that is, the things said and the things left undone, the ordinary intimacies we assumed there would be more time for. Our souls, the poem says, dependent upon their nurture, now shrink, wizened; our minds are formed and informed by their radiance and fall away.

(v) The closing stanza is where the poem turns from loss to inheritance. After a period in which peace 'blooms slowly and always irregularly', spaces fill with a kind of soothing electric vibration, and our senses — restored — 'whisper to us. They existed. They existed. We can be. Be and be better. For they existed.'

That ending justifies the statement completely. The great soul's influence does not end with the death; it survives as a proof of possibility. The repetition of 'They existed' is the argument in its simplest form — the fact that such a person was once alive establishes that such a life can be lived, and therefore obliges and enables those left behind to attempt it. 'We can be. Be and be better' converts grief into permission.

The structure of the whole poem supports this reading: it moves from shudder and flight, through shrinking and sterile air, to a slow return of breath and finally to affirmation — so the lasting influence is not asserted but arrived at.

C. Short Answer Type

Q4. The poem moves in three stages, and the movement is its meaning.

It begins with disturbance in the natural world: rocks shudder, lions hunker down, elephants lumber towards safety. Nobody is mourning yet — everything is simply shaken by the fall.

It then moves inward to human grief, and this is the bleakest section. The air turns light, rare and sterile; we breathe briefly; our eyes see with a hurtful clarity; memory becomes painful, full of gnawing, unaccustomed sounds and unspoken words; our souls, which depended on the great soul's nurture, shrink and wizen; our minds fall away.

And it ends with recovery, deliberately slow. Peace blooms — but 'slowly and always irregularly', which refuses any easy consolation. Spaces fill with a soothing vibration. The senses return, and speak: 'They existed. They existed.' The last movement converts the loss into an inheritance — we can be, and be better, because such a person once was.

The shape is therefore from the physical to the emotional to the moral, and from shock through desolation to affirmation, without skipping the desolation.

Q5. Angelou writes in short, irregular unrhymed lines, and the choice does three things.

It enacts the subject. Grief does not proceed in regular measures, and a poem about being knocked off balance would be false in neat quatrains. The lines break unpredictably, sometimes after a single word, so the reader's breath is repeatedly interrupted — which matters particularly in a poem whose central image is difficulty in breathing.

It allows emphasis by isolation. Because there is no metrical pattern to fulfil, a phrase set on its own line carries disproportionate weight, and Angelou uses this at the climax: 'They existed' stands almost alone and is repeated.

And it keeps the poem speech-like. Angelou was a performer, and the rhythms here are those of a voice speaking to people who are grieving — plain, unhurried, without ornament. Rhyme would have made it a formal elegy; free verse makes it something closer to consolation offered aloud.

D. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q6. SUBJECT. An elegy on the death of great souls, built on a single extended metaphor: the fall of a great tree and the disturbance it causes to everything around it.

STRUCTURE. Three movements — the physical shock felt through the natural world; the interior desolation of those left behind; and the slow return of peace, ending in affirmation. The proportions matter: the desolation is given the most space, and the consolation is withheld until the very end and even then qualified ('slowly and always irregularly').

FORM. Free verse in short irregular lines, unrhymed. The broken lineation interrupts the reader's breathing, which is deliberate in a poem whose central physical image is the inability to draw breath.

IMAGERY. Two registers. The natural — trees, rocks, lions, elephants, tall grasses — establishes scale and instinctive alarm without any human sentiment. The bodily — sterile air, brief breathing, hurtful clarity of sight, shrinking and wizened souls — brings the loss inside the reader. The transition between the two is the poem's main technical achievement.

DICTION. Plain and largely monosyllabic, with almost no ornament. The one moment of heightened language is the closing repetition, and it earns its effect because everything before it has been so restrained.

THEME. That greatness is not extinguished by death. The dead are not said to live on in memory in any comfortable sense; the claim is harder and better — that their having existed proves what a human life can contain, and therefore imposes an obligation on the living. 'We can be. Be and be better. For they existed.'

CRITICAL COMMENT. The poem's strength is its refusal to console too quickly: three-quarters of it is loss, and the affirmation arrives only after the reader has been given no reason to expect it. Its risk is abstraction — 'great souls' is never particularised, and the reader must supply the person. Angelou evidently intends that: the blank is where each reader's own dead go.
