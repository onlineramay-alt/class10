Q4. (i) M. Hamel, the schoolmaster, speaking from his chair to his class in the village school in Alsace.

(ii) The bulletin-board at the town-hall carried the order from Berlin that only German would be taught in the schools of Alsace and Lorraine. For the last two years all the bad news had come from that board — lost battles, the draft, the orders of the commanding officer — and Franz, passing it that morning and seeing a crowd in front of it, had wondered idly what the matter was and gone on without stopping.

(iii) Any two of: the village was unusually quiet, without the bustle and the sound of lessons repeated in unison that could normally be heard in the street; there was no scolding for his lateness, and M. Hamel spoke to him kindly and told him to go quickly to his place; M. Hamel wore the green coat, frilled shirt and embroidered black silk cap that he kept for inspection and prize days; and on the back benches, which were always empty, sat the village people — old Hauser with his three-cornered hat, the former mayor, the former postmaster — sitting silent and sorrowful.

(iv) Because it takes away, at a stroke, something he had assumed would always be there. He had thought of French as a chore to be put off; he now learns that there will be no more of it, that he will never learn to write properly, and that his own idleness has become permanent and irreparable. The suddenness is the point — nothing has changed in the room, and everything has.

(v) They reverse completely. A moment before, his books had been 'a nuisance and a bother' and he had thought of leaving them behind to run off after birds' eggs and go sliding on the Saar; now his grammar and his history of the saints are 'old friends' that he could not give up. He had been afraid of M. Hamel and his terrible iron ruler; now he forgets the ruler and how cranky the master was, and understands why he is dressed in his Sunday best and why the old men of the village have come. The story turns on this exchange of an obligation for a loss — Franz values what he is about to lose only because he is losing it, which is Daudet's judgement on his whole country.

Q5. (i) That language is a people's last possession and their means of survival. A conquered people may lose their government, their territory and their freedom, but so long as they keep their own tongue they keep their identity and their hope of recovering the rest — they hold 'the key to their prison'. It is the most quoted sentence in the story and the centre of its argument.

(ii) He distributes the blame widely and takes his own share. The parents, who were not anxious to have their children learn, and who preferred to send them to work on a farm or at the mills for a little more money. The children, who put off learning till tomorrow. And himself: he had often sent them to water his flowers instead of learning, and when he wanted to go fishing he had given them a holiday. He does not exempt himself, which is what makes the self-reproach bearable rather than sanctimonious.

(iii) M. Hamel had prepared new copies for them, written in a beautiful round hand — 'France, Alsace, France, Alsace' — and they hung from the desk-rods like little flags floating everywhere in the schoolroom. Everyone worked in complete silence; the only sounds were the scratching of pens on paper and the cooing of pigeons on the roof, at which Franz wondered whether they too would be made to sing in German. Even the little children traced their fish-hooks with all their heart, and old Hauser, at the back, held his spelling-book in both hands and spelled the letters with the children, crying so that his voice trembled.

(iv) The church clock strikes twelve and the Prussian trumpets sound under the windows, returning from drill. M. Hamel stands up, very pale; he tries to speak — 'My friends,' he says, 'I — I —' — and something chokes him so that he cannot go on. He turns to the blackboard, takes a piece of chalk and writes with all his might, as large as he can, 'Vive La France!' Then he stops, leans his head against the wall, and without a word makes a gesture with his hand: 'School is dismissed — you may go.'

(v) It is about language in the obvious sense: the story exists to say what M. Hamel says in the extract, that a people's tongue is the key to their prison, and it was written for a France that had just lost Alsace and Lorraine to Germany.

But the emotional force comes from regret rather than from patriotism. Almost everything in the story is a thing valued too late. Franz discovers his books are old friends on the morning he must give them up. The old men of the village come to school for the first time in forty years, on the last day there is a school to come to. M. Hamel realises what he owed his pupils only as he loses them, and blames himself along with the rest. Even the pigeons on the roof become precious when Franz wonders whether they too must sing in German.

Daudet's method is to make the loss ordinary and irreversible at the same time. Nothing dramatic happens; a teacher gives a lesson and dismisses a class. The grief is entirely in the recognition that this ordinary thing will not happen again — and that it was always available, and always neglected.

Their presence carries the story's regret in its plainest form. These are men who had years of opportunity to learn and did not, and who have come to a schoolroom on the one morning when it is too late.

Q7. He appears first as the schoolmaster Franz fears — cranky, carrying a great iron ruler under his arm, given to rapping the desk and calling for silence. That is the man of the opening paragraphs, seen entirely through the eyes of a boy who has not done his homework.

What the last lesson reveals is quite different. He is a man of deep and undemonstrative patriotism, who has served the village for forty years and marks his last day by putting on the clothes he keeps for prize-givings. He is generous: he does not scold Franz for being late, and when the boy stumbles hopelessly over the rule for participles he does not punish him but says that Franz is not the greatest trouble, and turns the failure into an indictment of the whole of Alsace, himself included.

His honesty about his own share of the blame — the flowers he made them water, the fishing holidays he gave himself — is the most attractive thing in him, and it is what saves his long speech about the French language from being a lecture.

His last minutes are wordless. He tries to speak, cannot, writes 'Vive La France!' on the blackboard with all his might, and dismisses the class with a gesture, his head against the wall. Daudet gives him no farewell speech, and the restraint is what makes the ending unforgettable.

Q8. He begins as an idle, ordinary boy: late for school, having learnt nothing of participles, tempted by the warm day, the birds and the Prussian soldiers drilling in the open field, and thinking of his books as a nuisance and a bother.

The announcement reverses everything at once. His books become old friends; his fear of M. Hamel turns into pity and understanding; and when he is called on and cannot get past the first words of the rule, his shame is no longer about punishment but about waste — 'my last French lesson!'

By the end he is capable of an attention he has never given before. He listens to the grammar lesson and finds it easy, and thinks he has never listened so carefully nor M. Hamel explained so patiently. He notices everything in the room, understands why the old men have come, and is moved almost beyond bearing by the sight of Hauser spelling with the children.

The change is not a reform — there will be no more lessons for him to be good in. It is an awakening that arrives exactly one lesson too late, which is the story's point.

D. Long Answer / Essay Type

Q9. The title works on three levels, and the story activates all of them.

Literally it is the last French lesson to be given in that school: the order has come from Berlin, the new master arrives tomorrow, and after twelve o'clock no French will be taught in Alsace or Lorraine.

It is also M. Hamel's last lesson after forty years in the same place — his farewell to a life's work, which is why he wears the clothes he keeps for inspection days and why the village comes to sit at the back.

Most importantly, it is the lesson the village finally learns, and the lesson is about itself. What M. Hamel teaches that morning is not grammar but the meaning of what they are losing: that French is the clearest and most logical of languages, that a conquered people who keep their tongue keep the key to their prison, and that Alsace's tragedy is partly its own doing — parents who preferred the mills to the schoolroom, children who put off learning till tomorrow, and a master who gave himself fishing holidays.

The irony in the title is complete. It is the last lesson, and it is the first one anybody has properly attended: Franz has never listened so carefully, and old Hauser, spelling the letters with the little children, is learning at last. A lesson which can only be understood when there will be no more of them is the sharpest form Daudet could have found for his subject, which is regret.

E. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q10. Telling the story through Franz gives Daudet two things he could not otherwise have had.

The first is discovery. Because Franz does not know what has happened, the reader learns it as he does, through a sequence of small wrongnesses — the quiet village, the absence of scolding, the Sunday clothes, the old men on the back benches — before M. Hamel's announcement explains them. A narrator who understood the situation would have had to state it; a child can only report, and the reader assembles the meaning ahead of him.

The second is emotional honesty. A patriotic adult narrator would risk sounding like propaganda, particularly in a story written so soon after France's defeat. A boy who was going to skip school to hunt birds' eggs, who dreads a grammar question, and who wonders whether the pigeons will be made to coo in German, cannot be accused of speechmaking. The politics reaches us through a child's small concerns, and is the stronger for it.

The limitation is deliberate too. Franz cannot tell us what M. Hamel felt, or what became of Alsace. The story ends where his knowledge ends — with a man's back, a piece of chalk and a gesture — and refuses the summary an adult narrator would have supplied.
