B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q3. (i) His singing could be heard in the streets and open spaces of the town — he sings out of doors, in the marketplace and the roads, where nobody can escape him — and in the hot season, in the blazing summer sun, which adds to the general suffering.

(ii) It causes physical distress and panic. People cry out that they are dying, hold their heads and their ears, and run from the place; the whole neighbourhood empties. What they plead for is simply that he should stop singing — they do not ask him to sing better, only to stop.

(iii) The animals suffer as much as the people, and the poem catalogues them for comic effect. Horses bolt or collapse; fish leap out of the water — some accounts say they die or are stunned; birds fall out of the sky or fly away in terror. The joke lies in the completeness of the destruction: no creature in any element is safe.

(iv) A buffalo. It charges at Bhishma Lochan and butts him or knocks him over, so that his singing is stopped at last and silence returns. The poem's comic point is that a large, stupid, irritated animal accomplishes what all the pleading of the human population could not.

(v) An example of exaggeration: that fish leap out of the water, or that birds drop from the sky, at the sound of a man's voice — physically impossible, and offered quite seriously by the poem.

Exaggeration makes the poem appealing because it converts a small everyday annoyance — a neighbour who sings badly — into a public catastrophe, and the gap between the trivial cause and the enormous effect is the source of all the humour. It also allows the poem to accumulate: each stanza can add a fresh absurdity, and the reader's pleasure comes from wondering how far it will go. And because nothing in it is meant to be believed, no one is actually hurt, so the comedy stays good-natured.

An alternative title. 'The Singer of Sharma Street' would be defensible for its mock-heroic ring; but the best choice is probably something like 'A Public Menace' or 'The Silence at Last', because either would keep the poem's central irony — that this is a poem about music in which music is the disaster and silence the happy ending. Whatever title is offered, the justification must be argued from the poem, which is what carries the marks.

C. Short Answer Type

Q4. The phrase normally praises music's ability to move, console and delight — to soothe the savage breast, in the old formula. Ray uses it to mean the exact opposite.

Bhishma Lochan's music does have enormous power: it empties streets, scatters crowds, panics horses, drives fish from the water and birds from the air. Everything the phrase traditionally promises is present except the pleasure — the effect is total, and it is entirely destructive.

The irony is completed by the ending. If music has power, so does its cessation: the happiest event in the poem is a silence, and it is brought about by a buffalo. Ray has taken a solemn commonplace and turned it inside out, which is the characteristic move of the nonsense tradition he was writing in.

Q5. By hyperbole, chiefly. Every effect is impossibly out of proportion to its cause: one man singing badly produces a general evacuation, animals in flight, fish leaving the water and birds falling from the sky. Nothing is understated anywhere in the poem.

By accumulation. The disasters are listed one after another in a rising sequence, so that the reader's amusement comes partly from anticipating what will be ruined next.

By mock-solemnity. The verse form is bouncy and regular, the tone is that of a serious report, and the singer is given a grand full name — Bhishma Lochan Sharma — that sounds far too dignified for the calamity he causes.

By bathos in the ending. After the cosmic scale of the destruction, deliverance arrives in the form of a buffalo. The smallness and stupidity of the solution, set against the enormousness of the problem, is the poem's best joke.

And by the translator's rhymes, which are deliberately forced and comic — part of the pleasure of the English version is hearing the rhyme scheme strain to keep up.

D. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q6. SUBJECT. A man named Bhishma Lochan Sharma sings in public. His singing is so dreadful that people flee, animals panic, fish leave the water and birds fall from the sky; a buffalo finally charges him and silence is restored.

TRADITION. The poem belongs to Bengali nonsense verse, of which Sukumar Ray is the master — a tradition comparable to Edward Lear's in English, in which absurdity is pursued with a straight face and a strict metre. It is worth saying so in an examination answer, because it explains why nothing in the poem is meant to be plausible.

FORM. Regular, strongly rhymed, bouncing verse with a driving rhythm. The regularity is essential to the comedy: the more orderly the metre, the funnier the chaos it describes, and the reader is carried along at a pace that leaves no time to object.

TECHNIQUE. Hyperbole throughout, arranged as a rising catalogue; mock-heroic naming; and bathos at the close. The humour is structural rather than verbal — it lies in the proportions of the thing, not in individual jokes.

THEME. There is no moral, and looking for one is a mistake. If the poem has a subject beyond its own absurdity it is the comedy of self-importance: Bhishma Lochan never doubts for a moment that he is making music, and the gap between his conviction and everyone else's suffering is where the poem lives.

CRITICAL COMMENT. The poem's achievement is sustained tone. It commits to its single joke completely, escalates it with real inventiveness, and stops at exactly the right moment — with a buffalo, and silence. What is lost in translation is a good deal of Ray's Bengali wordplay; what survives is the rhythm and the shape, which is why the English version still works.
