Keep three quantities rigidly apart, and most of the chapter's difficulty disappears:

NOMINAL (FACE) VALUE — the value printed on the share. DIVIDEND IS ALWAYS CALCULATED ON THIS.
MARKET VALUE — the price at which the share is bought or sold. INVESTMENT IS ALWAYS CALCULATED ON THIS.
RETURN (YIELD) % = (annual dividend ÷ investment) × 100.

At par: MV = NV. Above par (at a premium): MV > NV. Below par (at a discount): MV < NV.

A. Multiple Choice Questions

Q1. (b) ₹15.

Dividend is on the FACE value:
Dividend per share = 9% of ₹20 = (9/100) × 20 = ₹1.80

Return is on the INVESTMENT, that is on the market value:
12% of MV = 1.80
MV = 1.80 ÷ 0.12 = **₹15**

So she bought each share at ₹15 — below par, at a discount of ₹5.

SANITY CHECK: to earn a return HIGHER than the dividend rate (12% > 9%), she must have paid LESS than the face value. ₹15 < ₹20. ✓ If your answer comes out above ₹20, you have divided the wrong way round.

B. Worked Problems

Q8. GIVEN: Investment = ₹18,000. Nominal value = ₹100. Premium = ₹20, so market value = 100 + 20 = ₹120. Dividend = 15%.

(i) NUMBER OF SHARES = investment ÷ market value
= 18,000 ÷ 120
= **150 shares**

(ii) ANNUAL INCOME — dividend is on the FACE value.
Total face value = 150 × 100 = ₹15,000
Income = 15% of 15,000 = (15/100) × 15,000 = **₹2,250**

(iii) PERCENTAGE RETURN = (income ÷ investment) × 100
= (2,250 ÷ 18,000) × 100
= 0.125 × 100
= **12.5%**

NOTE that the return (12.5%) is LESS than the dividend rate (15%), because the shares were bought at a premium — he paid ₹120 to get the dividend on ₹100. That relation is worth using as a check on every problem of this type.

Q9. GIVEN: NV = ₹50, dividend 12%, discount ₹10, so MV = 50 − 10 = ₹40. Annual income = ₹4,800.

(i) NUMBER OF SHARES — work back from the income, remembering the dividend is on face value.
Dividend on ONE share = 12% of 50 = ₹6
Number of shares = total income ÷ dividend per share = 4,800 ÷ 6 = **800 shares**

(ii) INVESTMENT = number of shares × market value
= 800 × 40
= **₹32,000**

(iii) PERCENTAGE RETURN = (income ÷ investment) × 100
= (4,800 ÷ 32,000) × 100
= 0.15 × 100
= **15%**

CHECK: the shares were bought at a DISCOUNT, so the return (15%) should exceed the dividend rate (12%). ✓

Q10. Let the amount invested in the FIRST kind be ₹x. Then the amount in the second is ₹(24,000 − x).

FIRST INVESTMENT: MV = ₹120, dividend 8% on ₹100 = ₹8 per share.
Number of shares = x/120
Income = (x/120) × 8 = 8x/120 = x/15

SECOND INVESTMENT: MV = ₹80, dividend 6% on ₹100 = ₹6 per share.
Number of shares = (24,000 − x)/80
Income = [(24,000 − x)/80] × 6 = 3(24,000 − x)/40

TOTAL INCOME:
x/15 + 3(24,000 − x)/40 = 1,700

Multiply throughout by 120 (the LCM of 15 and 40):
8x + 9(24,000 − x) = 204,000
8x + 216,000 − 9x = 204,000
−x = −12,000
x = **₹12,000**

So he invested **₹12,000 in the first kind** and **24,000 − 12,000 = ₹12,000 in the second.**

CHECK:
First: 12,000/120 = 100 shares, income = 100 × 8 = ₹800
Second: 12,000/80 = 150 shares, income = 150 × 6 = ₹900
Total = 800 + 900 = ₹1,700 ✓

A USEFUL PRELIMINARY CHECK on any problem of this kind: work out the income if the WHOLE sum went into each kind separately. Here that is 24,000/15 = ₹1,600 and 3(24,000)/40 = ₹1,800. The stated total must lie between those two bounds, and ₹1,700 does. If it does not, the data are inconsistent and you have misread something.
