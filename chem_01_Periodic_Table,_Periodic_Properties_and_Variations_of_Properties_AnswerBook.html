Chemistry — Periodic Table, Periodic Properties and Variations of Properties

The periodic trends are the whole of this chapter, and almost every question reduces to one of them. Learn the four — atomic size, ionisation energy, electron affinity, electronegativity — with the direction of change across a period and down a group, and the reason for each. Metallic character and non-metallic character follow from them.

A. Multiple Choice Questions

Q1. (b) 2. The configuration 2,8,2 has two valence electrons, so the element is in Group 2 and forms a divalent cation M²⁺. Its hydroxide is therefore M(OH)₂, which gives two hydroxyl ions per molecule. (The element is magnesium, atomic number 12.)

Official Answer

Q2. (c) 12. Forming X²⁺ means losing two electrons, so X must have two valence electrons and belong to Group 2. Of the options, only atomic number 12 gives the configuration 2,8,2 — magnesium. Atomic number 16 is 2,8,6 (gains 2 electrons), 10 is 2,8 (a noble gas), and 14 is 2,8,4 (shares).

Official Answer

Q3. (c) R. Across a period from left to right the nuclear charge increases while electrons are added to the same shell, so the electrons are pulled in more tightly and the atomic radius DECREASES. P, with one valence electron, is the leftmost (Group 1); R, with high electronegativity, is furthest right. R therefore has the smallest radius.

Official Answer

Q4. (c) R. An ionic bond requires a metal that loses electrons easily and a non-metal that gains them — that is, a large difference in electronegativity. Group 2 (two valence electrons, readily lost) with Group 17 (seven valence electrons, one needed) gives exactly that, forming MX₂. P is two metals, which cannot transfer to each other; Q is two non-metals, which share; and Group 18 in S is a noble gas and does not normally bond at all.

Official Answer

Q5. (d) its outermost shell is completely filled. Neon has the configuration 2,8 — a complete octet, which is exceptionally stable. Removing an electron from it needs more energy than for any other element in the period. Ionisation potential also rises across a period, and neon is at the end of Period 2.

Official Answer

Q6. (d) Br. Electron affinity is the energy released when an atom gains an electron, and it is greatest for small atoms close to a complete octet — the halogens. Bromine needs just one electron. Magnesium and lithium are metals that lose electrons, and argon already has a complete octet, so its electron affinity is effectively zero.

Official Answer

Q7. (d) Argon. Argon has a complete octet (2,8,8) and no tendency to accept a further electron — one would have to enter a new shell — so its electron affinity is effectively zero. Neon is also a noble gas but belongs to Period 2.

Official Answer

Q8. (c) Lithium. All four are in Period 2, so all have two shells; the radius then decreases from left to right as the nuclear charge increases and pulls the outer electrons in. Lithium is furthest to the left (Group 1), so its radius is the largest.

Official Answer

Q9. (d) Na. All four are in Period 3. Across a period the atomic radius decreases from left to right, because the nuclear charge increases while electrons are added to the same shell. Sodium is the leftmost (Group 1), so it has the largest radius; sulphur, further right, the smallest of the four.

Official Answer

Q10. (a) metal. A low ionisation potential means the outermost electron is removed easily — exactly the property of a metal, which forms positive ions by losing electrons. Non-metals and inert gases hold their electrons firmly and have high ionisation potentials.

Official Answer

Q11. (d) an ionic solid. The configuration 2,2 is a Group 2 metal with two valence electrons it readily loses. With oxygen it forms an ionic oxide MO — a high-melting crystalline solid that conducts only when molten, not when solid. That rules out (a), (b) and (c).

Official Answer

Q12. (c) Both 3 and 4. Group 1 metals lose their single valence electron readily, so they are strong REDUCING agents, not oxidising agents (3 is false), and they form IONIC, not covalent, compounds with non-metals (4 is false). They are genuinely good conductors of heat and electricity, so 1 and 2 are true.

Official Answer

Q13. (b) 2, 8, 1. Electropositive character — the tendency to lose electrons — increases down a group and decreases across a period. (a) and (b) are both Group 1; of these, 2,8,1 (sodium) has one more shell than 2,1 (lithium), so its valence electron is further out and lost more easily. Sodium is the most electropositive of the four.

Official Answer

Q14. (a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A. An alkali metal has one valence electron; losing it gives the very stable noble-gas configuration of the previous period. Removing a SECOND electron would break into that complete octet, which needs an enormous amount of energy, so the dipositive ion does not form. R is exactly why A is true.

Official Answer

Q15. (b) Chlorine. Electronegativity increases across a period and decreases down a group, so it is highest towards the top right (excluding noble gases). Of the four, chlorine is furthest to the right in Period 3 and attracts a shared pair most strongly.

Q17. The element is NITROGEN. Period 2 means two shells; Group 15 means five valence electrons — so the configuration is 2,5, which is atomic number 7, nitrogen.

Needing three electrons to complete its octet, it shares three pairs with hydrogen, forming AMMONIA, NH₃.

Official Answer

Q18. (a) ATOMIC SIZE (atomic radius) — the distance from the centre of the nucleus to the outermost shell of an isolated atom.

(b) IONISATION ENERGY (ionisation potential) — the energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom, so as to form a positively charged ion.

(c) ELECTRON AFFINITY — the energy released when a neutral gaseous atom accepts an electron to form a negatively charged ion.

(d) ELECTRONEGATIVITY — the tendency of an atom to attract towards itself the shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q19. A PERIODIC PROPERTY is a property of the elements that shows a regular gradation — a repeating pattern of increase or decrease — along a period and down a group of the periodic table, because it depends on the electronic configuration, which itself repeats periodically.

The exceptions in Period 2 are BERYLLIUM and NITROGEN. Beryllium (2,2) has a completely filled 2s subshell and nitrogen (2,5) a half-filled 2p subshell; both arrangements are unusually stable, so more energy than the trend predicts is needed to remove an electron from them. Boron therefore has a lower ionisation energy than beryllium, and oxygen a lower one than nitrogen.

C. Short Answer Type

Q25. (a) ACROSS A PERIOD, atomic size DECREASES from left to right. Along a period the number of shells stays the same, but the nuclear charge increases by one proton with each element. The greater positive charge attracts the electrons of the same outermost shell more strongly and pulls them closer to the nucleus, so the radius contracts. Sodium is much larger than chlorine although both have three shells.

(b) DOWN A GROUP, atomic size INCREASES. Each successive element has one more shell than the one above it, so the outermost electrons are further from the nucleus. The nuclear charge also increases, but its effect is more than offset by the addition of a shell and by the SHIELDING of the outer electrons by the inner ones. Lithium is much smaller than caesium.

The rule to carry away: the number of shells decides size down a group, and the nuclear charge decides it across a period.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q26. (a) Because the atomic size decreases and the nuclear charge increases across a period, so the outermost electron is held more tightly and more energy is needed to pull it away.

(b) Metallic character is the tendency to LOSE electrons and form positive ions. Across a period the nuclear charge rises and the size falls, so electrons are held more firmly and are lost less readily; the elements therefore become less metallic and more non-metallic, ending in the halogens, which gain electrons instead.

(c) Because a noble gas already has a complete octet (or duplet, for helium) in its outermost shell. That arrangement is stable, and there is no tendency to accept a further electron — an added electron would have to enter a new shell, which requires energy rather than releasing it. Their electron affinity is therefore effectively zero.

Q27. P would be the most difficult to reduce.

Reduction is the GAIN of electrons. P has a single valence electron which it loses readily, so it exists most stably as the positive ion P⁺; to reduce it would mean forcing an electron back onto a species that has just given one away in order to reach a stable configuration. P is therefore the strongest reducing agent and the hardest to reduce itself.

R, by contrast, is highly electronegative and takes electrons readily, so R is the easiest to reduce.

Official Answer

Q28. (a) X has GREATER oxidising power than Y. A high electron affinity means X accepts electrons readily; an oxidising agent is a substance that gains electrons, so the readier acceptor is the stronger oxidising agent.

(b) X is MORE electronegative than Y. Electron affinity and electronegativity run together — both measure the pull an atom has for electrons.

(c) X is placed to the RIGHT of Y. Both electron affinity and electronegativity increase across a period from left to right.

Official Answer

Q29. (a) Be > C > F. Across Period 2 the size decreases left to right, so beryllium (Group 2) is largest and fluorine (Group 17) smallest.

(b) K < Na < Li. Ionisation potential decreases down Group 1 as shells are added.

(c) P < Si < Al < Mg < Na. Metallic character decreases across a period.

(d) I < Br < Cl < F. Electronegativity increases up Group 17.

(e) He < Ne < Ar < Ca. He has 1 shell, Ne 2, Ar 3, and Ca (2,8,8,2) 4.

Official Answer

Q30. (a) Down a group each successive element has one more shell, so the outermost electron is further from the nucleus and better shielded by the inner shells. It is held less firmly, so less energy is needed to remove it.

(b) A reducing agent gives up electrons. An alkali metal has a single, loosely held valence electron which it loses very readily to reach a noble-gas configuration, so it is a powerful electron donor.

(c) Across a period the nuclear charge increases by one proton per element while electrons enter the same shell, so the atomic radius falls and the outer electron is pulled in more tightly — more energy is then needed to remove it.

D. Structured / Section B Questions

Q31. (i) SODIUM. Across a period the atomic radius decreases from left to right, because the nuclear charge increases while electrons continue to enter the same shell, so they are drawn in more tightly. Sodium is furthest to the left of the elements listed and therefore has the largest radius.

(ii) ARGON. Ionisation energy increases across a period, for the same reason — smaller size and larger nuclear charge mean the outer electron is held more firmly. Argon, at the end of the period, has in addition a complete and stable octet, so removing an electron from it requires more energy than from any other element in the period.

(iii) Al < Mg < Na, in increasing order of metallic character. Metallic character is the tendency to lose electrons; it DECREASES across a period as the nuclear charge grows and the atoms hold their electrons more tightly. Sodium, with one loosely held valence electron, loses it most readily and is the most metallic; aluminium, with three and a higher nuclear charge, is the least.

(iv) The oxides change from BASIC through AMPHOTERIC to ACIDIC. Na₂O and MgO are basic, being metal oxides; Al₂O₃ is amphoteric, reacting with both acids and alkalis; SiO₂ is weakly acidic; and P₄O₁₀, SO₂/SO₃ and Cl₂O₇ are acidic, being non-metal oxides that form acids with water. The change follows directly from the fall in metallic character across the period.
