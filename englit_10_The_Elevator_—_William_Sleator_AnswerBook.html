B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q4. (i) Because it is very small, very old and very slow — a tiny, self-service box barely bigger than a coffin, which creaks and jerks and takes an age between floors. Martin is claustrophobic; being shut in a space that size, alone, with the door closed and no way to get out until it decides to stop, frightens him before any person is involved.

(ii) She is grossly fat — so fat that she fills the small elevator and Martin is pressed against the wall. She wears a shapeless coat, she does not speak, and she stares at him steadily the whole way down, breathing heavily. Nothing she does is threatening in itself, which is exactly what makes it unbearable.

(iii) He refuses to take it seriously. He tells Martin the elevator is perfectly safe, that he is behaving like a baby, and that a boy his age should not be afraid of a lift. He treats the fat woman as an irrelevance — she is only a fat woman — and offers neither help nor belief.

(iv) He takes the stairs. It fails because they live on the seventeenth floor: seventeen flights is exhausting, it makes him late, and it cannot be sustained day after day. The building's design closes the only exit, and he is forced back into the elevator.

(v) Because the fear in the story is Martin's, and an explanation would replace it with a fact. So long as we do not know what the woman is, we are in the same position as the boy: something is wrong, nobody believes him, and he cannot say what the danger is — which is exactly the condition of a child's fear.

An explanation would also make the story arguable. If we were told she was a ghost, or a murderer, we could assess the danger and the story would become a plot. As it stands there is nothing to assess: she is a fat woman in a lift who says hello, and the reader's own imagination supplies the horror.

And the refusal to explain is what makes the ending possible. Sleator stops at the closing door, so the worst thing in the story is the thing that has not happened yet — which is where fear actually lives.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q5. (i) He has again dismissed Martin's fear and told him to use the elevator like everybody else — that there is nothing wrong with it and nothing to be afraid of. Martin goes down alone because his father will not come with him and will not believe him.

(ii) The elevator starts down and then slows and stops at a floor Martin has not pressed. He watches the door open. The fat woman is standing there waiting, and she gets in. The space is so small that she fills it. The door closes.

(iii) 'Hello, Martin.' They are spoken by the fat woman, and they are the last words of the story.

(iv) Because until that moment everything could have been coincidence — a neighbour who happens to take the same lift, a stare that meant nothing, a boy's imagination. The use of his name destroys that possibility at a stroke. She knows him; she has been waiting for him; the encounters were not accidental. In two words the story converts everything the reader has been told to dismiss into evidence, and confirms that the person who was right all along was the child nobody listened to.

(v) It is effective because it stops exactly one moment too early. The door closes and the story ends: we are given the trap and not the outcome, and the reader is left in the box with Martin.

It also delivers a double blow at once. It confirms the supernatural or at least the deliberate — she knows his name — and it confirms the ordinary human failure that made it possible, because Martin is alone in that elevator only because his father would not believe him. The real horror of the story is not the woman; it is a child telling the truth and not being heard.

And there is nothing gruesome anywhere in it. Sleator produces genuine dread out of a small lift, a fat stranger and a greeting — which is a considerable technical achievement, and worth saying so in an examination answer.

C. Short Answer Type

Q6. By repetition with variation. Martin meets the woman once, and it is nothing. He meets her again, and it is uncomfortable. Each encounter is slightly closer, slightly longer, slightly more deliberate, until the pattern itself becomes the threat — the reader begins to dread the next ride before anything has happened.

By closing the exits one at a time. He tries the stairs; seventeen floors defeat him. He tells his father; his father does not believe him. Every escape route is shown and then removed, so that the final ride is inevitable.

By withholding. We never learn who the woman is, what she wants, or whether she is even supernatural. There is nothing to reason about, so the fear cannot be argued away.

And by the setting itself. A small, slow, old elevator is a perfect instrument of suspense: it is confined, it cannot be left once it starts, and the time between floors is dead time in which nothing can be done. Sleator chose a box that does the work for him.

Q7. It is the story's real subject, and its failure is what makes the horror possible.

Martin is twelve, small for his age, and afraid. His father is impatient with fear and equates it with weakness: he tells Martin the elevator is safe, that he is acting like a baby, that a big boy should not behave so. When Martin tries to describe the woman, the father hears only a boy making excuses.

There is no cruelty in it. The father is not a villain; he is a busy adult applying a rough common sense, and everything he says would be reasonable if Martin were imagining things. That is precisely the trap — his dismissal is entirely plausible, and entirely wrong.

The cost is that Martin is alone. He rides down by himself at the end because there is nobody who will come with him, and the last words of the story are addressed to a boy whose father told him there was nothing there. Sleator's quiet argument is that a child's fear may be information, and that refusing to hear it can be a kind of abandonment.

D. Long Answer / Essay Type

Q8. Almost everything that would normally constitute a horror story is missing from this one. There is no violence, no blood, no monster described, no explanation, and no ending.

What is left out first is the woman's nature. We are never told whether she is a ghost, a predator, something supernatural, or simply a fat neighbour with an unnerving stare — and because we cannot classify her, we cannot decide how frightened to be. A named danger can be measured; an unnamed one expands to fill whatever space the reader gives it.

What is left out second is corroboration. Nobody else sees anything wrong. The father's scepticism is reasonable, and for most of the story the reader is offered the ordinary explanation — a nervous, claustrophobic boy — as a live possibility. Sleator withholds confirmation until the last two words, and the delay is what makes those two words land.

What is left out third, and most importantly, is the outcome. The door closes and the story stops. We do not know what happens to Martin, and there is no rescue, no reveal and no aftermath. The reader is left holding the fear instead of being released from it, and it is the withholding that produces the effect: an ending would have been a relief, whatever it contained.

The technique is worth noticing because it is so economical. The materials are an old lift, a stranger, an unbelieving father and a greeting. Everything frightening in the story is supplied by the reader, and Sleator's craft consists almost entirely in knowing what not to write.

E. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q9. The elevator is a real object doing real work in the plot, and it also carries the story's meaning.

As a symbol of confinement it is nearly perfect: a box barely larger than its occupant, with a door that shuts, no way out until it chooses to stop, and a mechanism the passenger cannot influence. Martin's claustrophobia gives it its physical charge, but its structural function is that it is a trap he must enter voluntarily, every day, in order to live his life.

It is also a symbol of the modern urban condition. Seventeen floors of strangers share this machine and know nothing of one another; the woman can be an unknown quantity precisely because a large building is a place where one meets people repeatedly and never learns who they are. In a village she would have a name.

And it works as an image of helplessness before adult authority. The elevator is old, slow and unreliable, and the adults insist it is perfectly safe. Martin is required to trust a machine he has good reason to distrust, because someone bigger has told him to — which is exactly his position with his father.

The symbol is never announced, and Sleator never comments on it. It is simply the setting, and it means all these things by being what it is.
