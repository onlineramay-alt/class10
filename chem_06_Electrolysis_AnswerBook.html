Q1. (a) Copper metal is deposited at the negative electrode.

With COPPER (active) electrodes the anode dissolves and the cathode gains: Cu²⁺ ions are discharged at the cathode as copper metal, so the cathode gains mass, while at the anode copper atoms go into solution as Cu²⁺, so the anode loses mass. No oxygen is evolved at all, because the copper of the anode is oxidised in preference to the hydroxide ions.

That rules out (b), (c) and (d). This is the principle of the ELECTRO-REFINING of copper.

Official Answer

Q2. (b) 2Br⁻ − 2e⁻ → Br₂.

The anode is the positive electrode, to which the negative ions (anions) migrate, and there they LOSE electrons — that is, they are OXIDISED. Bromide ions therefore give up their electrons and are discharged as bromine gas. Option (a) shows a gain of electrons, which is reduction, and that happens at the cathode.

Q3. (a) (A) is true but (R) is false.

The assertion is correct: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂, so hydrogen and oxygen are liberated in the ratio 2:1 by volume, and twice as much hydrogen is collected.

The reason is stated the wrong way round. Water contains hydrogen and oxygen in the ratio 2:1 by volume (and by atoms), not 1:2. Since the reason as written is false, the answer is (a) — and this is exactly the trap the examiner set.

Q8. (b) Platinum. An inert electrode takes no part in the reaction — it neither dissolves nor reacts with the products. Platinum (or graphite) is inert; nickel, copper and silver are all active electrodes that would dissolve at the anode.

Official Answer

Q9. (c) Redox reaction. Reduction (gain of electrons) always occurs at the cathode and oxidation (loss of electrons) always at the anode, at the same time — so every electrolysis is a redox reaction. In acidified water, H⁺ is reduced to H₂ at the cathode and OH⁻ is oxidised to O₂ at the anode.

B. Identify / Name / Very Short Answer

Q13. In an ELECTROLYTE the current is carried by IONS — mobile positive and negative ions moving in opposite directions through the molten or dissolved substance — and the passage of current causes a chemical change, the electrolyte being decomposed.

In a METALLIC CONDUCTOR the current is carried by FREE ELECTRONS, which drift through the fixed lattice of metal ions. No chemical change occurs and the conductor is not decomposed.

Official Answer

Q14. (a) ELECTROLYSIS — the process by which an electrolyte, in the molten state or in aqueous solution, is decomposed by the passage of an electric current through it.

(b) An ELECTROLYTE — a substance which, in the molten state or in aqueous solution, conducts an electric current and is chemically decomposed by it. Examples: acids, bases and all salts.

(c) A NON-ELECTROLYTE — a substance which does not conduct an electric current in the molten state or in aqueous solution, because it contains no ions. Examples: sugar solution, alcohol, carbon tetrachloride, kerosene.

(d) ELECTROLYTIC DISSOCIATION — the splitting up of an electrolyte into free ions when it is melted or dissolved in water, which is what makes the mobile charge carriers available. It happens on dissolving, before any current is passed.

Q15. (a) CuO + C → Cu + CO — **4, REDOX.** Copper oxide is reduced to copper while carbon is oxidised to carbon monoxide; both happen together, so the reaction is redox.

(b) Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al — **3, REDUCTION.** Gain of electrons.

(c) PbBr₂ → Pb²⁺ + 2Br⁻ — **5, ELECTROLYTIC DISSOCIATION.** The molten salt splits into its free ions; no electrons are gained or lost.

(d) 2O²⁻ − 2e⁻ → O₂ — **1, OXIDATION.** Loss of electrons.

(e) HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻ — **2, IONIZATION.** A covalent molecule splits into ions in water.

NOTE the distinction between (c) and (e), which is the point of the question: DISSOCIATION is the separation of ions that already existed in an ionic compound, while IONIZATION is the formation of ions from a covalent molecule that had none.

C. Short Answer Type

Q19. (i) THE POSITION OF THE ION IN THE ELECTROCHEMICAL SERIES. The ion LOWER in the series is discharged in preference — that is, the one that loses or gains electrons more readily. Among cations the order of ease of discharge is K⁺, Ca²⁺, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Pb²⁺, H⁺, Cu²⁺, Ag²⁺ — with silver and copper discharged most readily and potassium least. Among anions: SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, OH⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ — with iodide discharged most readily.

(ii) THE CONCENTRATION of the ion in the solution. An ion present in much higher concentration may be discharged in preference to one that would otherwise be favoured — which is why concentrated brine gives chlorine at the anode rather than oxygen, although hydroxide is below chloride in the series.

(iii) THE NATURE OF THE ELECTRODE. An ACTIVE electrode takes part in the reaction, as copper does when copper sulphate is electrolysed with copper electrodes, so that the anode dissolves instead of oxygen being evolved. An INERT electrode — platinum or graphite — takes no part.

Q20. Molten lead bromide dissociates into Pb²⁺ and Br⁻ ions, which are free to move.

AT THE CATHODE (negative electrode), the Pb²⁺ ions migrate, GAIN electrons and are reduced:
Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb
A greyish-silvery deposit of MOLTEN LEAD collects at the bottom of the cathode.

AT THE ANODE (positive electrode), the Br⁻ ions migrate, LOSE electrons and are oxidised:
2Br⁻ − 2e⁻ → Br₂
REDDISH-BROWN FUMES of bromine vapour are evolved, with a pungent smell.

Note that the electrolyte must be MOLTEN. Solid lead bromide does not conduct, because although the ions are present they are locked in the crystal lattice and cannot move.

Q21. ELECTROPLATING is the deposition, by electrolysis, of a thin adherent layer of one metal on the surface of another, in order to protect it from corrosion, to improve its appearance, or to give it a harder surface.

THE FOUR CONDITIONS, for silver plating:
(i) The ARTICLE TO BE PLATED is made the CATHODE, so that the metal ions are discharged and deposited on it.
(ii) The ANODE is a plate of PURE SILVER, the metal being deposited, so that it dissolves and keeps the concentration of the electrolyte constant.
(iii) The ELECTROLYTE is a solution of a COMPLEX SALT of the plating metal — sodium argentocyanide for silver — rather than a simple salt, because a complex salt ionises slowly and gives a slow, even, fine-grained and firmly adherent deposit.
(iv) The CURRENT must be LOW and STEADY, and the article thoroughly cleaned and degreased beforehand. A high current gives a rough, loose, powdery deposit that flakes off.

Q24. (a) At the cathode Pb²⁺ gains electrons and is reduced to lead; at the anode Br⁻ loses electrons and is oxidised to bromine. Reduction and oxidation occur together, so the process is a redox reaction.

(b) Copper conducts by the movement of free ELECTRONS through its fixed lattice of atoms, and it is not decomposed by the current. An electrolyte conducts by the movement of IONS and IS decomposed. Copper has no ions, so it is a conductor but not an electrolyte.

Q25. (i) From the copper sulphate: Cu²⁺ and SO₄²⁻. From the water: H⁺ and OH⁻. So four ions in all.

(ii) WITH INERT (PLATINUM) ELECTRODES:
At the cathode, Cu²⁺ is discharged in preference to H⁺, being lower in the electrochemical series:
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu — a reddish-brown deposit of copper.
At the anode, OH⁻ is discharged in preference to SO₄²⁻:
4OH⁻ − 4e⁻ → 2H₂O + O₂ — oxygen gas is evolved.
The blue colour of the solution FADES as Cu²⁺ ions are removed, and the solution becomes acidic as H⁺ and SO₄²⁻ accumulate.

(iii) WITH ACTIVE (COPPER) ELECTRODES:
At the cathode, exactly as before:
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu — the cathode GAINS mass.
At the anode, the copper of the electrode itself is oxidised in preference to the hydroxide ions:
Cu − 2e⁻ → Cu²⁺ — the anode DISSOLVES and LOSES mass.
Because copper enters the solution as fast as it leaves it, the blue colour and the concentration of the solution remain UNCHANGED, and no oxygen is evolved.

(iv) The ELECTRO-REFINING of copper — the impure copper is made the anode and a thin sheet of pure copper the cathode, so that pure copper transfers across and the impurities fall below the anode as anode mud.
