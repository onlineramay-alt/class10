A. Multiple Choice Questions

Q1. (c) Propene, C₃H₆.

Apply the general formulae. Alkanes are CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, so propane is C₃H₈ — saturated, and the wrong hydrogen count. Alkenes are CₙH₂ₙ, so propene is C₃H₆ — unsaturated, with one double bond, and the count matches. Alkynes are CₙH₂ₙ₋₂, so propyne is C₃H₄. Propanol is not a hydrocarbon at all, since it contains oxygen.

Q2. (c) C₂H₆ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + HCl.

Ethane is SATURATED, so it cannot undergo addition — it undergoes SUBSTITUTION, in which a hydrogen atom is replaced by a chlorine atom and hydrogen chloride is given off. Ultraviolet light supplies the energy to start the reaction.

Option (a) is an addition product, impossible for a saturated hydrocarbon. Option (b) invents free hydrogen. Option (d) would require the carbon–carbon bond to break, which does not happen.

Q3. (b) Y only.

From the scheme, X is BUTANE, C₄H₁₀ — a four-carbon chain with only single bonds, so it is SATURATED. It is cracked into Y, PROPENE (C₃H₆), which has a carbon–carbon DOUBLE bond and is therefore UNSATURATED, and Z, METHANE (CH₄), which is saturated.

Only Y contains a multiple carbon–carbon bond, so only Y is unsaturated.

THE TEST, which is what is examinable: a hydrocarbon is unsaturated only if it has a C=C or C≡C bond — an alkene (CₙH₂ₙ) or an alkyne (CₙH₂ₙ₋₂). An alkane (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂) has only single bonds and is saturated.

Q19. ETHANE, C₂H₆.

This is DECARBOXYLATION — heating the sodium salt of a carboxylic acid with soda lime removes the carboxyl group as sodium carbonate and leaves a hydrocarbon with ONE CARBON FEWER:

C₂H₅COONa + NaOH → C₂H₆ + Na₂CO₃

The trap is the carbon count: propanoate has three carbons, so the alkane produced has two — ethane, not propane. Candidates lose this mark constantly.

Q20. ALKANES — CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Saturated, containing only single bonds. They are comparatively unreactive and undergo SUBSTITUTION reactions, as when ethane reacts with chlorine in ultraviolet light to give chloroethane and hydrogen chloride.

ALKENES — CₙH₂ₙ. Unsaturated, containing one carbon–carbon DOUBLE bond. They are reactive and undergo ADDITION reactions — with hydrogen, halogens, hydrogen halides and water. They decolourise bromine water, which is the standard test for unsaturation.

ALKYNES — CₙH₂ₙ₋₂. Unsaturated, containing one carbon–carbon TRIPLE bond. They are the most reactive of the three and undergo addition in two stages, first to the alkene and then to the alkane. They also decolourise bromine water.

Q21. (a) A HOMOLOGOUS SERIES is a family of organic compounds having the same general formula and the same functional group, in which each successive member differs from the previous one by a –CH₂– group (a difference of 14 in molecular mass). Its members are prepared by similar methods, show a gradation in physical properties with rising molecular mass, and have similar chemical properties.

(b) A FUNCTIONAL GROUP is the atom or group of atoms that determines the characteristic chemical properties of a class of organic compounds — for example –OH in alcohols, –COOH in carboxylic acids, –CHO in aldehydes, and the C=C double bond in alkenes.

(c) ISOMERISM is the phenomenon in which two or more compounds have the SAME molecular formula but DIFFERENT structural formulae, and therefore different properties. For example n-butane and iso-butane both have the formula C₄H₁₀.

Q26. (a) PROPAN-1-OL. Three carbons in the longest chain (prop-), all single bonds (-an-), an –OH group (-ol) on carbon 1.

(b) ETHANOIC ACID. Two carbons including the carboxyl carbon (eth-), –COOH group (-oic acid).

(c) PROPENE (prop-1-ene). Three carbons, a double bond (-ene) beginning at carbon 1.

(d) PROPYNE (prop-1-yne). Three carbons, a triple bond (-yne).

(e) PROPANAL. Three carbons including the –CHO carbon, aldehyde group (-al).

THE METHOD, in four steps: (1) count the carbons in the LONGEST continuous chain and take the root — meth, eth, prop, but, pent; (2) identify the bonding — -ane for all single, -ene for a double, -yne for a triple; (3) identify the functional group and take its suffix — -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid; (4) NUMBER the chain from the end that gives the functional group the LOWEST number, and put that number before the suffix.

Official Answer

Q27. ALKANES are SATURATED — every carbon valency is already used up in a single bond, and there is no room to attach anything further without first removing something. The only way an alkane can react, therefore, is for one of its hydrogen atoms to be REPLACED by another atom or group. Being saturated they are also comparatively stable and unreactive, so substitution generally needs sunlight or ultraviolet light to start it.

ALKENES AND ALKYNES are UNSATURATED — they contain a double or triple carbon–carbon bond. In such a bond one pair (or two pairs) of electrons is more loosely held and more exposed than in a single bond, which makes the molecule reactive and gives it a site at which new atoms can be taken on. The multiple bond simply opens up, and the two new atoms attach themselves to the two carbons — an ADDITION reaction, in which nothing is displaced and there is no by-product.

That is why addition is the characteristic reaction of unsaturation, and why decolourising bromine water — an addition — is the test for it.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q28. By BROMINE WATER, or by alkaline potassium permanganate.

Pass each gas separately through reddish-brown BROMINE WATER.

ETHENE, being unsaturated, DECOLOURISES the bromine water at once, because the double bond opens and adds bromine across it:
C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂ (1,2-dibromoethane, colourless)

ETHANE, being saturated, produces NO CHANGE — the bromine water stays reddish-brown, because a saturated hydrocarbon cannot undergo addition.

The same distinction is shown by alkaline potassium permanganate (Baeyer's reagent), which is decolourised from purple by ethene and unaffected by ethane.

Q34. (a) C₂H₄Br₂ + 2KOH (alcoholic) → C₂H₂ + 2KBr + 2H₂O
Double DEHYDROHALOGENATION. Alcoholic potassium hydroxide removes two molecules of hydrogen bromide from 1,2-dibromoethane, giving ACETYLENE (ethyne). The word 'alcoholic' matters: AQUEOUS potassium hydroxide would give a glycol instead, by substitution. Examiners set this distinction deliberately.

(b) CaC₂ + 2H₂O → C₂H₂↑ + Ca(OH)₂
The laboratory preparation of acetylene from calcium carbide.

(c) C₂H₅OH + CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O (conc. H₂SO₄)
ESTERIFICATION. Ethanol and ethanoic acid, warmed with concentrated sulphuric acid as a catalyst and dehydrating agent, give ETHYL ETHANOATE (ethyl acetate) — a sweet, fruity-smelling ester — and water. The reaction is reversible, which is why the sulphuric acid is needed to remove the water and drive it forward.
