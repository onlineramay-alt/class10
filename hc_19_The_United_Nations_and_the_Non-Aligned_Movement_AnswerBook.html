C. Short Answer Type

Q16. (i) To maintain international peace and security — to take effective collective measures for the prevention and removal of threats to the peace, and to bring about the settlement of international disputes by peaceful means. (ii) To develop friendly relations among nations on the basis of respect for the principle of equal rights and self-determination of peoples. (iii) To achieve international cooperation in solving problems of an economic, social, cultural and humanitarian character, and in promoting respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms for all, without distinction of race, sex, language or religion. A fourth is to be a centre for harmonising the actions of nations in the attainment of these common ends.

Official Answer

Q17. It consists of all the member states of the United Nations — at present 193. Each member may send up to five representatives to its meetings, but each member state has one vote only, so that the largest and smallest countries are formally equal.

It meets in regular session once a year, beginning in September, and may be summoned in special session at the request of the Security Council or of a majority of members. It elects its own President for each session, and ordinary questions are decided by a simple majority, important ones — peace and security, admission of members, the budget — by a two-thirds majority of those present and voting.

Official Answer

Q18. (i) Deliberative — it may discuss any question or matter within the scope of the Charter, including any matter of international peace and security brought before it, and may make recommendations upon it. This is why it is called the world's forum, or the deliberative organ of the United Nations.

(ii) Elective — it elects the ten non-permanent members of the Security Council, the members of the Economic and Social Council, and, together with the Security Council, the judges of the International Court of Justice; and it appoints the Secretary-General on the recommendation of the Security Council. It also admits new members on that Council's recommendation.

(iii) Financial — it considers and approves the budget of the United Nations and apportions its expenses among the member states, so that no organ of the UN can act without its money.

It also receives and considers the annual reports of the Security Council and the other organs; and under the 'Uniting for Peace' resolution it may act on a matter of peace and security when the Security Council is paralysed by a veto.

Official Answer

Q19. It has fifteen members. Five are permanent — the United States, the United Kingdom, Russia, France and China — and each of these possesses the power of veto, so that a single negative vote from any of them defeats a substantive resolution.

The other ten are non-permanent, elected by the General Assembly for a term of two years, five retiring each year; a retiring member is not eligible for immediate re-election, and the seats are distributed so as to give a fair geographical representation. Each member has one vote; a decision on a procedural matter needs nine votes, and on a substantive matter nine votes including the concurring votes of all five permanent members.

Official Answer

Q20. (i) It bears the primary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and security. It may investigate any dispute or situation which might lead to international friction, and call on the parties to settle it by negotiation, arbitration or judicial settlement, recommending the terms if necessary.

(ii) It may take enforcement action against a state that breaks the peace. It can call on members to apply economic sanctions or sever diplomatic relations, and if such measures prove inadequate it may authorise the use of armed force, member states making forces available for the purpose. It also establishes and directs peacekeeping operations.

(iii) It shares in the constitutional functions of the organisation: it recommends the admission, suspension or expulsion of members and the appointment of the Secretary-General to the General Assembly, and elects the judges of the International Court of Justice jointly with the Assembly.

Official Answer

Q21. It consists of fifteen judges, elected for a term of nine years by the General Assembly and the Security Council voting independently of each other; a candidate must obtain an absolute majority in both. No two judges may be nationals of the same country, and the bench as a whole is chosen to represent the main forms of civilisation and the principal legal systems of the world.

Five judges retire every three years, and a retiring judge is eligible for re-election. The Court elects its own President and Vice-President for three years and appoints its Registrar. It sits at The Hague, and nine judges form a quorum.

Official Answer

Q22. (i) It decides, in accordance with international law, the legal disputes submitted to it by states — questions of treaty interpretation, of boundaries, of rights of passage and of state responsibility. Only states, not individuals, may be parties before it, and its jurisdiction depends on the consent of the states concerned.

(ii) It gives advisory opinions on legal questions referred to it by the General Assembly, the Security Council or other authorised organs and specialised agencies of the United Nations; such opinions are not binding but carry great authority.

(iii) It applies and thereby develops international law — international conventions, international custom, the general principles of law recognised by civilised nations, and, as subsidiary means, judicial decisions and the writings of the most highly qualified publicists.

Official Answer

Q23. (i) It identifies sites of outstanding universal value and inscribes them on the World Heritage List, and it recognises living traditions on the list of Intangible Cultural Heritage — Kolkata's Durga Puja was given that status in 2021 — so that they are protected by international agreement.

(ii) It provides technical advice and financial assistance for the preservation and restoration of monuments and works of art, and helps member states train the specialists needed to look after them; its rescue of the Abu Simbel temples in Egypt is the best known instance.

(iii) It promotes museums, libraries and archives, encourages the translation of the classic works of each country into other languages, and supports the study and revival of traditional crafts, music and languages — so that cultures are kept alive and made known to the rest of the world, not merely preserved as monuments.

Official Answer

Q24. UNICEF — the United Nations Children's Fund, originally the United Nations International Children's Emergency Fund, set up in 1946 to help the children of war-devastated Europe and now working for children everywhere.

(i) Health and nutrition — it runs and supplies immunisation programmes against the killer diseases of childhood, provides oral rehydration salts and therapeutic food for malnourished children, and works for safe drinking water and sanitation.
(ii) Education — it supports primary education, especially for girls and for children in remote or disadvantaged communities, supplying materials, building schools and training teachers.
(iii) Protection — it works against child labour, child marriage, trafficking and every form of exploitation and abuse, and it is among the first agencies on the ground in war and natural disaster, providing emergency relief for children and reuniting families.

Official Answer

Q25. (i) It directs and coordinates international health work, acting as the authority to which governments turn in an epidemic and setting the international rules under which outbreaks are reported and contained.

(ii) It fights communicable disease — running global immunisation and eradication campaigns, of which the eradication of smallpox in 1980 is the outstanding success, and combating malaria, tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS and polio.

(iii) It sets international standards — for the safety, quality and naming of drugs and vaccines, for food and water, and for the classification of diseases — and collects and publishes the world's health statistics.

(iv) It strengthens national health services, advising governments on health policy, helping train doctors, nurses and health workers, and promoting maternal and child health, nutrition, sanitation and health education.

Official Answer

Q26. (i) To stay out of the military blocs and alliances of the Cold War, and to preserve an independent judgement — remaining friendly with both the power blocs while deciding every international question on its own merits rather than on instructions from an ally.

(ii) To oppose colonialism, imperialism, racialism and apartheid, and to support the peoples still struggling for national independence — a natural aim for a movement made up of countries that had themselves only recently been colonies.

(iii) To work for world peace and for disarmament, particularly nuclear disarmament, and for the settlement of international disputes by peaceful means, on the principles of Panchsheel. To these may be added the demand for a fairer international economic order, so that the newly independent countries could develop, and the strengthening of the United Nations and of the role of the developing world within it.

Q29. (i) It has the primary responsibility for maintaining international peace and security, and may investigate any dispute likely to endanger it and recommend terms of settlement; it may take enforcement action, calling for economic sanctions or the severing of relations and, if necessary, authorising armed force, and it establishes peacekeeping operations; and it recommends the admission of members and the appointment of the Secretary-General, and elects the judges of the ICJ jointly with the General Assembly.

(ii) Jawaharlal Nehru of India and Gamal Abdel Nasser of Egypt (Tito, Sukarno and Nkrumah equally acceptable). Two objectives: to stay out of the Cold War military blocs while keeping friendly relations with both and judging each issue on its merits; and to oppose colonialism, imperialism, racialism and apartheid and support peoples still struggling for independence.

(iii) Non-alignment means refusing to join either of the two power blocs while remaining free to deal with both and to decide each question on its own merits — an independence of judgement, not neutrality or isolation. The newly independent countries adopted it because they had just emerged from foreign rule and were not willing to exchange one dependence for another; because their urgent need was development, not armaments, and alignment would have drawn them into other people's quarrels and expenditure; and because, being individually weak, they could exert far more influence in the world by acting together as a third voice than by adding themselves to a bloc already led by a superpower.

Q32. (i) UNESCO — the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organisation.
(ii) Intangible cultural heritage is living culture rather than built culture: the practices, performances, rituals, festivals, crafts and oral traditions that a community inherits and keeps alive — a festival like Durga Puja, or a form of music or weaving. A World Heritage Site is a physical place of outstanding universal value, such as a monument or a natural landscape. The one is protected by conserving stone; the other only by keeping the practice alive in the community that performs it, which is why UNESCO's protection here takes the form of recognition, documentation and support rather than restoration.
(iii) Any two of: inscribing monuments and natural sites on the World Heritage List and so bringing them under international protection; giving technical advice and funds for restoring monuments and training conservation specialists, as in the rescue of the Abu Simbel temples; and promoting museums, libraries and archives and encouraging the translation of each country's classic literature into other languages.
