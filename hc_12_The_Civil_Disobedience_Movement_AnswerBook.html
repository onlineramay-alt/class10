Q11. Lord Irwin. The agreement they signed on 5 March 1931 is known as the Gandhi-Irwin Pact, or the Delhi Pact.

Official Answer

Q12. Its significance lies chiefly in its failure. Gandhi attended it in London as the sole representative of the Congress, under the terms of the Gandhi-Irwin Pact, and it was the only one of the three Conferences the Congress joined. But the British government would not discuss the transfer of real power, and turned the proceedings instead upon the question of minorities and separate electorates — on which Gandhi could not agree, refusing in particular to accept separate electorates for the depressed classes, whom he regarded as part of the Hindu community.

He returned to India empty-handed in December 1931. The government at once resumed repression, arresting the leaders and issuing ordinances, and the Civil Disobedience Movement was renewed in January 1932. The Conference thus destroyed the last hope of a negotiated settlement in that period and proved to Indians that constitutional discussion alone would yield nothing.

C. Short Answer Type

Q13. (i) The Simon Commission of 1927. Appointed to review the working of the 1919 Act, it contained not one Indian, which was taken as a public declaration that Indians were unfit to have a voice in their own constitution. It was boycotted throughout the country, and at Lahore in 1928 Lala Lajpat Rai was struck down in a lathi charge while leading the protest and died of his injuries.

(ii) The refusal of Dominion Status. The Nehru Report of 1928 had asked for it and set a deadline; the government let the deadline pass, and Gandhi's Eleven Demands of 1930 were likewise ignored. The Congress therefore declared Purna Swaraj — complete independence — as its goal at Lahore in December 1929, and observed 26 January 1930 as Independence Day.

(iii) Economic distress. The world depression had brought a collapse in agricultural prices, while land revenue was still demanded in full, so the peasantry was ready for a movement as it had not been for years.

The movement began with the Dandi March, or Salt March, of March-April 1930.

Official Answer

Q14. On 12 March 1930 Gandhi set out from the Sabarmati Ashram near Ahmedabad with 78 chosen followers, and walked some 240 miles to the village of Dandi on the Arabian Sea, reaching it on 6 April. Along the way he addressed enormous meetings, and thousands joined the march; village headmen resigned their offices as he passed. At Dandi, on the morning of 6 April, he waded into the sea and picked up a lump of natural salt from the shore, so breaking the law which reserved the manufacture of salt to the government.

The act was in itself trivial and that was its power: it was an offence any Indian anywhere could commit, peacefully and without weapons. Within weeks salt was being made illegally the length of the coast, foreign cloth and liquor shops were picketed, and the government had arrested some 60,000 people, Gandhi among them. The march thus launched the Civil Disobedience Movement, and the world press carried it everywhere.

Official Answer

Q15. (i) The deliberate breaking of the salt law — the manufacture of salt in defiance of the government monopoly, all along the coast and inland wherever it could be done. (ii) The boycott of foreign cloth and of liquor shops, with peaceful picketing of the shops that sold them, and the refusal to pay taxes — a no-tax campaign in Bardoli and elsewhere, and the refusal of the chowkidari tax in Bengal and Bihar. (iii) The boycott of government institutions — the resignation of Indians from government service and of village officials from their posts, the withdrawal of students from government schools and colleges, the boycott of the legislatures and of the courts, and the deliberate breach of the forest laws by the tribal people of central India.

Q17. The Government agreed to:
(i) release all political prisoners who had not been convicted of violence;
(ii) withdraw all ordinances and pending prosecutions, and restore the property confiscated from satyagrahis;
(iii) permit the peaceful picketing of liquor shops and shops selling foreign cloth, and allow people living within a certain distance of the coast to manufacture salt free of duty for their own use.

The Congress agreed to:
(iv) suspend the Civil Disobedience Movement, to participate in the Second Round Table Conference, and not to press for an inquiry into the excesses committed by the police.

NOTE — The last of these was a real concession on Gandhi's part, and was criticised at the time by younger Congressmen, since it left the beatings and firings of the previous year uninvestigated.

Official Answer

Q18. (i) It made the demand for complete independence irreversible and forced the British to negotiate with the Congress as an equal — the Viceroy himself treating with a man the government had imprisoned, which the diehards in England found intolerable, and the Congress attending the Round Table Conference as the representative of India. It led on to the Government of India Act of 1935.

(ii) It broadened the movement enormously. Women came into public political life in very large numbers for the first time, picketing shops and courting arrest; the peasantry joined through the no-tax campaigns; and it reached regions barely touched before, notably the North West Frontier Province, where Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan's Khudai Khidmatgars made it a mass movement among the Pathans. Imports of foreign cloth fell steeply, and some 90,000 people went to prison.

Official Answer

Q20. (i) The Rowlatt Act of 1919; the Jallianwala Bagh massacre and the Punjab atrocities, followed by the British defence of General Dyer; and the Khilafat grievance, which Gandhi joined to the national cause.

(ii) The all-British Simon Commission of 1927 and the death of Lala Lajpat Rai in the protest against it; the refusal of Dominion Status, leading the Congress to declare Purna Swaraj at Lahore in 1929; and the acute agricultural distress of the depression years. The movement began with the Dandi March.

(iii) The Pact was an attempt to end the confrontation because each side gave up something of what it had been fighting for. The Government released political prisoners not convicted of violence; withdrew the repressive ordinances and the prosecutions under them and restored confiscated property; and conceded peaceful picketing and the right of coastal villagers to make salt for their own use — the very point on which the movement had begun. In return the Congress suspended the Civil Disobedience Movement, agreed to attend the Second Round Table Conference, and dropped its demand for an inquiry into police excesses. Both sides, in short, stepped back: the government from repression and the Congress from defiance, so that negotiation could take the place of confrontation.

Official Answer

Q21. (i) Gandhi left the Sabarmati Ashram near Ahmedabad on 12 March 1930 with 78 followers and walked some 240 miles to Dandi on the Arabian Sea, arriving on 6 April, addressing great meetings on the way. There he picked up natural salt from the shore, breaking the law that reserved its manufacture to the government, and so launched the Civil Disobedience Movement.

(ii) Because the salt monopoly and the tax on salt were an injustice that needed no explanation to anybody. Salt is a necessity of life for every person, rich or poor, and a tax on it falls hardest on the poorest; that the government should forbid a fisherman to gather salt from his own shore was an absurdity as well as a hardship. It was also the perfect vehicle for mass civil disobedience: breaking the salt law required no organisation, no weapons and no leader — any villager on any coast could do it — and it was unmistakably peaceful, so that repression against it could only make the government look ridiculous and brutal at once.

(iii) It made complete independence the settled demand of the nation and forced the government to negotiate with the Congress as an equal, leading to the Gandhi-Irwin Pact and the Round Table Conference. It brought women into public political life in very large numbers for the first time. It spread the movement to regions barely touched before, notably the North West Frontier under Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan. And it hit British commercial interests hard — imports of foreign cloth fell steeply — while some 90,000 Indians went to prison, demonstrating a willingness to suffer that no government could indefinitely resist. It led on to the Government of India Act of 1935.
