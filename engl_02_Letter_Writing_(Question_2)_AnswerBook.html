English Language — Letter Writing (Question 2)

ICSE Class 10 Answer Book

Generated 2026-09-01

The syllabus states that 'the format of the letter with address, introduction, conclusion, etc. will form part of the assessment'. Format marks are therefore free marks and are lost every year by candidates who write a good letter in the wrong shape. Two complete model letters are given below — one formal, one informal — and the remaining answers give the plan and the specific content points, on the assumption that the format has been learnt from those two.

A. Formal Letters

Q1. FULL MODEL LETTER — study the format as carefully as the content

14, Sunder Nagar
Off Station Road
Dhanbad 826001

12 February 2026

The Municipal Commissioner
Dhanbad Municipal Corporation
Civic Centre
Dhanbad 826001

Sir,

I write on behalf of the residents of Sunder Nagar regarding the condition of the drainage system in our locality, which has now deteriorated to a point at which it is affecting public health.

The open drain running along the western edge of the colony has not been desilted for over two years. It is choked with plastic and silt, so that waste water no longer flows but stands. After even moderate rain it overflows across the approach road, which is the only route to the primary school at the end of the lane; children walk through it twice a day. The standing water has bred mosquitoes in numbers we have not seen before, and four households in our lane alone have had cases of dengue since September. The smell in the afternoons has made it impossible to keep windows open.

May I respectfully suggest three measures. First, an immediate desilting of the stretch between the water tank and the school gate, which is where the blockage begins. Second, the covering of that stretch with concrete slabs, as has already been done in the adjoining ward, so that refuse cannot be thrown in. Third, a fixed monthly schedule for cleaning, displayed at the ward office, so that residents can report a lapse rather than discovering it after a flood.

The residents' association is willing to assist your staff in identifying the worst-affected points and to help ensure that households do not dispose of solid waste into the drain.

I should be grateful for an early inspection of the area.

Thank you.

Yours faithfully,
Ankita Prasad
(Secretary, Sunder Nagar Residents' Association)

FORMAT CHECKLIST FOR EVERY FORMAL LETTER
1. Sender's address, top left, no name above it. 2. Date below it, in full. 3. Receiver's designation and address. 4. Salutation 'Sir' or 'Madam' — not 'Dear Sir' in the ICSE convention, and never 'Respected Sir'. 5. Body in three or four paragraphs: purpose, detail, request. 6. 'Yours faithfully' with a small f, followed by your name. 7. 'Yours sincerely' only if you have addressed the person by name. Note that a subject line is optional in a letter and compulsory in an e-mail.

Q2. PLAN — the format is as in the model letter above.

Paragraph 1 — purpose in one sentence: you are writing about the irregular clearing of garbage bins in a named locality.

Paragraph 2 — the problem, in concrete detail. Which bins, at which junction; how long since they were last emptied; what has followed — stray dogs and cattle scattering refuse across the road, flies, the smell, the shopkeepers whose customers have gone, the risk to children walking to school. Detail is what distinguishes a complaint from a grumble: 'the bin at the corner of Nehru Road has not been emptied since 3 January' is worth more than 'garbage is lying everywhere'.

Paragraph 3 — suggestions, which the question explicitly requires. An increase in the frequency of collection to alternate days; the replacement of the two damaged bins with covered ones; separate bins for wet and dry waste; a display of the collection schedule at the site so lapses can be reported; and an offer from the residents to cooperate.

Paragraph 4 — a short, courteous request for early action.

TONE: firm but never rude. A letter that accuses the Commissioner personally of negligence loses marks for tone; one that states facts and proposes remedies gains them.

Q3. PLAN

A letter to your own Principal is formal: sender's address, date, 'The Principal' with the school's name and address, salutation 'Madam', and 'Yours faithfully'. Sign with your name, class and section — this is expected in a letter to your own school and is often forgotten.

Paragraph 1 — the request, stated plainly: permission to start a self-defence club.

Paragraph 2 — objectives, which the question names as compulsory. To teach basic techniques of self-protection and escape; to build awareness of personal safety and how to avoid risk; to develop fitness, alertness and confidence.

Paragraph 3 — benefits to students. Confidence in moving about the city independently; physical fitness; the discipline of regular practice; and — worth saying — that a student who has trained is less likely to panic, which matters more than technique.

Paragraph 4 — the practical proposal, which is what turns a request into a workable one: the proposed day and hour (after school, so no class time is lost), the space required, a qualified instructor you have identified, an estimate of numbers, and the assurance that a teacher would supervise.

WATCH: address every element the question lists. Here that means objectives AND benefits — two distinct things, and candidates routinely merge them and lose the marks for one.

Q4. PLAN

This letter succeeds or fails on precision. A lost-property letter without exact particulars is useless, and the examiner is testing whether you understand that.

Paragraph 1 — the loss, with the facts that identify it: the train's name and number, the date, the coach and berth number, the station at which you alighted and the approximate time.

Paragraph 2 — the description of the suitcase, detailed enough to distinguish it from every other: colour, size, material, make, any strap or sticker, the name tag if there was one, and — importantly — the contents, since a claimant who can list the contents proves ownership.

Paragraph 3 — the request: that the coach be checked at the next halt or the terminus, that the suitcase be held in the station's lost property office, and that you be informed. Give a telephone number and address.

Paragraph 4 — thanks and an offer to collect it in person with proof of identity.

WATCH: no anger and no blame — the loss was your own doing, and the letter should say so without grovelling. Courtesy is more likely to get the suitcase back than indignation, and the examiner is marking judgement as well as English.

Q5. PLAN

Three elements are named and all three must appear: the problem, the danger, the solution.

Problem — where exactly the lamp post stands, how many wires, how low they hang, how long they have been so, and whether they are live.

Danger — and this is the paragraph that carries the letter. Be specific and factual rather than dramatic: the wires hang at head height over a footpath used by six hundred children twice a day; the ground floods in the monsoon and water conducts; a stray dog was electrocuted at the same post last year. One concrete instance is worth three sentences of warning.

Solution — immediate cordoning of the area, inspection by the electricity department, insulation or re-routing of the wires, and, since the school is directly affected, a request that it be done before the school reopens after the coming holiday.

TONE: urgency without hysteria. The strongest sentence such a letter can contain is a plain statement of what will happen if nothing is done, unaccompanied by exclamation marks.

Q6. PLAN

The difficulty here is tone. You are asking the head of your school to change an examination timetable, and the letter must be reasonable rather than complaining, or it will fail whatever its English.

Paragraph 1 — the request, courteously and specifically: a postponement of the half-yearly examinations by (say) ten days.

Paragraph 2 — why it is necessary. Name the activities and their dates — the inter-school sports meet, the annual concert rehearsals, the science exhibition — and show the overlap with revision time, ideally with a rough count of the days lost. Be careful to praise the activities rather than resent them; you are not arguing that they should be cancelled.

Paragraph 3 — the benefit to pupils, which the question requires. Better-prepared candidates and therefore results that reflect ability; students able to give the activities their full effort instead of resenting them; less anxiety and illness in the week before the papers.

Paragraph 4 — acknowledge the difficulty you are creating (the term's calendar, the teachers' marking schedule) and offer flexibility. A request that shows awareness of the other person's constraints is far more likely to succeed, and examiners reward that maturity.

B. Informal / Friendly Letters

Q7. FULL MODEL LETTER — note how the format differs from a formal letter

27, Lake Gardens
Kolkata 700045

8 February 2026

Dear Ananya,

I hope the cough has finally gone. My mother asked after you twice this week, which in her language means she is worried.

I am writing with a proposal. The preliminaries begin in five weeks and I have discovered, sitting at my desk for three hours on Sunday and retaining almost nothing, that I cannot revise alone. I would like to form a small study group — four of us at most — and I would very much like you to be one of them.

Here is what I have in mind. We would meet three afternoons a week, at my house or yours, from half past four to half past six. Each meeting would take one subject, decided in advance, so that nobody arrives unprepared. The first hour each of us would work through past papers separately and in silence; the second we would spend explaining to one another whatever we got wrong. I am told that explaining a thing you have just understood is the surest way of finding out whether you have actually understood it, and my own experience with Trigonometry last year suggests this is true.

I think it would help us both, and for different reasons. You are far better than I am at organising an answer, and I am quicker at spotting where a question is really going, and there is no sense in each of us struggling alone with what the other finds easy. There is also the plain matter of discipline: I will not abandon a session at five o'clock if somebody else is sitting across the table.

Do think about it and telephone me this week. If your mother would rather you did not travel in the evenings, we can meet entirely at your house — I do not mind the bus at all.

Give my regards to your grandmother.

Yours affectionately,
Meher

FORMAT CHECKLIST FOR EVERY INFORMAL LETTER
1. Sender's address and date — yes, these are still required. 2. NO receiver's address: this is the commonest error, and candidates who have learnt only the formal layout insert one automatically. 3. Salutation 'Dear ___' with the first name. 4. A natural opening line — ask after the person before you come to your business. 5. 'Yours affectionately' or 'Yours lovingly' for family and close friends; 'Yours sincerely' for an acquaintance. 6. First name only at the end. 7. The register should be relaxed — contractions are fine — but never slangy, and never in message-abbreviations.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q8. PLAN

An informal letter — sender's address and date, 'Dear Grandmother' or 'Dear Ammamma', no receiver's address, 'Yours affectionately'.

Choose a skill with texture: cooking one particular dish, a stitch, tuning an instrument, grafting a plant, a card game, a language, mending things. The more specific the skill, the better the letter.

Paragraph 1 — open warmly and come to the thanks quickly.

Paragraph 2 — a memory of the teaching itself. This is the heart of the letter and the part candidates skip: the afternoons, her patience or impatience, the thing you did wrong eleven times, what she said when you finally got it. A remembered sentence in her own words is worth a great deal here.

Paragraph 3 — how you will use it, which the question requires. Be concrete: you have made the dish twice for the family; you intend to teach your cousin; you have mended three things this month.

Paragraph 4 — why it matters to you, and this should go beyond the skill itself — it is a thread back to her, or to a place, or a way of being useful.

WATCH: keep the warmth genuine and unsentimental. A letter that calls her 'my dearest sweetest grandmother' in every paragraph reads as insincere; one that reports she told you your first attempt looked like a bandage reads as true.

Q9. PLAN

Three required elements: thanks, how you will spend it, and why that choice.

Paragraph 1 — thank him, and say you missed him. Mention one thing about the day he would have enjoyed, so that the letter feels like a report to someone who cares rather than a receipt.

Paragraph 2 — the plan for the money, and this is where the marks are. Make one clear choice and describe it exactly: a particular book or set of books; a cricket bat you have been to look at twice; the remaining amount towards a bicycle; art materials; a contribution to something for the house.

Paragraph 3 — why that choice, which is what the examiner is really testing. The reasoning should show some thought — you chose the thing you would still be using in a year; you have wanted it long enough to be sure; you are saving part of it because he told you once that a gift spent immediately is forgotten immediately.

Paragraph 4 — a warm close and an invitation for him to visit.

WATCH: a candidate who writes 'I will spend it on whatever I like' has thrown away the two parts of the question that carry the marks.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q10. PLAN

Three elements in a given order — preparation, performance, feelings afterwards — so use them as your three body paragraphs.

Preparation: the weeks before. The piece chosen and why; the passage that would not come right; the teacher's corrections; practising until the neighbours complained; the last rehearsal.

Performance: slow this down. The wait in the wings, the walk to the microphone, the first note, the mistake nobody noticed or the one everybody did, the moment the nervousness left. One vivid detail — a squeaking chair, a fan, someone's phone — will do more than a paragraph of adjectives.

Afterwards: be honest. Relief usually comes before pride; sometimes there is disappointment even in a good result, or a strange flatness. If you did not win, say so plainly — a letter that reports a second place with good humour is more attractive than one that reports a triumph.

WATCH: it is a letter to a grandfather, so a little affection and a little teasing both belong. Ask after him at the start; do not make the whole letter about yourself without a word for the reader.

Q11. PLAN

Informal letter. Open by asking after the illness — a letter that plunges straight into the competition without a word about the friend's health reads badly, and examiners notice.

Paragraph 2 — the event: which schools took part, where and when, the play your school performed, how the audience received it, who won.

Paragraph 3 — your role, which the question requires explicitly. Whether you acted, and as whom; or worked backstage, on lights, on costumes. Give one incident from it — the line you forgot, the prop that broke, the boy who missed his entrance.

Paragraph 4 — say you missed them, and why the day would have been better with them there. A specific reason ('you would have known what to do when the curtain jammed') is worth more than a general one.

Paragraph 5 — an offer to bring the notes over, and a hope for a quick recovery.

WATCH: the ICSE 2023 specimen sets exactly this topic, and the 2019 paper before it, so it is very well worn — which means the examiner will be reading many versions of it. The letters that stand out are those with one particular, unexpected detail.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q12. PLAN

Two elements: the event, and your feelings at being publicly praised. The second is the more interesting and the more often neglected.

Paragraph 2 — the event and your part in the organising. Committees do unglamorous work: registrations, chairs, the sound system, feeding two hundred visitors, the thing that went wrong at nine in the morning. Say what you actually did.

Paragraph 3 — the assembly. The surprise of hearing your own name; standing up in front of the whole school; the particular embarrassment of being looked at; what the Principal said.

Paragraph 4 — your feelings, honestly. Pride, certainly, but the good version of this letter admits the complications: that you were mostly relieved; that you thought of the one person on the committee who was not named; that praise in public is uncomfortable as well as pleasant.

WATCH: a grandmother is a reader who is entirely on your side, which permits a warmth and a frankness the formal letters do not. Use that — it is why the question specifies her.
