B. Short Answer Type

Q3. CLIMATE. Sugarcane is a tropical crop needing a LONG, HOT, HUMID growing season — temperatures of 20 to 27 °C for eight to ten months — with rainfall of 100 to 150 cm, or irrigation where the rain is less. Frost is fatal, and a cool dry spell at ripening increases the sugar content. It cannot tolerate very high temperatures above 45 °C.

SOIL. Deep, well-drained fertile soil — the alluvial loam of the northern plains and the black soil of the Deccan are both suitable. It exhausts the soil rapidly, so it requires heavy manuring and rotation.

THE TWO REGIONS, and the contrast between them is regularly examined.
THE NORTH — Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Punjab and Haryana — grows the larger area and the larger tonnage, but the growing season is shorter, the winter frost is a risk, and the sucrose content is lower, so the yield of sugar per hectare is poorer.
THE SOUTH — Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh — has a longer growing season, no frost, an equable maritime climate and assured irrigation, so the sucrose content and the yield per hectare are much higher, and the mills are larger and more efficient. Maharashtra now produces the most sugar, though Uttar Pradesh grows the most cane.

Q4. Sugarcane is weight-losing because only about ten per cent of the weight of the cane emerges as sugar — the rest is water, fibre (bagasse) and molasses. To carry the whole cane any distance is therefore to pay freight on nine parts of waste for one part of product.

Worse, cane is PERISHABLE. Once cut, its sucrose begins to convert and it loses about five per cent of its sugar within twenty-four hours, so it must be crushed almost at once. It is also bulky and awkward to load.

The consequence is that sugar mills are located IN THE CANE-GROWING AREAS THEMSELVES, usually within a few kilometres of the fields, rather than near the market or the port. It is one of the clearest examples in Indian geography of an industry tied to its raw material, and it explains why the sugar mills of India are scattered through the countryside of Uttar Pradesh and Maharashtra rather than concentrated in the cities.

Q5. GROUNDNUT. A KHARIF crop of the tropics. Needs 20 to 30 °C, moderate rainfall of 50 to 75 cm, and a dry spell at ripening — heavy rain at harvest ruins the pods, which develop underground. Soil: light, well-drained SANDY LOAM or red or black soil; it will not grow in heavy waterlogged clay. Being a legume it fixes nitrogen and improves the soil. Chief areas: Gujarat (the largest), Rajasthan, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Maharashtra.

MUSTARD (and rapeseed). A RABI crop of the cool season. Needs 10 to 25 °C, a cool moist growing period and dry weather at harvest, with 25 to 40 cm of rainfall or light irrigation. Soil: light loam and alluvial soil. Chief areas: Rajasthan (much the largest), Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal — that is, the north and north-west.

SOYA BEAN. A KHARIF crop. Needs a warm moist climate, 20 to 30 °C, with 60 to 100 cm of rainfall. Soil: well-drained fertile loam and black soil. It is a legume and enriches the soil with nitrogen, and it is exceptionally rich in protein as well as oil, so the residue after crushing is valuable as cattle feed and as food. Chief areas: Madhya Pradesh (over half the crop), Maharashtra and Rajasthan.

USES OF OILSEEDS GENERALLY: edible oil for cooking; vanaspati; soap, paints, varnishes, lubricants and cosmetics; and the oil-cake left after crushing is a valuable cattle feed and manure.
