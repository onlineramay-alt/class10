C. Short Answer Type

Q17. He must be a citizen of India, and he must either (i) have held a judicial office in the territory of India for at least ten years, or (ii) have been an advocate of a High Court, or of two or more such courts in succession, for at least ten years.

NOTE — Compare this with the Supreme Court, where five years as a High Court judge or ten years at the Bar will do, and where a 'distinguished jurist' may also be appointed. There is no 'distinguished jurist' route to a High Court.

Official Answer

Q18. Original Jurisdiction is the power of the High Court to hear and decide a case in the first instance, rather than on appeal from a lower court.

Three such cases: (i) petitions for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights, in which it issues writs under Article 226; (ii) cases relating to the revenue of the State and matters arising in its collection; (iii) cases relating to wills, divorce, admiralty and company law, and contempt of court. (Election petitions disputing the election of a member of Parliament or of the State Legislature, and cases withdrawn from a subordinate court because they involve a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution, also come to it directly.)

Official Answer

Q19. It is the power of the High Court to hear appeals against the decisions of the courts subordinate to it. In civil matters an appeal lies to the High Court from the decisions of the District Courts, and from those of subordinate civil judges where the amount involved exceeds the prescribed limit. In criminal matters an appeal lies from the judgements of the Sessions Courts where the sentence exceeds a prescribed term of imprisonment; and a sentence of death passed by a Sessions Judge has no effect at all unless it is confirmed by the High Court. The High Court may confirm, reverse or vary the decision appealed against.

Official Answer

Q20. The judgements and proceedings of the High Court are recorded, printed and preserved permanently. Those records are admitted as evidence and cannot be questioned when produced before any subordinate court, and they are cited as precedents which bind the courts below. Being a Court of Record also carries the power to punish for contempt of itself — whether committed in the face of the court or outside it — so that its dignity and authority are maintained.

Official Answer

Q21. (i) Habeas Corpus — directing that a detained person be produced before the court, so that the legality of the detention may be examined and he may be released if it is unlawful. (ii) Mandamus — commanding a public official, corporation or inferior court to perform a public duty which it has failed or refused to perform. (iii) Prohibition — forbidding an inferior court or tribunal to continue proceedings in a matter beyond its jurisdiction. (iv) Certiorari — quashing an order already made by an inferior court or tribunal that acted without jurisdiction or against natural justice, or transferring the case to the High Court. (The fifth writ is Quo Warranto, restraining a person from holding a public office to which he is not entitled.)

Official Answer

Q22. Lok Adalats are 'People's Courts', established under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, in which pending or pre-litigation disputes are settled by conciliation and mutual compromise rather than by a contested trial. They are presided over by serving or retired judicial officers and other persons of repute.

Three advantages: (i) They cost the parties nothing — no court fee is charged, and any fee already paid on the petition is refunded when the matter is settled. (ii) They are speedy: a dispute that has dragged on for years in the regular courts can be disposed of in a single sitting, and the procedure is simple enough that a party may present his own case without a lawyer. (iii) The settlement is by compromise, so neither side is a defeated litigant and relations between the parties are preserved; and the award is final and binding, with no appeal against it, so the litigation truly ends there.

Official Answer

Q23. They take a very large number of small and compoundable disputes — motor accident claims, family and matrimonial matters, cheque dishonour cases, petty civil suits — out of the regular stream and settle them by compromise. This greatly reduces the arrears and the mounting burden of pending cases in the District Courts and High Courts, leaving those courts free to devote their time to the serious and contested matters that only they can decide.

Official Answer

Q24. Two advantages of the Lok Adalat: there is no court fee, and any fee already paid is refunded on settlement, so it is inexpensive; and cases are disposed of quickly, often at a single sitting, by compromise rather than by a contested trial.

Difference: the Court of the District Judge is the highest civil court in a district and tries civil suits, while the Sessions Court is the highest criminal court in the district and tries criminal cases. The same officer normally presides over both, sitting as District Judge on the civil side and as Sessions Judge on the criminal side.

Official Answer

Q25. A High Court consists of a Chief Justice and such other judges as the President may from time to time think it necessary to appoint. Unlike the Supreme Court, no fixed maximum number of judges is laid down in the Constitution — the strength of each High Court varies with the volume of its work.

The Constitution provides for a High Court for each State, but Parliament may establish a common High Court for two or more States, or for a State and a Union Territory, and several such common High Courts exist.

Official Answer

Q28. (i) Original Jurisdiction is the power to hear a case in the first instance rather than on appeal. Two such cases: petitions for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights, decided by the issue of writs under Article 226; and cases relating to the revenue of the State.

(ii) Advantages of the Lok Adalat: no court fee is charged and any fee paid is refunded on settlement; and disputes are settled quickly, by compromise, often at a single sitting. Difference: the Court of the District Judge is the highest civil court of the district, the Sessions Court its highest criminal court — usually the same officer sitting in two capacities.

(iii) (a) Court of Record — the High Court's judgements are recorded and preserved permanently, are cited as binding precedents in the courts subordinate to it and cannot be questioned there, and it has the power to punish for contempt of itself. (b) Judicial Review — the power to examine the constitutional validity of a State law or an executive order and to declare it void if it conflicts with the Constitution, particularly where it infringes a Fundamental Right.

Official Answer

Q29. (i) Appellate Jurisdiction is the power to hear appeals from the courts subordinate to the High Court. On the civil side, appeals lie from the District Courts and from subordinate civil judges where the amount involved exceeds the prescribed limit; on the criminal side, from the Sessions Courts where the sentence exceeds a prescribed term, and a death sentence passed by a Sessions Judge cannot be executed unless the High Court confirms it. The High Court may confirm, reverse or modify the decision appealed from.

(ii) As a Court of Record its proceedings and judgements are recorded and preserved permanently, are admitted as evidence and cannot be questioned before any subordinate court, and are cited there as binding precedents. It also has the power to punish for contempt of itself.

(iii) Judicial Review is the power to examine whether a law made by the State Legislature or an order made by the executive is consistent with the Constitution, and to declare it null and void if it is not. It is exercised chiefly where a law is alleged to infringe a Fundamental Right, and it is the means by which the High Court holds the State Legislature and the State executive to the limits of the Constitution.

Official Answer

Q30. (i) It means that there is one hierarchy of courts for the whole country, administering both Union and State law. The Supreme Court stands at the apex, below it the High Court of each State, and below that the subordinate courts of each district. There are not, as in some federations, separate sets of federal and state courts trying separate classes of case: an appeal runs up the same ladder from the lowest court in a district to the Supreme Court, and the law declared by the Supreme Court binds every court in India.

(ii) The Court of the District Judge is the highest civil court in a district and tries civil suits — those concerning property, contracts, money and similar matters. The Sessions Court is the highest criminal court in the district and tries criminal cases; the Sessions Judge may pass any sentence authorised by law, including a sentence of death, but a death sentence must be confirmed by the High Court before it can be carried out. In practice the same officer holds both offices, sitting as District Judge on the civil side and as Sessions Judge on the criminal side.

(iii) Lok Adalats are 'People's Courts' constituted under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, in which disputes are settled by conciliation and mutual compromise, the award being final and binding with no appeal against it. They were established because ordinary litigation had become slow, expensive and technical, so that arrears mounted in the courts while poor litigants were effectively shut out of justice. By charging no fee, disposing of matters in a single sitting, and settling them by agreement rather than by a contested judgement, Lok Adalats give cheap and quick justice and at the same time relieve the regular courts of a great mass of small cases.

Q31. (i) Speed — a Lok Adalat disposed of at one sitting a dispute that the regular courts had failed to settle in fifty-three years, because it proceeds by conciliation instead of a full contested trial with its appeals and adjournments. (ii) It relieves the regular courts — a case that had been travelling through 'various courts' for half a century was taken off their file altogether, and since a Lok Adalat award is final and no appeal lies against it, the litigation ended there for good.

Official Answer

Q32. (i) That it works in the spirit of compromise. Instead of declaring one party the winner, it brought the couple to a settlement, and the relationship survived — an outcome a contested divorce decree could never have produced.
(ii) A Lok Adalat does not try the case and pronounce judgement. It is presided over by a serving or retired judicial officer, assisted by others of repute, who hear both sides and persuade them towards a mutually acceptable settlement; the terms they agree on are recorded as the award of the Lok Adalat. That award is deemed to be a decree of a civil court and is final and binding on both parties — no appeal lies against it, precisely because it rests on the parties' own consent.

Official Answer

Q33. (i) A Lok Adalat, or People's Court, established under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987.
(ii) Any two of: no court fee is payable, and any fee already paid on the petition is refunded if the matter is settled, whereas a civil suit must be stamped with a fee; the parties may appear and speak for themselves without engaging a lawyer, and the procedure is informal, with none of the strict rules of pleading and evidence that bind a civil court; the matter is decided by persuading the parties to a compromise rather than by a judgement imposed after trial; and the award is final and binding at once, with no right of appeal, whereas a civil decree may be appealed through several tiers of courts.
