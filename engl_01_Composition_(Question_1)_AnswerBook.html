Composition has no official mark scheme — only marking criteria for content, expression, grammar and organisation. The answers below are therefore MODEL answers throughout: a full worked composition where one is given, and a plan with a specimen opening where the point is method rather than a single correct text. Learn the approach from these, do not memorise them; a memorised composition is easily spotted and rarely fits the topic actually set.

A. Narrative — Short Story

Q1. FULL MODEL COMPOSITION (approx. 340 words)

It was midnight and the doorbell rang. I lay still for a moment, telling myself I had imagined it. Then it rang again, twice, with the flat insistence of someone who knows the house is occupied.

My grandmother had been asleep since nine. I went down the stairs slowly, one hand on the banister, and put my eye to the peephole. A woman stood on the landing in a wet raincoat, holding a cardboard box against her chest. She was perhaps sixty, and she was crying without making any noise at all, which frightened me more than shouting would have.

'I'm sorry,' she said through the door. 'I know how this looks. Does Mrs. Sarala Iyer live here?'

That is my grandmother's name. I did not open the door. 'Who are you?'

'My name is Fatima. I lived in 4B. Thirty years ago.' She shifted the box. 'I found these when we were clearing my mother's flat. They're letters. They're addressed to your grandmother.'

I heard the tap of a stick behind me. My grandmother had come down without a sound, as she always does, and she was staring at the door as though she could see through it.

'Open it,' she said.

I did. The two women looked at each other for a long time. Then my grandmother said, in a voice I had never heard her use, 'You have your mother's chin.'

They sat in the kitchen until three in the morning. I made tea nobody drank. The letters, it turned out, had been written by my grandmother to a friend she had quarrelled with in 1994 over something so small that neither of them could now remember what it was — and the friend had kept every one, unopened, in a biscuit tin, for thirty years.

'She never read them,' Fatima said. 'But she never threw them away either.'

My grandmother put her hand flat on the pile, the way you would touch someone's face.

'That,' she said, 'is the same thing as forgiveness. It only took a little longer.'

WHY THIS WORKS — the opening line is given, so the marks lie in what you build from it. Note that the story has one incident, three characters and a single location; it resists the temptation to make the midnight caller a criminal; and the last line turns the plot into a point without stating a moral. A story that ends 'and then I woke up' throws away twenty marks.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q2. PLAN

Decide first who the visitor is and why the visit matters — the title is a promise, and the story must deliver something genuinely strange, not merely unexpected. Three workable directions: (a) a stranger who turns out to know something about your family that you did not; (b) an animal that gets into the house and disrupts a tense household into laughing together; (c) a visitor whose strangeness is ordinary to them and only strange to you — a relative from a very different life.

Structure: open in the middle of the ordinary evening the visit interrupts (2-3 sentences of normality); the arrival; the mounting oddity, revealed through what the visitor says and does rather than through your narrator's comments; the turn — the moment the strangeness is explained or transformed; a short close.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'My mother was counting the spoons for the third time when the gate creaked. She had counted them twice already that evening, which was how we knew she was nervous about something she was not going to tell us.'

WATCH: keep to one visitor and one evening. Ghosts and aliens are permitted but very hard to make convincing in 350 words; examiners see hundreds of them.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q3. PLAN

The title requires a reversal — something the narrator confidently believed turns out to be false. The shock must be earned: the reader has to have been allowed to believe the same thing.

A reliable shape: (i) establish the false belief as though it were simply a fact — the narrator's certainty is the trap; (ii) two or three small details that will only make sense afterwards; (iii) the moment of discovery, told plainly and without exclamation marks; (iv) the narrator's adjustment, which is where the marks for maturity lie.

Subjects that work: discovering a friend's kindness was calculated; discovering that a person you had dismissed had been quietly helping you; results day; realising a parent has been concealing a difficulty.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'I had known Ritwik since Class Three, and there were exactly two things I was sure of: that he could not swim, and that he never lied to me. By the end of that afternoon I had lost both certainties.'

WATCH: the shock should change the narrator, not just startle them. A story where nothing is different at the end has described an event rather than told a story.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q4. PLAN

The elements are prescribed — a poor tailor, an award, a changed life — so the marks go to the specificity with which you imagine them, not to the plot.

Make the tailor particular before the award arrives: what he mends, whose clothes, the shop's smell, the one sewing machine, the customer who never pays. Then the award: better if it is small and local than a national prize, because a modest prize can plausibly change a life while a fortune only ends the story.

The important choice is what 'changes his life' means. The obvious answer is money. The better answer is that being seen changes him — customers, a daughter, or the tailor himself now look at his work differently. Consider ending on a small, concrete change rather than a large abstract one.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'Ismail Bhai had been letting out the same three school uniforms every July for eleven years, watching the same three boys grow, and he could have told you which of them would be tall before their mothers knew.'

WATCH: avoid the rags-to-riches summary ending ('and soon he was famous all over India'). Twenty marks are for one scene done well, not for a life story compressed.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q5. PLAN

Two requirements: the misunderstanding must be believable, and the friendship must be unexpected — so the two people should be ones the reader would not expect to become friends (different ages, rival teams, a neighbour long disliked, a person from a different background).

Structure: (i) the misunderstanding, shown as it happens rather than explained — perhaps the narrator overhears half a conversation, or is blamed for something; (ii) the hostility that follows, with one or two sharp exchanges; (iii) the accidental circumstance that forces them together; (iv) the clearing-up, which should come out sideways in conversation rather than as a formal explanation; (v) a brief close showing the new footing.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'For four months I believed that Mrs. D'Souza on the ground floor had complained to my father about my cycling in the compound. For four months I did not say good morning to her. Both of these facts turned out to be my own invention.'

WATCH: dialogue is where this topic earns marks — a misunderstanding is made of things said and heard. Use it, and punctuate it correctly.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q6. PLAN

The title invites the obvious reading — a lost object recovered — and rewards the less obvious one: a person lost and found, a lost temper, a lost year, confidence lost and regained. Either is acceptable, but the second is likelier to stand out.

If you take the literal reading, make the object matter: a thing is only interesting in a story because of who gave it or what it proves. If you take the figurative reading, anchor it in one concrete incident so it does not become an essay about feelings.

Structure for the figurative version: the state of being 'lost' shown through one small failure; the low point; the accidental encounter or task that begins the recovery; the finding, understated.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'The watch was not valuable. That is what everyone kept telling me, as though the price of a thing had anything to do with it.'

WATCH: whichever reading you take, the title must be earned by the story — do not simply mention the words at the end.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q7. PLAN

A tension story, and the commonest mistake is to make the danger enormous — a bomb, a shootout — which almost always reads as unconvincing. A near-miss at a level crossing, a rip current at a beach, a wall that collapses ten seconds after you move, a fire in a kitchen: small, plausible dangers are far more frightening on the page.

Structure: (i) ordinary circumstances, with one detail that will matter later; (ii) the decision or accident that creates the danger; (iii) the escape itself, told in short sentences — this is where sentence length does the work, because short sentences read fast; (iv) the aftermath, which should be quiet, because the reaction usually comes afterwards.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'The gatekeeper had gone for his tea. I remember thinking that was lucky, because it meant nobody would stop us crossing.'

WATCH: do not narrate the escape in slow motion for two hundred words. The proportion should be roughly one-third setup, one-third danger, one-third aftermath.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q8. PLAN

All three characters must be main characters — a story in which the police officer appears for one line in the last paragraph does not answer the question.

Give each a want: the woman wants something explained; the neighbour wants to be left alone; the officer wants the matter closed with the least trouble. Conflict comes free once the three wants are incompatible.

A workable plot: the woman reports her neighbour for something (noise at night, a light, a smell); the officer investigates half-heartedly; the explanation turns out to be sad rather than criminal — the neighbour is caring for someone, or working at night to pay a debt — and the woman is left with her own suspicion to answer for.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'Constable Salunke had been to the building twice already that month, and both times about the same flat, and both times he had left convinced there was nothing in it.'

WATCH: 'strange' should mean unexplained, not sinister. Resisting the crime-story ending is what will distinguish this from the majority of scripts.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q9. PLAN

The opening is given and must be reproduced exactly. Note that it commits you to three things — night, rain, and a black car whose presence matters — so all three must be used.

The sentence stops at 'when', so your first act of writing is to complete it, and that completion sets the whole story. Complete it with something small and concrete ('…the front door was already open', '…my mother's slippers were not on the step') rather than something melodramatic.

Structure: complete the sentence; establish what is wrong; the investigation or confrontation; the resolution, in which the black car must be accounted for.

WATCH: this was a 25-mark composition in the older format (350-400 words); current papers set 20 marks for 300-350 words. Adjust length to the paper in front of you.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q10. PLAN

The story must demonstrate the statement, not state it. The structure is given by the topic itself: a small lie, told for a good enough reason; a second lie needed to protect the first; a third; the collapse.

Make the first lie sympathetic — a lie to avoid hurting someone, or to cover a small failure — so that the reader goes along with it. The comedy or the pain both come from the escalation, so give each lie its own short paragraph and let them get less defensible each time.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'I told my grandmother I had eaten. That was the first one, and I still do not think it was a bad thing to say.'

WATCH: do not append a moral sentence at the end ('So I learnt that we should never tell lies'). The plot has already made the point; saying it again suggests you do not trust your own story.

B. Descriptive

Q11. FULL MODEL COMPOSITION (approx. 330 words)

The stall has no name. It has a number — 14 — painted on the shutter of the shop behind it, and for as long as I have been alive my father has called it 'fourteen', as in 'shall we go to fourteen?', and everyone in our house knows what he means.

It stands at the mouth of the lane behind the vegetable market, and you smell it before you see it: hot oil first, then something sharper underneath, the ajwain that Salim Chacha throws into the batter by the handful without ever appearing to measure. The griddle runs the whole length of the cart, black and slightly concave in the middle from thirty years of the same scraper. In the evening it throws up a wall of steam that catches the tube light above it, so that the whole stall seems to be standing inside a small weather system of its own.

There are no chairs. You eat standing, off a steel plate, at a ledge that runs along the wall, and the wall is warm because the bakery on the other side of it has its oven there. In winter people press their backs against that wall while they wait.

The sounds are as fixed as a liturgy. The scrape of the spatula. The hiss when the batter goes down. Salim Chacha shouting an order to his nephew without turning his head, and the nephew answering before it is finished.

What brings us back is not really the food, although the food is very good. It is that at fourteen my father becomes a different man. In an office he is careful and quiet. At that ledge, with a plate in one hand, he argues about cricket with a man whose name he has never asked, and he laughs with his mouth open, and he lets me order a second plate without saying anything about it.

That is why it is my favourite. A restaurant feeds you. Fourteen gives me my father for forty minutes.

WHY THIS WORKS — the description is built from the five senses in sequence (smell, sight, touch, sound), it uses exact detail rather than adjectives ('concave from thirty years of the same scraper' does more than 'very old'), and the final section answers the third part of the question, which many candidates forget to do at all. Read the topic to the end: it asked for sights, smells AND why you like eating there.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q12. PLAN

Two parts, both compulsory: the description, and the reflection on why solitude matters. Give roughly three-quarters to the first and one-quarter to the second — but do not omit the second, which is where the better candidates separate themselves.

For the description, choose one afternoon and stay inside it. The clock is your structure: what the house sounds like at two, at three, at four as the light changes. Concrete detail — the fan, the particular quality of afternoon silence, the sounds from other flats, what you actually do rather than what you ought to do.

For the reflection, avoid generalities about 'the stress of modern life'. Say what solitude does for you specifically: it lets thoughts finish; it is the only time nobody is asking you a question; it is where you notice things.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'By two o'clock the house has emptied of noise the way a tide goes out, leaving small sounds behind that you never hear at any other hour.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q13. PLAN

The question hands you your structure: before the bell, and after. Use it — two clearly distinct movements, with the bell as the hinge.

First movement: break time. Move through the space rather than listing it — start at your classroom door and walk, so the description has a route. Sight and sound are named in the question, so make sure both are strongly present: the canteen queue, the ground, the corridor where the younger children run, the particular pitch of four hundred voices in a stairwell.

Second movement: the change. This is where marks are won, because most candidates rush it. Show the sequence — the bell, the two seconds before anyone reacts, the surge, then the emptiness, and then what a corridor sounds like when it is empty but for one late boy running.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'The bell for break does not so much ring as detonate.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q14. PLAN

'As the day progressed' asks for chronological structure — morning, midday, evening, night — and the examiner will look for that shape.

Choose an event with texture: a wedding, a housewarming, a grandparent's eightieth birthday, a festival. Resist summarising the event; describe four or five moments from it.

A useful discipline: give each stage of the day one dominant sense. Early morning — sound (the first vessels in the kitchen). Late morning — movement and colour. Afternoon — heat and slowness. Evening — light and food. Night — quiet, and one person left tidying.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'The cooking started at four in the morning, which meant that the first thing I heard on the most important day of my grandmother's life was the sound of somebody quietly trying not to wake anybody.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q15. PLAN

Note 'how it was different' — the question requires a contrast, so establish what an ordinary evening in your house is like before describing this one. Without the contrast, the composition has only answered two-thirds of the question.

Structure: the usual evening in brief (noise, television, homework, people coming and going); the circumstance that made this one different (a power cut, everyone out, an illness, rain); the quiet evening itself in detail; what it gave you.

A power cut is the most-used version of this topic and can still be done well if the detail is specific — but consider a fresher circumstance.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'Our house has never been quiet. There are five of us and one television, and my grandfather is deaf in one ear, which settles the volume question for everybody.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q16. PLAN

'Each day' and 'familiar' are the key words: this is a description of a routine, not of one particular journey, so use the habitual present tense ('the bus turns', 'the same man is always') rather than the past.

Structure by the route itself — the school gate, the main road, the level crossing, the market, your stop. Four or five fixed points, each with one or two exact observations: the dog that chases the bus to the same corner and no further; the woman selling flowers who has finished by the time you pass; the smell as you cross the fish market.

Sound is explicitly required — the horn, the rattle of the window frame in its rubber, forty children, the conductor's whistle.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'Our bus takes forty minutes to cover six kilometres, and in that time I could tell you, with my eyes shut, exactly where we are.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q17. PLAN

A pure description with no narrative, which makes it harder, not easier: with nothing happening, only the quality of the observation earns marks.

Give yourself a route and a time of day — the same garden at six in the morning and at six in the evening is two different places, and choosing one lets you be specific about light. Move through the garden rather than cataloguing it.

All three senses named in the question must appear. Scent is the one most often forgotten: wet earth, cut grass, a particular flower, the smell of the tea stall at the gate.

Avoid the register of 'nature's beauty' and 'a feast for the eyes'. One precisely observed thing — the way a gardener's hose makes a small rainbow, the sound of a badminton shuttle from beyond the hedge — is worth a paragraph of adjectives.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'At half past six the light comes in almost horizontally through the gulmohars, so that the path is striped and the dust in the air is visible.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q18. PLAN

Three parts: describe, detail the sights and sounds, and say what you especially like. All three must be answered.

The risk with 'my locality' is a list of amenities — 'there is a park, a school, a temple and two shops'. Instead choose a walk and take the reader on it, and let the buildings appear as you pass them.

People are what make a locality; give two or three of them a sentence each — the ironing man under the tree, the watchman who reads the newspaper aloud, the children who play cricket with the compound wall as the boundary.

For the last part, be specific and personal. 'It is peaceful' says nothing. 'Nobody here locks their doors during the day, and I did not know that was unusual until I visited my cousin in Bangalore' says a great deal.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'You could walk from one end of our colony to the other in eleven minutes, and be stopped four times.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q19. PLAN

A hybrid: description first, then narrative. Both halves are required and the question gives their order, so follow it.

Part one — the scene at the closed school: the gate, the notice or the watchman, the other children arriving and being turned back, water, traffic, the particular sound rain makes on a tin awning.

Part two — the journey home and the rest of the day. This is the narrative half and needs at least one incident: a road under water, a stranded auto, helping someone, an unexpected long walk with a classmate you barely knew.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'The gate was chained, which I had never seen before, and about thirty of us stood outside it in the rain as though the chain might be a mistake that would shortly be corrected.'

C. Argumentative — Express your views for or against

Q20. PLAN

The instruction is 'either for or against' — take a side and hold it. A composition that concludes 'thus we see there are advantages and disadvantages' has refused the question and will be marked accordingly.

Note the exact wording: 'a necessary evil' already concedes that homework is unpleasant, so arguing that it is unpleasant is not an argument against the statement — it is agreement with it. Arguing against the statement means arguing either that homework is not necessary, or that it is not an evil.

Structure (four-paragraph model): (1) state your position clearly in the first two sentences; (2) your strongest argument, with a concrete example; (3) your second argument; (4) the best argument on the other side, honestly stated — and then answered. Close by restating the position in different words.

FOR (necessary but unpleasant): practice is how skill is built and cannot happen only in class; it teaches independent work and time management; the alternative — learning only under supervision — produces students who cannot work alone.

AGAINST: the volume set is unrelated to how much practice a subject needs; it transfers teaching to homes that are unequally equipped to provide it, so it widens the gap between children; and it costs the sleep and free time on which learning itself depends.

WATCH: the third-paragraph concession, properly answered, is the single most reliable way to lift an argumentative composition into the top band.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q21. PLAN

Define your terms in the first paragraph — 'banned' where, and for whom? Banned in school canteens is a very different proposition from banned nationally, and a composition that does not say which it is arguing about will drift.

FOR: the health evidence on sugar, salt and trans fats in children; children cannot be expected to make informed dietary choices against advertising designed by professionals; we already ban the sale of tobacco to minors on exactly this reasoning, so the principle is established.

AGAINST: prohibition does not work and creates black markets; the real problem is availability, price and advertising, which regulation and taxation address better than bans; a ban punishes the street vendor and not the corporation; and personal choice in food is not the state's business once a person is informed.

Strongest structure: pick the narrow claim you can actually defend — 'junk food should be banned from school premises' — and defend it thoroughly, rather than the broad one you cannot.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'Before we can argue about banning junk food, we have to say who is doing the banning and to whom, because those two questions decide the whole case.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q22. PLAN

The word to attack or defend is 'most'. Nobody will argue that honesty is unimportant, so a composition that spends 350 words proving honesty is good has argued against nobody.

FOR: every other virtue depends on it — courage, loyalty and generosity are worthless if you cannot believe what the person says; trust is the foundation of family, contract, medicine and law alike, and it is built only from honesty repeated.

AGAINST: compassion has the better claim, since honesty without it becomes cruelty ('I was only being honest' is the commonest defence of unkindness); most moral traditions permit the lie that protects a life; and a person who is honest but indifferent to suffering is not admirable.

Either side can score full marks. What distinguishes the answer is whether you engage with 'most' — that is, whether you compare honesty with a named rival virtue.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'I want to argue against this statement, and I should say at once that I am not arguing that honesty does not matter. I am arguing that something else matters more.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q23. PLAN

FOR: the problems that now matter most — climate, disease, poverty — cannot be solved by any country or laboratory alone, and the vaccine effort of 2020-21 is the clearest recent proof; competition inside schools produces anxiety and rote learning rather than understanding; and children who are ranked against each other learn to conceal what they know.

AGAINST: competition is what drives improvement, in sport, science and industry alike; collaboration without it tends to settle at the level of the least willing member, as anyone who has done a group project knows; and the two are not opposites — the best teams compete with other teams, so what is wanted is competition between groups and collaboration within them.

That last point is the sophisticated move and, well made, will lift the composition considerably.

WATCH: 'the need of the hour' is a phrase examiners see in every second script. Do not use it anywhere except in the quoted statement itself.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q24. PLAN

Note 'as important as' — this is a comparison of weight, not a question of whether co-curricular activities have value.

FOR: they teach what classrooms structurally cannot — teamwork, losing, performing under an audience's eyes, sustained practice at something one is not immediately good at; a great deal of what employers and universities describe as ability is learnt on a field or a stage; and they are, for many students, the only part of school where they experience competence.

AGAINST: they are important but not equally so, because literacy and numeracy are the foundation everything else stands on; time is finite, and a school that gives equal weight to both must take hours from the subjects that determine a student's options; and the argument is often used to justify demanding both at full measure, which simply exhausts children.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'I have spent four years in the school choir and I would defend every hour of it, which is why I want to argue that the statement, as written, is not quite true.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q25. PLAN

FOR: a voter who cannot read cannot check what a candidate claims, and is therefore governed by whoever controls what he hears; democracy requires citizens to judge arguments, which is a trained skill; and the framers of the Indian Constitution made free and compulsory education a directive principle for exactly this reason.

AGAINST: this is the argument that has been used throughout history to withhold the vote — from the poor, from women, from colonised peoples — always on the ground that they were not yet ready; India adopted universal adult franchise in 1950 at very low literacy and its democracy survived, which is the strongest single piece of evidence available; and political judgement is not the same thing as schooling — voters without formal education have repeatedly removed governments that educated commentators expected to win.

The Indian example is decisive evidence and belongs in either version of the answer — as an objection to be met if you argue FOR, and as your main proof if you argue AGAINST.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q26. PLAN

The interest of this topic is that the phrase 'peer pressure' is almost always used negatively, so arguing FOR requires you to recover a fair meaning of the words: the influence of the people around us on what we do.

FOR: the same mechanism that spreads bad habits spreads good ones — students work harder in a class where working hard is normal; friends stop each other doing dangerous things far more often than they push each other into them; and a group's expectations are how a person learns to keep promises before they have the character to keep them unaided.

AGAINST: pressure by definition overrides judgement, and a good action done under pressure builds no character; it falls hardest on the least secure; and what is called good peer pressure is usually just friendship, which works by example rather than by pressure.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'We use the phrase only when we disapprove of the result. Nobody says a boy took up the violin because of peer pressure — but that is exactly what happened to me.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q27. PLAN

FOR: freedom from real responsibility; friendships formed before people learn to be guarded; and the fact that everything is being encountered for the first time, which is a kind of happiness not available later.

AGAINST: it is a claim made by adults about a period they are remembering rather than living; school contains real unhappiness — examinations, exclusion, bullying — which the saying dismisses; and telling a struggling fifteen-year-old that these are the happiest days of his life is a cruelty disguised as comfort.

The AGAINST case is the more interesting and less often attempted. If you take it, be careful not to slide into self-pity; argue about the claim, not about your own timetable.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'This is said to children almost exclusively by people who are no longer children, which ought to make us suspicious of it.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q28. PLAN

Avoid the two clichés this topic attracts: 'money cannot buy happiness' and 'money is the root of all evil'. Both are assertions, not arguments, and examiners have read them several hundred times.

FOR: the absence of money is a reliable source of misery — illness untreated, education abandoned, the daily anxiety of rent; it buys the thing happiness most depends on, which is security; and the people who say money does not matter almost always have enough of it.

AGAINST: above the level at which needs are met, more money adds very little, which is why the comparison that matters is with one's neighbours rather than with one's own past; and pursuing it costs the time and relationships that happiness is actually made of.

The strongest version of either side concedes the other's territory: money matters enormously up to a point and very little after it. Decide which half of that sentence you are defending, and say so.

D. Expository / Reflective — Account of an experience

Q29. PLAN

Three questions are asked and all three must be answered: which period, why it needs changing, and what replaces it. Number them mentally and check at the end that each has a paragraph.

A warning about tone: an answer that simply attacks a subject ('Mathematics is useless and boring') will lose marks for immaturity. The stronger approach is to accept the subject's value and argue about placement, method or proportion — the last period of the day is the wrong hour for a subject requiring concentration; six periods a week of one subject and none of another is a question of balance, not of worth.

The replacement should be defended as seriously as the removal. Good candidates for replacement: a period of physical activity; a life-skills or personal finance period; supervised reading; a second language; a structured discussion period.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'I would not remove Mathematics. I would move it. At the moment it is the eighth period on Wednesdays, which is to say that it is taught to a room of people who are no longer capable of learning anything.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q30. PLAN

Three required elements — the task, the carrying out, the feelings at the end — and the third is the one most often skimped.

Choose a task that was difficult in an interesting way. Being sent to buy vegetables is difficult only if something went wrong; being left in charge of a younger sibling, or asked to speak to a relative about a family matter, or trusted with money, carries its own tension.

The best answers make the difficulty partly internal: the task was hard because it required you to do something you were afraid of, or to be trusted in a way you were not sure you deserved.

For the ending, avoid 'I felt very proud and happy'. Try for something more exact — relief that arrived before pride; the discovery that your parents had been more nervous than you were; the odd flatness that sometimes follows a thing you have been dreading.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'My father gave me the keys and the envelope and said, "Count it in front of him, not afterwards," and then he went inside, and that was the whole of my instruction.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q31. PLAN

Note 'for both of you'. Many candidates write only about what the elderly person gains, which is half an answer and also slightly condescending — as though the visit were charity. Say honestly what you get from it: stories, an adult who is not assessing you, quiet, a different account of the city you live in.

Make the person specific and give them a personality with some edge — they need not be a saintly figure dispensing wisdom. Someone who is funny, or blunt, or a little difficult, is far more convincing.

'A detailed account of how you spend the time' asks for the ordinary shape of a visit: what you do first, the tea, the same photograph, the argument you always have.

SPECIMEN OPENING
'Mrs. Kurien does not want company and says so at the door, every time, and then she puts the kettle on.'

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q32. PLAN

The question asks for a change in outlook, so the composition must end somewhere different from where it started.

Be honest about the feeling in the middle. A candidate who writes that he gave up the thing gladly and felt only joy has written something nobody believes. Resentment, followed by something more complicated, is both truer and better rewarded.

The sacrifice should be proportionate: a place in a team given to someone who needed it more, a room, a long-planned trip abandoned for a family illness, money saved for something of your own.

Structure: what the thing meant to you (this must be established or the sacrifice has no weight); the circumstance; the decision and what it cost; the alteration in outlook, stated with restraint.

'I had wanted the corner room for six years, which is a long time to want a room.'

The word 'harmless' is doing work: the prank must not have hurt anyone, so the humour has to come from the situation and from your own reaction rather than from someone's humiliation.

Three required parts: what happened, how you responded, what you learnt.

The most interesting versions are those in which the narrator's response is the real subject — you over-reacted, or took it badly, or pretended not to mind and did mind. What is learnt can then be about yourself rather than about friendship in general.

Comic writing rewards precision and restraint. Understating the moment of discovery is almost always funnier than exclaiming about it.

Q34. PLAN

Two parts: what you did, and what you learnt. Give real weight to the first — the practical detail of running a house is what makes this composition convincing, and most candidates skate over it into sentiment.

So: the specific tasks. The cooking that did not work. The realisation that the milk has to be ordered by someone. The younger sibling who has to be taken to school. The medicines and their times, written on a piece of paper.

What is learnt should follow from that detail rather than being announced: the discovery of how much invisible work was being done for you every day is a better ending than 'I learnt that we should always respect our mother'.

Q35. PLAN

The question specifies 'when it was hard', so the offence must be real. A composition about forgiving someone for borrowing a pen has not met the terms.

Three parts: what helped you forgive, the positive impact, what you learnt.

'What helped you' is the most interesting and least attempted: was it time, or an explanation, or seeing the person diminished, or someone else's advice, or simply exhaustion at carrying the grievance? Be specific — this is where an ordinary answer becomes a good one.

Be careful not to make yourself the hero. The best versions admit that forgiveness was partly self-interested: you wanted the friendship back, or you were tired of the feeling.

A straightforward narrative-plus-reflection. The trouble should be one a school student could plausibly face and could plausibly help with: a friend falling behind after an illness, a friend being excluded, a lost or damaged possession, trouble at home that you noticed before anyone else.

E. Picture-Based Composition

Q37. PLAN — HOW TO ANSWER ANY PICTURE-BASED COMPOSITION

This question appears in every paper, always in the same words, so the method can be learnt once.

1. Read the instruction exactly. You may write a story, a description, or an account, and you may take suggestions from the picture rather than describing it. The only firm requirement is a clear connection. Candidates lose marks not for straying from the picture but for having no visible link to it at all.

2. Decide which of the three forms suits what you see. A picture with people mid-action suggests a story. A landscape or a market suggests a description. A picture that carries an idea — an old man alone, a flooded street — suggests a reflective account.

3. Do not spend your first paragraph describing the photograph. The commonest weak answer opens 'In this picture we can see a man standing near a tree.' Enter the scene instead, and let the details of the picture appear inside the writing.

4. Take one element and build on it rather than accounting for everything in the frame. A single figure in the corner is enough for an entire story.

5. Make the connection unmistakable at least twice — early and late — so the examiner never has to look for it.

SPECIMEN OPENING (for a picture of a crowded railway platform)
'The 6.40 was late again, and the platform had reached the density at which strangers stop apologising for touching each other.'

WATCH: this option is chosen by students who could not think of anything for (i) to (iv), and it shows. Choose it because the picture has genuinely given you something, not as a retreat.
