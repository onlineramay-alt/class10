B. Character Sketches

Q3. Brutus is the protagonist and the moral centre of the play, and Shakespeare makes him admirable and mistaken in equal measure.

His honour is genuine and is acknowledged by everyone, including his enemies. He joins the conspiracy for no personal reason at all — 'I know no personal cause to spurn at him, / But for the general' — and refuses to swear an oath because he holds that the cause itself binds honest men. He will not raise money by vile means. Antony, who destroyed him, ends the play by separating him from every other conspirator on precisely this ground: all the rest acted in envy, 'He only, in a general honest thought / And common good to all, made one of them.'

He is also affectionate. He loves Caesar and says so; his tenderness towards the sleeping boy Lucius, whose instrument he takes gently from his hands, is one of the quietest moments in the play; and his relationship with Portia is one of real intimacy, however much the conspiracy strains it.

His weaknesses all grow from the same root. He judges other men by himself, and is therefore consistently wrong about them: he trusts Antony's promise, he assumes the crowd will follow reasons, he takes forged letters for the voice of Rome. He is stubborn in exactly the places where he should listen — he overrules Cassius on the oath, on Antony, on the funeral and on Philippi, and Cassius is right every time. And there is a streak of self-righteousness in him, most visible in the quarrel scene, where he condemns Cassius for an itching palm and then asks him for the money.

The honesty of his own mind is what saves him from being merely a prig. In the orchard soliloquy he admits that his case against Caesar has no basis in anything Caesar has done, that it 'will bear no colour for the thing he is', and that the quarrel must therefore be 'fashion'd'. He knows what he is doing, and does it anyway, for a principle. That is what makes him a tragic figure rather than a villain or a fool — and it is why Shakespeare gives him, not Caesar, the last death and the final tribute.

Q4. Cassius is the practical intelligence of the conspiracy and its least attractive member, and the play grows steadily kinder to him.

He is shrewd above every other character. His reading of men is exact: he knows Brutus can be moved only through honour, he knows Antony is dangerous, he knows the funeral oration is a catastrophe waiting to happen, and he knows the army should wait rather than march. On every question of judgement in the play, Cassius is right.

He is also envious, and Shakespeare establishes this before anything else. The stories he tells Brutus — Caesar sinking in the Tiber, Caesar shaking with fever in Spain — prove nothing about tyranny and everything about the teller: what he cannot bear is that a man no stronger than himself should have become a god. Caesar's judgement of him is faultless: he reads much, observes, sees through the deeds of men, and seldom smiles.

He is unscrupulous where Brutus is not. He forges the letters that decide Brutus; he admits in soliloquy that he is seducing a nobler man; and Brutus's charge in the quarrel scene — that his officers sell offices for gold — is never actually denied, only resented.

And yet he is the more human of the two, and the play knows it. His anger in the quarrel is the anger of a man who cannot endure being thought badly of by someone he loves, and he ends it by baring his breast and offering his dagger. He is silenced completely by the news of Portia's death. He yields on Philippi against his own certain judgement because he cannot go on opposing his friend — and that yielding kills him.

His death is the most pathetic in the play: he dies on a false report, on the sword that killed Caesar, held by a slave whose freedom he is buying with the act, having sent his best friend to a capture that never happened. 'This day I breathed first: time is come round, / And where I did begin, there shall I end.' Brutus's epitaph on him — 'The last of all the Romans' — is more generous than anything Cassius said about anyone, and the play appears to endorse it.

Q5. Antony is the most effective man in the play and the hardest to like, and Shakespeare shows us his development in three distinct stages.

Before the assassination he is barely there — a runner in the Lupercal games, a man given to sports, wildness and much company, whom Brutus dismisses as 'but a limb of Caesar'. That underestimation is the play's most expensive mistake, and Shakespeare arranges it deliberately so that Antony's emergence is a shock.

In Act III he reveals himself as a tactician of extraordinary nerve. He sends a servant ahead with a message of complete submission, comes in person to a room of armed murderers, grieves openly, offers them his own life, shakes every bloody hand, and asks for one small thing — permission to speak at the funeral. Every word is calculated, and the moment he is alone he shows what he really feels: 'O, pardon me, thou bleeding piece of earth, / That I am meek and gentle with these butchers!' The funeral oration is the finest piece of persuasion in Shakespeare, and it keeps every promise Brutus extracted while destroying him.

In Act IV a colder man appears. He agrees to his own nephew's death by a stroke of the pen, plans to cut down the legacies in the will he had used so movingly, and describes Lepidus in his absence as a horse to be fed, trained and turned off. Whatever grief was genuine in Act III has been absorbed into the business of ruling.

His love for Caesar seems real; his use of it is not scrupulous. He is a man who can feel deeply and calculate simultaneously, and the combination is what makes him formidable.

And yet the last speech in the play is his, and it is generous. Standing over the body of the man who killed his friend, he distinguishes Brutus from every other conspirator and grants him a nobility he need not have acknowledged in victory. It is the one moment when Antony gains nothing by what he says — and it is the strongest evidence in the play that he is more than a politician.

Q6. Caesar appears alive in only three scenes and dies halfway through the play, yet he dominates it, and Shakespeare's portrait is deliberately double.

His greatness is not in doubt. He has conquered; Rome fills the streets for him; Antony says that when Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed. His judgement is the sharpest in the play — his analysis of Cassius in Act I is exactly right and is confirmed by everything that follows. He refuses a crown three times in public. And he can speak nobly and mean it: 'Cowards die many times before their deaths; / The valiant never taste of death but once.'

His weaknesses are physical and, more damagingly, temperamental. He is deaf in one ear, subject to the falling sickness, and was once carried out of the Tiber by Cassius. Far more serious is his imprisonment inside his own public image. He habitually speaks of himself in the third person, as 'Caesar', as though the man and the name were separate beings — and the name cannot be seen to fear. So he identifies Cassius as dangerous and does nothing; he yields to Calpurnia in private and reverses himself the instant Decius hints that staying home will look cowardly; he refuses Artemidorus's paper with 'What touches us ourself shall be last served' because a great man must appear selfless. Each of these is a decision taken to protect an image, and each brings him closer to the daggers.

His last speech is the summary of him — the claim to be constant as the northern star, superb and true to his sense of himself, delivered to men kneeling in front of him only to get within reach. Two lines later he is dead.

Whether he would have become a tyrant the play never says, and that silence is deliberate: Brutus kills him for what he might become, and Shakespeare refuses to settle whether Brutus was right. What is certain is that Caesar dead is stronger than Caesar living, as Brutus himself admits — 'O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet!'

C. Short Answer Type

Q7. Three reasons are usually given, and all three are good.

First, Caesar is the cause of everything. Every action in the play is done because of him, for him or against him, from the tribunes tearing scarves off his images in the opening scene to Octavius avenging his three-and-thirty wounds in the last act.

Second, he dominates the play after his death more completely than before it. His spirit walks in Brutus's tent; Cassius dies on the sword that killed him with 'Caesar, thou art revenged'; Brutus dies with 'Caesar, now be still'. Brutus states the theme himself: 'O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet! / Thy spirit walks abroad and turns our swords / In our own proper entrails.'

Third, the play is about Caesarism rather than about Caesar — about whether one man should hold that much power, and what happens to a republic that decides the question with knives. The title names the issue, not the protagonist.

It is nevertheless fair to say, as many critics have, that 'The Tragedy of Marcus Brutus' would describe the play's structure more accurately.

Q8. They are constant, they are accurate, and they are always ignored — which is their point.

The soothsayer's 'Beware the ides of March' is exact. Calpurnia's dream of the statue running blood, in which many Romans bathe their hands, is a literal description of what happens. The storm in Act I Scene iii, the lioness whelping in the streets, the fiery warriors in the clouds, the beast without a heart, the birds of prey over the army at Philippi, and the Ghost of Caesar in Brutus's tent all point one way, and all prove right.

Shakespeare uses them for atmosphere and for structure, but chiefly for character. Each person interprets the signs according to what he already is. Casca is terrified. Cicero observes shrewdly that men construe things after their fashion, clean from the purpose of the things themselves. Cassius, who denies that the stars govern anything, cheerfully uses the storm as an argument. Decius reinterprets Calpurnia's dream to serve the conspiracy. Caesar dismisses the soothsayer as a dreamer.

The play therefore leaves the metaphysics open. The omens may be the gods speaking, or they may be weather; what is certain is that men hear in them what they have already decided. The prophecy comes true not in spite of human choice but through it — Caesar dies on the ides of March because he chooses to go to the Capitol.

Q9. This is a personal-response question, and the mark scheme for the paper accepts either answer provided it is argued from the text. What follows is one defensible position; the opposite position, equally well supported, would score as well.

I sympathise chiefly with Brutus, and not with the conspiracy.

The conspiracy as a whole deserves little sympathy. Cassius's motive is envy, admitted in his own soliloquy; the letters that recruit Brutus are forged; and the men who killed to prevent one man ruling Rome are, by Act IV, quarrelling over bribes and selling offices to undeservers. Their case against Caesar rests on nothing he has done — Brutus concedes as much — but on what he might become, which would justify almost any murder.

Caesar himself is harder. He is arrogant, superstitious, and so bound to his own image that he cannot take an obvious precaution; and the play never disproves the fear that he would have taken the crown. But he is killed by men who owe him their positions, on his way to the Senate, by thirty-three wounds, having done nothing to deserve them, and his death is a personal betrayal as much as a political act — 'Et tu, Brute?' asks the only question that matters.

Where sympathy settles is on Brutus, because he is the only person in the play who acts against his own interest and his own affection for a public reason, and because he is honest with himself about the weakness of his case. He is wrong from beginning to end, and he is not contemptible for a moment. Antony's verdict is the right one, and it is the play's.

D. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q10. The conflict is stated in Brutus's first long speech and is worked out through every relationship in the play.

Brutus loves Caesar and kills him. He says so in the forum with complete sincerity — 'Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more' — and the orchard soliloquy shows him searching for a public reason to do what no private feeling supports. The same choice costs him his marriage: he cannot tell Portia, and her exclusion from his mind is what breaks her.

Caesar faces a smaller version of it and fails the other way round. He would stay at home for Calpurnia, and cannot, because the public figure must not be seen to be governed by a private fear.

Portia's plea is the clearest statement of the private claim: if a wife may not share her husband's mind, she dwells 'but in the suburbs' of his good pleasure and is his harlot, not his wife. She is answered with a promise that the knocking at the gate prevents him from keeping.

The play's judgement is severe on the public claim. Brutus sacrifices friendship, marriage and finally his own peace to a principle, and the principle produces a civil war, mob murder, proscriptions and the empire he meant to prevent. Meanwhile it is private affection that produces the play's finest moments — Brutus with the sleeping Lucius, the reconciliation with Cassius, Lucilius risking death to impersonate his general, Strato turning away his face.

Shakespeare does not conclude that public duty is worthless. He shows that a man who subordinates everything to it may be admirable and may still destroy everything he touches, and that the cost is paid by the people who love him.
