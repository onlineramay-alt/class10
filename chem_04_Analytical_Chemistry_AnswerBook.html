B. Short Answer Type

Q4. WITH SODIUM HYDROXIDE SOLUTION:

(a) Ca²⁺ — WHITE precipitate, INSOLUBLE in excess. (Sparingly soluble, so the precipitate may be faint.)
(b) Fe²⁺ — DIRTY GREEN precipitate, insoluble in excess; turns reddish-brown on standing in air.
(c) Fe³⁺ — REDDISH-BROWN precipitate, insoluble in excess.
(d) Cu²⁺ — PALE BLUE precipitate, insoluble in excess.
(e) Zn²⁺ — WHITE precipitate, SOLUBLE in excess, giving a colourless solution (sodium zincate).
(f) Pb²⁺ — WHITE precipitate, SOLUBLE in excess, giving a colourless solution (sodium plumbite).

THE KEY DISTINCTIONS. The three white precipitates are separated by their behaviour in excess: calcium stays, zinc and lead dissolve. Zinc and lead are then separated by AMMONIUM HYDROXIDE — with ammonium hydroxide the zinc precipitate dissolves in excess but the lead precipitate does NOT.

Q5. WITH AMMONIUM HYDROXIDE SOLUTION:

(a) Cu²⁺ — PALE BLUE precipitate, SOLUBLE in excess giving a deep inky-blue solution (tetrammine copper(II) complex). This is the classic confirmatory test for copper.
(b) Zn²⁺ — WHITE precipitate, SOLUBLE in excess giving a colourless solution.
(c) Pb²⁺ — WHITE precipitate, INSOLUBLE in excess.
(d) Fe³⁺ — REDDISH-BROWN precipitate, insoluble in excess.

WHY BOTH REAGENTS ARE NEEDED. Sodium hydroxide alone cannot distinguish zinc from lead, since both give a white precipitate soluble in excess. Ammonium hydroxide separates them at once: zinc dissolves in excess, lead does not. Copper is likewise confirmed only by ammonium hydroxide, because the deep blue solution is unmistakable.

Q6. (a) The ANION is CHLORIDE, Cl⁻. Silver nitrate gives a white precipitate of silver chloride, and its solubility in ammonium hydroxide confirms chloride rather than bromide or iodide.

(b) The CATION is COPPER(II), Cu²⁺. A pale blue precipitate with sodium hydroxide is copper(II) hydroxide.

The salt Y is therefore COPPER(II) CHLORIDE, CuCl₂.

METHOD FOR EVERY 'IDENTIFY THE SALT' QUESTION: the test with silver nitrate, barium chloride or dilute acid identifies the ANION; the test with sodium hydroxide or ammonium hydroxide identifies the CATION; then name the salt formed from the two.

Q7. (a) CARBONATE — brisk EFFERVESCENCE; a colourless, odourless gas is evolved which turns lime water MILKY. The gas is CARBON DIOXIDE, CO₂.

(b) SULPHITE — a colourless gas with the choking smell of BURNING SULPHUR is evolved, which turns acidified potassium dichromate solution from orange to green. The gas is SULPHUR DIOXIDE, SO₂.

(c) SULPHIDE — a colourless gas with the smell of ROTTEN EGGS is evolved, which turns lead acetate paper black. The gas is HYDROGEN SULPHIDE, H₂S.

Note that carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide both turn lime water milky, so the smell is what separates them: CO₂ is odourless and SO₂ is choking.

C. Structured / Section B Questions

Q8. (i) P is ZINC, Zn²⁺; Q is LEAD, Pb²⁺; R is CALCIUM, Ca²⁺.

(ii) All three give a white precipitate with sodium hydroxide, so the cation must be one of calcium, zinc or lead — no other common cation gives a white precipitate.

R's precipitate does not dissolve in excess sodium hydroxide, so R is calcium: calcium hydroxide is not amphoteric and stays.

P and Q both dissolve in excess sodium hydroxide, so both are amphoteric — zinc or lead. Ammonium hydroxide then separates them, because it is a weak alkali: the zinc precipitate dissolves in excess ammonium hydroxide forming a soluble complex, while the lead precipitate does not. P, which dissolved, is therefore zinc; Q, which did not, is lead.

(iii) Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + 2H₂O

That is, zinc hydroxide dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide to give SODIUM ZINCATE and water — the reaction that demonstrates the amphoteric nature of zinc hydroxide.
