B. Short Answer Type

Q6. Because the physical conditions that control vegetation vary so widely across the country. RAINFALL ranges from over 1,000 cm at Cherrapunji to under 20 cm in the Thar desert, and rainfall is the chief control. TEMPERATURE varies from the tropical heat of the south to the sub-zero cold of the high Himalayas. ALTITUDE produces a complete succession of belts on a single mountainside, from tropical forest at the foot to alpine pasture near the snow line. SOIL varies from the deep alluvium of the plains to the leached laterite of the Ghats and the saline mud of the deltas.

Since each of these differs greatly from one part of India to another, and vegetation is the response to all of them together, the natural vegetation varies correspondingly — evergreen forest, deciduous forest, thorn scrub, mangrove and alpine vegetation all occurring within one country.

Official Answer

Q7. (a) Because the trees do not occur in pure stands — many different species grow mixed together, so a lumberman cannot fell a single commercially valuable kind in quantity in one place. The forests are also extremely dense, with tangled undergrowth and creepers, they grow in regions of very heavy rainfall and swampy ground, and they are remote and lack transport; the timber of many species is besides too hard and heavy to float down rivers.

(b) Because they grow in comparatively pure stands over large areas, so that valuable species can be felled economically; they occur in the accessible parts of the country with roads and railways; their timber — sal, teak, sandalwood, sheesham — is strong, durable and in demand for furniture, construction and railway sleepers; and the forests are open, with less undergrowth, which makes felling and extraction easier.

(c) Because they grow in tidal saline swamps where the mud is waterlogged and airless, so the roots cannot obtain oxygen from the soil. They therefore send up special roots called pneumatophores, which grow vertically out of the mud into the air and take in oxygen directly. The stilt roots of the mangrove also anchor the tree in soft shifting mud against the movement of the tides.

Official Answer

Q8. (a) Because rainfall is scanty and the surface soil is dry for most of the year, so plants must reach down to the water table far below. The long tap roots of desert plants penetrate deep into the ground to draw on subsoil moisture, which is the only reliable supply.

(b) Because these are coniferous trees, growing in regions of heavy snowfall. The conical shape, with sloping branches, allows the snow to slide off instead of accumulating on the crown; if snow collected, its weight would break the branches. The shape also presents less resistance to the strong mountain winds, and the needle-like leaves reduce transpiration and are not shed.

Official Answer

Q9. ADAPTATIONS, all directed to obtaining water and to losing as little of it as possible. Long tap roots that reach the water table far below the dry surface. Leaves reduced to thorns or spines, or thick, waxy and leathery, so that the surface from which transpiration can occur is minimal. Thick fleshy stems that store water. A waxy coating on stem and leaf to check evaporation. Few or no leaves at all, the stem itself carrying out photosynthesis.

Two such plants: cactus and acacia (babool). Also acceptable: khejri, kikar, date palm, euphorbia and wild date.

Official Answer

Q10. WHY. Forests bind the soil with their roots and break the force of the rain with their canopy, so they prevent soil erosion and the silting of rivers and reservoirs. They regulate the water cycle — increasing rainfall, checking run-off and helping ground water to recharge — and so reduce both floods and droughts. They maintain the balance of oxygen and carbon dioxide in the atmosphere and absorb pollutants, which moderates climate change. They are the habitat of wildlife and the reservoir of biodiversity. And they support the economy directly, supplying timber, fuel, fodder, bamboo, resin, gum, lac and medicinal plants, and providing the livelihood of tribal communities.

TWO METHODS. AGROFORESTRY — a sustainable system of managing a piece of land through the combined production of agricultural crops, forest crops and animal rearing on the same holding. SOCIAL FORESTRY or avenue plantation — planting trees along the sides of roads, railway lines, canal banks and on hill slopes and waste land, so that fuel and fodder needs are met without cutting natural forest.

(Also acceptable: afforestation and reforestation; the creation of reserved and protected forests and national parks; controlled and selective felling; firelines to check forest fires; and public awareness campaigns such as the Chipko movement.)

Official Answer

Q11. TROPICAL EVERGREEN — rainfall above 200 cm. Trees never shed all their leaves at once, so the forest is always green; dense, multi-storeyed, with hardwoods such as mahogany, ebony and rosewood. Found on the windward slopes of the Western Ghats, in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and in Assam and the north-east.

TROPICAL DECIDUOUS (monsoon forest) — rainfall 100 to 200 cm. Trees shed their leaves for six to eight weeks in the dry season to check transpiration. Chief trees teak, sal, sandalwood, sheesham. The most widespread and most commercially exploited forest in India: the lower Himalayan slopes, the Chota Nagpur plateau, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha and the eastern slopes of the Western Ghats.

TROPICAL DESERT or THORN — rainfall below 50 cm. Thorny scrub and stunted acacia, cactus and khejri, with long roots and reduced leaves. Found in Rajasthan, and in the drier parts of Gujarat, Punjab, Haryana and the Deccan interior.

LITTORAL or TIDAL (mangrove) — in the deltas and along the coasts, in saline tidal swamps. Sundari, and other trees with stilt roots and aerial breathing roots. The Sundarbans of the Ganga–Brahmaputra delta, and the deltas of the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Cauvery.

MOUNTAIN — arranged in belts by altitude rather than by rainfall. Deciduous forest at the foot, then temperate evergreen and coniferous forest of pine, deodar, spruce and fir between about 1,500 and 3,500 metres — conical trees that shed snow — then alpine shrubs and pastures, and finally tundra mosses and lichens below the snow line. Found throughout the Himalayas, and on the Nilgiris.

Q13. (a) Because on a mountain the temperature falls and the character of the rainfall changes with ALTITUDE. So the belts of vegetation change one above the other — deciduous and evergreen forest low down, then temperate wet (oak, chestnut) forest, then coniferous forest (pine, deodar, silver fir), then alpine scrub and meadow, and finally bare rock and snow.

(b) Littoral trees grow in soft, waterlogged, tidal mud that gives no firm anchorage and little air. STILT ROOTS arching down from the trunk prop the tree up in the shifting ooze, and pneumatophores (breathing roots) pushing up out of the mud take in oxygen for the roots, which the waterlogged soil cannot supply.

(c) The trees are BROAD-LEAVED EVERGREENS that shed their leaves a few at a time and continuously all year round, never all together — because rain and warmth are available every month, there is no dry or cold season to force a leafless rest. New leaves replace the old ones at once, so the canopy is always green and dense.
