B. Short Answer Type

Q5. Because Indian agriculture cannot rely on the rainfall alone. The monsoon is SEASONAL — nearly all the rain falls in four months, and the remaining eight are dry, so a second and third crop are impossible without irrigation. It is UNRELIABLE — it may come late, break in the middle, or fail altogether, and a failure without irrigation means famine. It is UNEVENLY DISTRIBUTED — some regions receive under 50 cm while others receive over 200 cm, and the dry regions can farm at all only by irrigating.

Beyond that, some crops — rice, sugarcane, jute — need far more water than even a good monsoon supplies; the high-yielding varieties of the Green Revolution require assured and regular watering to give their yield; and irrigation allows the rabi and zayad crops to be grown in the dry season, which is what makes Indian farming productive enough to feed the country.

Official Answer

Q6. (i) The WATER TABLE must be near the surface, so that ground water can be reached and lifted without excessive cost.

(ii) The ground water must be SUFFICIENT, SWEET AND RENEWED — a plentiful supply that is not saline or brackish, and that is recharged by percolation.

(iii) The SOIL must be soft and easily dug, and the ground water must not lie under hard rock; the alluvial soil of the northern plains is ideal, whereas hard crystalline rock makes wells difficult and expensive.

(iv) The land to be irrigated should be LEVEL, so that water flows over it easily, and the holding comparatively small, since a well can serve only a limited area.

(Also acceptable: a reliable source of power — animal, diesel or electric — to lift the water.)

Official Answer

Q7. (a) It captures rain where it falls, instead of letting it run off and be lost. India receives a great deal of rain in a very short season, and most of it flows away into rivers and the sea within days. Harvesting collects that water and either stores it for later use or directs it into the ground to recharge the aquifers and raise the water table. This reduces dependence on distant canals and on falling ground water; it provides a supply through the dry months; it improves the quality of ground water by dilution and checks the ingress of salt water in coastal areas; and it reduces flooding and soil erosion by cutting surface run-off.

(b) Two methods: ROOFTOP RAINWATER HARVESTING, in which rain falling on the roof of a building is led through gutters and pipes into a storage tank or into a recharge pit or well. And CHECK DAMS or PERCOLATION TANKS, small barriers built across seasonal streams so that the water is held back long enough to soak into the ground and recharge the wells nearby.

(Also acceptable: recharge pits and trenches; the traditional systems — tankas of Rajasthan, khadins, johads, kunds, and the bamboo drip irrigation of Meghalaya.)

Official Answer

Q8. SPRINKLER IRRIGATION. Water is carried under pressure through pipes and sprayed over the field through rotating nozzles, so that it falls on the crop like rain.

Two advantages: it uses far less water than flooding the field, because the water is distributed evenly and none is lost to run-off — which makes it suitable for regions where water is scarce. And it can be used on uneven or sloping ground and on sandy soil, where a canal or a flooded field would be impossible, because it does not require the land to be levelled and does not cause soil erosion.

(Also acceptable: it prevents waterlogging and the salinity that flooding causes; fertilizer can be applied with the water; and it saves labour.)

Official Answer

Q9. SURFACE WATER is water standing or flowing on the surface of the land — in rivers, lakes, ponds, tanks and reservoirs. It is replenished directly by rainfall and by snow-melt, is easily accessible, but is seasonal in most of India and is readily polluted and lost to evaporation.

GROUND WATER is water that has percolated through the soil and rock and is stored underground in the pores of the aquifer, reached by wells and tube wells. It is more reliable, available through the dry season, and cleaner — but it is being extracted far faster than it is recharged in much of northern and western India, so the water table is falling.

THE THREE CHIEF MEANS OF IRRIGATION. WELLS AND TUBE WELLS — the most important, accounting for the largest share of the irrigated area, best suited to the alluvial plains where the water table is high and the soil soft. CANALS — chiefly in the northern plains, where the land is level, the soil soft and perennial rivers supply the water; inundation canals draw water only in the flood season, perennial canals throughout the year. TANKS — chiefly in the Deccan, where the hard rock makes wells difficult and the uneven land holds natural hollows in which run-off can be stored.

Q10. An INUNDATION CANAL is taken straight off a river without any dam or barrage at its head. It carries water only when the river is high — that is, during the rainy season when the river is in flood — and is dry for the rest of the year.

A PERENNIAL CANAL is taken off a river behind a WEIR, DAM or BARRAGE that raises and regulates the water level. It therefore carries water ALL THE YEAR ROUND and gives a dependable supply for both the kharif and the rabi crop. Most inundation canals in India have now been converted into perennial canals.

Q12. (a) ADVANTAGE: a tank stores the monsoon run-off in a natural hollow at little cost and provides water through the dry months; it can also be used for washing, for cattle and for fish. DISADVANTAGE: many tanks are shallow and lose much water by EVAPORATION and SEEPAGE; they dry up in a year of poor rain, silt up quickly, and flood a large area of land.

(b) In the PENINSULAR PLATEAU — Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana. The hard, impervious rock will not hold ground water so wells are difficult; the uneven relief provides many natural hollows that need only a low bund to become a tank; the rivers are seasonal so their water must be stored; and the rock base stops the stored water from sinking away.

Q13. (a) In drip irrigation, water is carried in narrow pipes and released DROP BY DROP directly at the root of each plant. Almost none is lost by evaporation, seepage or run-off, so it saves 30–70 per cent of the water; it also saves fertiliser (fed in with the water), keeps weeds down, and lets crops be grown on sandy or sloping land and with brackish water.

(b) Because the equipment — the pipes, emitters, filters, pumps and controls — is EXPENSIVE to buy and to install, and needs regular cleaning and maintenance; the emitters clog with silt and salts; and India’s land holdings are small and its farmers poor, so most cannot afford it without a subsidy.
