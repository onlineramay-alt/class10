A. Multiple Choice Questions

Q1. (c) 3 — the action of a dilute acid on the metal. Copper lies BELOW hydrogen in the activity series, so it cannot displace hydrogen from a dilute acid and no reaction occurs. Copper salts must therefore be made from copper oxide, copper hydroxide or copper carbonate, all of which react with dilute acids normally.

Official Answer

Q2. (c) Both are true and (R) explains (A). Ionisation requires water: the more water present, the more completely the acid dissociates into H⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions. Concentrated sulphuric acid contains very little water and exists largely as un-ionised molecules, so it is a poor conductor; on dilution the degree of ionisation rises sharply, the concentration of mobile ions increases, and the solution becomes a strong electrolyte.

Official Answer

Q3. (a) ZnO. Zinc oxide is AMPHOTERIC — it reacts both with acids, forming a zinc salt, and with alkalis, forming a soluble zincate. With caustic potash it gives potassium zincate. Copper oxide is purely basic and reacts only with acids.

Official Answer

Q4. (c) Sodium sulphate + Lead nitrate. Lead sulphate is INSOLUBLE, so it must be made by PRECIPITATION — mixing two solutions so that the insoluble salt is thrown down and filtered off. Lead itself (a) would quickly be coated by insoluble PbSO₄ and stop reacting; (b) and (d) involve a nearly insoluble lead compound as a reactant, so the reaction would not go to completion.

Official Answer

Q5. (c) Aluminium hydroxide. It is AMPHOTERIC — with an acid it forms an aluminium salt and water, and with a strong alkali it forms a soluble aluminate and water. The other three hydroxides are purely basic and react only with acids.

Official Answer

Q6. (d) Sulphuric acid. An acid salt still contains replaceable hydrogen, so it can only form from a POLYBASIC acid — one with more than one replaceable hydrogen. Sulphuric acid is dibasic and can form the acid salt sodium hydrogen sulphate, NaHSO₄. Nitric, hydrochloric and acetic acids are all monobasic and form only normal salts.

Official Answer

Q7. (b) Amphoteric oxide. An amphoteric oxide (such as ZnO, Al₂O₃, PbO) behaves as a base towards an acid and as an acid towards an alkali, forming a salt and water in each case. Basic oxides react only with acids, acidic oxides only with alkalis, and neutral oxides with neither.

Official Answer

Q8. (d) CH₃COOH. An acid salt forms by the PARTIAL replacement of the replaceable hydrogen of an acid, which is only possible if the acid has more than one replaceable hydrogen. Acetic acid is MONOBASIC — only the one hydrogen of the –COOH group is replaceable — so it can form only a normal salt. Carbonic (dibasic), phosphoric (tribasic) and sulphuric (dibasic) acids all form acid salts.

Official Answer

Q9. (d) Phenolphthalein — colourless in acid and neutral solution, pink in alkaline solution. Methyl orange is the opposite kind of indicator (red/pink in acid, yellow in alkali), and litmus is red in acid, blue in alkali.

Official Answer

Q10. (b) salt + water. A metal oxide is basic (or amphoteric), so it neutralises an acid exactly as a base does, giving a salt and water only — for example CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O. No gas is evolved (unlike the reaction of an acid with a carbonate or an active metal).

Official Answer

Q11. (c) NaCl. A SALT dissociates into a positive ion that is neither H⁺/H₃O⁺ nor a metal-hydroxide cation, and a negative ion that is not OH⁻ — here Na⁺ and Cl⁻. An acid gives H₃O⁺; an alkali gives OH⁻.

Official Answer

Q12. (d) 6 → 2. Acidity INCREASES as pH falls, so only (c) and (d) are increases in acidity. A fall of 4 pH units (6→2) is a larger increase than a fall of 2 units (3→1), and each unit of pH is a tenfold change in H⁺ concentration, so 6→2 is the maximum increase in acidity.

Official Answer

Q13. (d) sodium hydroxide solution. With NaOH BOTH give a white precipitate that DISSOLVES in excess (both hydroxides are amphoteric), so NaOH cannot tell them apart. But KCl, Na₂SO₄ and dilute H₂SO₄ all give a white precipitate (PbCl₂, PbSO₄) with the lead salt and NO precipitate with the zinc salt, so each of those distinguishes them.

Official Answer

Q14. (c) Amphoteric oxides contain a metal. Amphoteric oxides — ZnO, Al₂O₃, PbO — are all metal oxides that happen to react with alkalis as well as acids. Basic oxides are METAL oxides, usually solids (a and d wrong); acidic oxides are non-metal oxides and are covalent, not ionic (b wrong).

Official Answer

Q15. (d) Sodium hydroxide — it is almost completely ionised in solution, giving a high concentration of Na⁺ and OH⁻ ions, so it conducts strongly. Acetic acid, oxalic acid and ammonium hydroxide are all only partly ionised and are weak electrolytes.

B. Identify / Name / Very Short Answer

Q16. ACETIC ACID, CH₃COOH, is MONOBASIC — although the molecule contains four hydrogen atoms, only the one in the carboxyl (–COOH) group is replaceable, so it furnishes ONE hydrogen ion per molecule.

SULPHURIC ACID, H₂SO₄, is DIBASIC — both its hydrogen atoms are replaceable, so it furnishes TWO hydrogen ions per molecule.

Official Answer

Q17. (a) ACID SALT — formed by the incomplete replacement of the replaceable hydrogen of an acid, so it still contains replaceable hydrogen. It can only be formed by a polybasic acid. Example: sodium hydrogen sulphate, NaHSO₄ (or sodium bicarbonate, NaHCO₃).

(b) BASIC SALT — formed by the incomplete replacement of the hydroxyl groups of a base, so it still contains hydroxyl. Example: basic lead nitrate, Pb(OH)NO₃ (or basic copper carbonate).

(c) NORMAL SALT — formed by the COMPLETE replacement of the replaceable hydrogen of an acid by a metal or ammonium radical; it contains neither replaceable hydrogen nor hydroxyl. Example: sodium sulphate, Na₂SO₄.

(d) COMPLEX SALT — a salt that on dissolving gives a complex ion which does not readily dissociate further. Example: sodium zincate, or tetrammine copper(II) sulphate.

(A fifth type, the DOUBLE SALT, dissolves to give ALL the ions of its constituent salts — for example potash alum.)

(a) A STRONG ELECTROLYTE is a substance that is almost COMPLETELY ionised in aqueous solution or in the molten state, and therefore contains a high concentration of mobile ions and conducts electricity well. (b) A WEAK ELECTROLYTE is one that is only PARTIALLY ionised, most of it remaining as molecules, so it contains few mobile ions and conducts poorly. Examples: acetic acid, carbonic acid, and ammonium hydroxide.

C. Short Answer Type

Q24. (i) DIRECT COMBINATION — heating two elements together. Suited to the salts of a metal with a non-metal where the metal shows variable valency, such as iron(III) chloride and iron(II) sulphide.

(ii) NEUTRALISATION or TITRATION — acid plus alkali. The only method for SOLUBLE salts of sodium, potassium and ammonium, whose oxides and carbonates are themselves soluble, so an insoluble excess cannot be filtered off.

(iii) SIMPLE DISPLACEMENT / DIRECT ACTION OF ACID ON A SOLID — a dilute acid acting on a metal, a metal oxide, a metal hydroxide or a metal carbonate, the insoluble excess being filtered off afterwards. Suited to SOLUBLE salts of the other metals — zinc, copper, magnesium, iron, lead. Note that the metal itself can only be used if it lies ABOVE hydrogen in the activity series, which is why copper salts must be made from the oxide or carbonate.

(iv) PRECIPITATION or DOUBLE DECOMPOSITION — mixing two solutions so that an INSOLUBLE salt is thrown down and can be filtered, washed and dried. The only method for insoluble salts such as lead chloride, barium sulphate, silver chloride and lead sulphate.

The choice of method therefore follows entirely from the SOLUBILITY of the salt to be made — that is the principle examiners are testing.

Q25. (a) Because copper lies BELOW hydrogen in the activity series and is therefore less reactive than hydrogen. It cannot displace hydrogen from the acid, so no reaction takes place. (Copper does react with concentrated sulphuric and with nitric acid, but there it is oxidised by the acid rather than displacing hydrogen.)

(b) Lead lies just above hydrogen in the activity series, so it would in principle displace hydrogen — but the LEAD CHLORIDE formed is INSOLUBLE and immediately coats the surface of the metal. That insoluble layer seals the lead off from the acid and the reaction stops almost at once. Lead chloride must therefore be prepared by PRECIPITATION instead, by mixing lead nitrate solution with dilute hydrochloric acid or sodium chloride solution.

Q26. Only observations 2 (iron) and 4 (zinc) are correct.

The principle is the ACTIVITY SERIES: a metal displaces hydrogen from a dilute acid only if it lies ABOVE hydrogen. Magnesium, zinc and iron all lie above hydrogen, so all three should give off hydrogen gas — and Rita's record for magnesium ('no gas') is therefore wrong. Copper lies below hydrogen and gives no gas at all, so her record for copper is also wrong.

The answer to the multiple-choice form of this question is 'Both 2 and 4'.

Official Answer

Q27. (a) LEAD(II) CHLORIDE — method R, PRECIPITATION. It is an insoluble salt, so it is thrown down by mixing lead nitrate solution with dilute hydrochloric acid (or sodium chloride solution) and then filtered.

(b) IRON(III) CHLORIDE — method S, DIRECT COMBINATION. Iron shows variable valency; heating iron in dry chlorine gas gives the iron(III) chloride directly, whereas dilute acid on iron would give iron(II) chloride.

(c) SODIUM SULPHATE — method Q, NEUTRALISATION BY TITRATION. It is a soluble salt of an alkali metal; sodium hydroxide and sulphuric acid are both soluble, so the salt is made by titrating one against the other and evaporating.

(d) ZINC SULPHATE — method P, SIMPLE DISPLACEMENT. Zinc lies above hydrogen in the activity series, so dilute sulphuric acid acting on excess zinc gives the soluble salt, and the unreacted zinc is filtered off.

Q29. (a) NO, they cannot be distinguished this way. D. Chemical Equations

Q30. (a) Zn + H₂SO₄ (dilute) → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑
(displacement — zinc is above hydrogen in the activity series)

(b) CuO + H₂SO₄ (dilute) → CuSO₄ + H₂O
(action of acid on a basic oxide — copper metal itself cannot be used)

(c) Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2HCl → PbCl₂↓ + 2HNO₃
(precipitation — lead chloride is insoluble; the equation may equally be written with NaCl)

(d) 2NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
(neutralisation — the only method for a soluble sodium salt)

Note the state symbols: ↑ for a gas evolved, ↓ for a precipitate. Examiners expect them, and expect the equation to be balanced.
