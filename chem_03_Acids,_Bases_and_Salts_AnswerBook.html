Q2. (c) Both are true and (R) explains (A). Ionisation requires water: the more water present, the more completely the acid dissociates into H⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions. Concentrated sulphuric acid contains very little water and exists largely as un-ionised molecules, so it is a poor conductor; on dilution the degree of ionisation rises sharply, the concentration of mobile ions increases, and the solution becomes a strong electrolyte.

Q3. (a) ZnO. Zinc oxide is AMPHOTERIC — it reacts both with acids, forming a zinc salt, and with alkalis, forming a soluble zincate. With caustic potash it gives potassium zincate. Copper oxide is purely basic and reacts only with acids.

B. Identify / Name / Very Short Answer

Q4. ACETIC ACID, CH₃COOH, is MONOBASIC — although the molecule contains four hydrogen atoms, only the one in the carboxyl (–COOH) group is replaceable, so it furnishes ONE hydrogen ion per molecule.

SULPHURIC ACID, H₂SO₄, is DIBASIC — both its hydrogen atoms are replaceable, so it furnishes TWO hydrogen ions per molecule.

Official Answer

Q5. (a) ACID SALT — formed by the incomplete replacement of the replaceable hydrogen of an acid, so it still contains replaceable hydrogen. It can only be formed by a polybasic acid. Example: sodium hydrogen sulphate, NaHSO₄ (or sodium bicarbonate, NaHCO₃).

(b) BASIC SALT — formed by the incomplete replacement of the hydroxyl groups of a base, so it still contains hydroxyl. Example: basic lead nitrate, Pb(OH)NO₃ (or basic copper carbonate).

(c) NORMAL SALT — formed by the COMPLETE replacement of the replaceable hydrogen of an acid by a metal or ammonium radical; it contains neither replaceable hydrogen nor hydroxyl. Example: sodium sulphate, Na₂SO₄.

(d) COMPLEX SALT — a salt that on dissolving gives a complex ion which does not readily dissociate further. Example: sodium zincate, or tetrammine copper(II) sulphate.

(A fifth type, the DOUBLE SALT, dissolves to give ALL the ions of its constituent salts — for example potash alum.)

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q6. (a) A STRONG ELECTROLYTE is a substance that is almost COMPLETELY ionised in aqueous solution or in the molten state, and therefore contains a high concentration of mobile ions and conducts electricity well. Examples: hydrochloric acid, sulphuric acid, nitric acid, sodium hydroxide, and all salts.

(b) A WEAK ELECTROLYTE is one that is only PARTIALLY ionised, most of it remaining as molecules, so it contains few mobile ions and conducts poorly. Examples: acetic acid, carbonic acid, and ammonium hydroxide.

C. Short Answer Type

Q7. (i) DIRECT COMBINATION — heating two elements together. Suited to the salts of a metal with a non-metal where the metal shows variable valency, such as iron(III) chloride and iron(II) sulphide.

(ii) NEUTRALISATION or TITRATION — acid plus alkali. The only method for SOLUBLE salts of sodium, potassium and ammonium, whose oxides and carbonates are themselves soluble, so an insoluble excess cannot be filtered off.

(iii) SIMPLE DISPLACEMENT / DIRECT ACTION OF ACID ON A SOLID — a dilute acid acting on a metal, a metal oxide, a metal hydroxide or a metal carbonate, the insoluble excess being filtered off afterwards. Suited to SOLUBLE salts of the other metals — zinc, copper, magnesium, iron, lead. Note that the metal itself can only be used if it lies ABOVE hydrogen in the activity series, which is why copper salts must be made from the oxide or carbonate.

(iv) PRECIPITATION or DOUBLE DECOMPOSITION — mixing two solutions so that an INSOLUBLE salt is thrown down and can be filtered, washed and dried. The only method for insoluble salts such as lead chloride, barium sulphate, silver chloride and lead sulphate.

The choice of method therefore follows entirely from the SOLUBILITY of the salt to be made — that is the principle examiners are testing.

Q8. (a) Because copper lies BELOW hydrogen in the activity series and is therefore less reactive than hydrogen. It cannot displace hydrogen from the acid, so no reaction takes place. (Copper does react with concentrated sulphuric and with nitric acid, but there it is oxidised by the acid rather than displacing hydrogen.)

(b) Lead lies just above hydrogen in the activity series, so it would in principle displace hydrogen — but the LEAD CHLORIDE formed is INSOLUBLE and immediately coats the surface of the metal. That insoluble layer seals the lead off from the acid and the reaction stops almost at once. Lead chloride must therefore be prepared by PRECIPITATION instead, by mixing lead nitrate solution with dilute hydrochloric acid or sodium chloride solution.

Q9. Only observations 2 (iron) and 4 (zinc) are correct.

The principle is the ACTIVITY SERIES: a metal displaces hydrogen from a dilute acid only if it lies ABOVE hydrogen. Magnesium, zinc and iron all lie above hydrogen, so all three should give off hydrogen gas — and Rita's record for magnesium ('no gas') is therefore wrong. Copper lies below hydrogen and gives no gas at all, so her record for copper is also wrong.

The answer to the multiple-choice form of this question is 'Both 2 and 4'.

D. Chemical Equations

Q10. (a) Zn + H₂SO₄ (dilute) → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑
(displacement — zinc is above hydrogen in the activity series)

(b) CuO + H₂SO₄ (dilute) → CuSO₄ + H₂O
(action of acid on a basic oxide — copper metal itself cannot be used)

(c) Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2HCl → PbCl₂↓ + 2HNO₃
(precipitation — lead chloride is insoluble; the equation may equally be written with NaCl)

(d) 2NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
(neutralisation — the only method for a soluble sodium salt)

Note the state symbols: ↑ for a gas evolved, ↓ for a precipitate. Examiners expect them, and expect the equation to be balanced.
