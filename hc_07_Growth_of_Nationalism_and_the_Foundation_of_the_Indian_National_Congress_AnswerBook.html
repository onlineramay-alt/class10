Official Answer

Q24. (i) The Vernacular Press Act, 1878. It imposed severe restrictions on newspapers published in Indian languages, requiring their printers to enter into bonds not to publish matter likely to excite disaffection, and empowering the government to seize their presses. The English-language press, read by Europeans, was expressly exempt. The same words in English were therefore lawful and in Bengali or Marathi criminal, which announced plainly that Indians were not to be trusted with the liberty Englishmen enjoyed.

(ii) The Arms Act, 1878. It made it an offence for an Indian to keep or carry arms without a licence, while Europeans, Anglo-Indians and their descendants were exempted from its operation entirely. The measure had nothing to do with public order and everything to do with race: it drew a legal line between the ruler and the ruled, and Indians felt the humiliation of it keenly.

Official Answer

Q25. The Bill, introduced under Lord Ripon by Sir Courtenay Ilbert, proposed to remove the anomaly by which an Indian district or sessions judge could not try a European accused of a criminal offence. The European community in India responded with a furious and well-organised agitation, and the government gave way: the Bill was so amended that a European could claim to be tried by a jury of which at least half were Europeans.

Its significance was twofold. It stripped away any illusion that the British meant to treat Indians as equals, since even a modest measure of judicial equality could not survive European protest. And it taught Indians the lesson of organisation — they had watched a determined minority force the government to retreat by agitation, and drew the obvious conclusion. The Indian National Congress was founded two years later.

Q28. (i) It gave educated Indians the ideas that were used against British rule. Reading Milton, Burke, Mill and Rousseau, and the history of the English, American and French revolutions, they absorbed the doctrines of liberty, equality, democracy and nationality, and applied them to their own condition. (ii) English became a common language for educated Indians of every province for the first time, so that a Bengali, a Maharashtrian and a Tamil could correspond, meet in conference and act together — an indispensable condition of an all-India movement. Official Answer

Q32. (i) The drain of wealth — India's revenues paying for British administration, British wars and the home charges — and the ruin of her handicrafts by duty-free British imports left the country poorer under British rule than before it. When Dadabhai Naoroji demonstrated this in figures, Indians could see that their poverty was not a misfortune but a consequence of the system, and economic grievance became the strongest argument for self-government.

(ii) The Bill of 1883 proposed only to let Indian judges try Europeans. The European community's furious agitation forced the government to water it down. Indians drew two lessons: that racial equality would never be conceded willingly, and that organised agitation could compel a government to retreat. The Congress was founded two years later.

(iii) Reformers like Rammohan Roy and Dayanand Saraswati attacked the abuses that the British used to justify their rule, and so restored Indian self-respect and the confidence that India could govern herself. Jyotiba Phule's work for the depressed classes and for girls' education, and the reform movements' attack on caste and communal barriers generally, helped make a common national feeling possible. And these movements formed the educated, publicly organised middle class from which the leadership of the national movement came.

Q34. (i) Allan Octavian Hume founded it; W. C. Bonnerjee presided over the first session, attended by 72 delegates.
(ii) Hume believed that the discontent building up in India needed a lawful outlet, and that an organised body through which educated Indian opinion could reach the government would prevent a repetition of 1857 — a 'safety valve', as it is often described. Whatever his intentions, the body he helped create did not remain a safety valve for long: within twenty years the Congress had become the principal vehicle of the demand for self-government, and within fifty it led the movement that ended British rule.
