B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q4. (i) She was born in Hasodzi, a village in the Central Region of Ghana. Her two favourite people are her grandmother, Nana, and her mother, Kaya.

(ii) They fought over Adjoa's legs. Nana held that they were too thin — 'spindly' — and that this was a serious matter, because in her view a woman needs proper hips and legs to bear children, so that thin legs threatened Adjoa's whole future as a woman. Kaya, who had thin legs herself and had been told the same thing, defended her daughter and refused to accept that the child was defective.

(iii) Whether Adjoa should go to school. Nana was doubtful — she could not see what school would do for a girl, and worried about the walking and the distance. Kaya insisted that Adjoa should be educated, and it is Kaya's view that prevails. The disagreement matters because school is what produces the running.

(iv) Any three of: Nana begins to wash and iron Adjoa's school uniform herself, with great care, instead of leaving it to Kaya; she carries Adjoa's things to the school; she comes to watch the races, which she had never done before; she polishes the cup and keeps it where visitors will see it; and she begins to speak of Adjoa's legs to other people, with pride rather than complaint.

(v) Adjoa is chosen to run for her school because she is fast, and she wins — bringing home a cup. The very legs Nana had grieved over turn out to be the reason her granddaughter is celebrated in a way no one in the family has been before. Nana is forced to revise a belief she has held all her life, and she does so out loud: thin legs can be useful, they can run.

Two traits make her remarkable. The first is her willingness to change her mind on the evidence — an old woman, deeply attached to a traditional view of what a woman is for, who accepts that she was wrong when her own granddaughter proves it, and says so publicly. The second is the depth of her love, which was never in doubt even while she was criticising: the worry about Adjoa's legs was worry about Adjoa's future, and the moment that future looks different, the whole force of that affection turns into pride. She is not a figure of oppressive tradition defeated; she is a person, and Aidoo treats her with respect throughout.

Official Answer

Q5. (i) At school she is found to be the fastest runner in her class, and is chosen to represent the school at the district games. She wins, and brings home a cup for the school — the first anyone in her family has been associated with.

(ii) Nana, who had never taken an interest in school affairs, comes to watch. Afterwards she takes charge of the cup, cleans and polishes it, and displays it where it will be seen; she carries Adjoa's things, washes and irons her uniform herself, and speaks of her granddaughter's legs to the other women of the village — the same legs she had been lamenting for seven years — with unmistakable pride.

(iii) That thin legs can be useful, because they can run. She does not merely stop objecting; she reverses the judgement openly, in front of others, and takes visible satisfaction in it. The concession is complete and it is public, which for a woman of her standing in a village is a considerable thing.

(iv) Because a seven-year-old can report without interpreting, and Aidoo needs exactly that. Adjoa hears the arguments about her legs, understands that they are about her but not what they mean, and repeats them to us without comment — so the reader grasps the weight of the tradition before the child does.

It also produces the story's humour and its tenderness. Adjoa's confusion about hips and babies, her worry that her legs are a family problem, and her careful observation of the grown-ups are funny in a way that keeps the story from becoming a lecture on gender.

And it makes the ending land without being stated. Adjoa does not tell us that a traditional belief about women's bodies has been overturned by a girl's achievement; she tells us that Nana polished the cup. The reader supplies the meaning, and the argument is far stronger for never being made.

C. Short Answer Type

Q6. Nana is Adjoa's grandmother and the strongest presence in the story. She is a woman of firm opinions, formed by a tradition in which a woman's worth is measured by her capacity to bear children — hence her long grief over Adjoa's thin legs, which she regards as a defect with serious consequences.

She is outspoken to the point of tactlessness. She discusses Adjoa's body in front of her, repeatedly, and argues about it with Kaya without any apparent thought that the child is listening — which is how Adjoa comes to believe her legs are a family problem.

But she is not unkind, and Aidoo takes care to show it. The criticism comes out of anxiety for Adjoa's future, not contempt; and when the evidence changes she changes with it, publicly and generously. She washes and irons the uniform, carries the child's things, comes to the sports, polishes the cup and tells the village that thin legs can run.

That capacity to revise a lifelong belief when a seven-year-old proves it wrong is what makes her remarkable, and it is why the story is affectionate towards tradition rather than dismissive of it.

Q7. The title answers the story's opening problem. Adjoa begins as the girl who cannot — who has the wrong legs, who may not be able to bear children, about whom the adults argue as though she were a difficulty to be managed. Everything is framed in terms of what she lacks.

'The Girl Who Can' reverses that at a stroke, and the sentence is deliberately left incomplete: can what? The story's answer is 'run', but the open ending of the phrase makes the larger claim — that a person's capacities are not settled in advance by what a tradition expects of her body.

There is also a quieter meaning. Adjoa can, and Nana can too: the old woman's capacity to change her mind is the story's second achievement, and it happens in the same last pages.

D. Long Answer / Essay Type

Q8. The story sets three generations of women against one another and refuses to make any of them a villain.

Nana holds the traditional view in its purest form: a woman's body is for childbearing, thin legs will not do, and the matter is important enough to discuss endlessly in front of the child concerned. It is not cruelty — it is a coherent worldview in which a woman's security depends on children, and Nana is anxious for a granddaughter she loves.

Kaya stands between. She has thin legs herself and has heard all this before; she defends her daughter, and she insists on school. She does not argue with the tradition in the abstract — she simply refuses to accept its verdict on her child, and she wins the one battle that matters.

Adjoa embodies the change without ever advocating it. She does nothing but be seven years old, go to school, and turn out to be fast. The story's argument is made entirely by events.

What makes Aidoo's treatment unusual is the ending. Tradition is not defeated, humiliated or abandoned; it revises itself. Nana is not proved a fool — she is proved wrong about one thing, and she says so, and then puts the full weight of her formidable personality behind her granddaughter. The cup she polishes is the emblem of that: an object from the new world, cared for by the old one.

The story is therefore optimistic in a way that is easy to miss. Change arrives not through confrontation but through demonstration, and the older generation turns out to be capable of it.

E. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q9. The whole story is spoken by Adjoa, and her age governs everything about it.

The syntax is a child's: long, tumbling sentences that double back, begin again, and worry at a thought — 'like all this business to do with my legs' — with parenthetical asides and repetitions. It reads as thinking aloud rather than as narration, and Aidoo sustains it without ever slipping into an adult cadence.

The limitation of understanding is the technique's real value. Adjoa reports the argument about hips and childbearing accurately and does not know what it means. She concludes, reasonably, that her legs are a problem for her family. The reader understands both what the adults are saying and what it is doing to the child, and neither has to be stated.

It also controls the tone. A story about a girl told that her body is inadequate could easily become bitter; told by a cheerful seven-year-old who is more puzzled than wounded, and who notices with great interest that her grandmother has started ironing her uniform, it stays warm and frequently funny.

And it makes the ending work. Adjoa never says that a belief has been overturned. She says Nana cleaned the cup — and the reader does the rest.
