B. Short Answer Type

Q2. BIODEGRADABLE waste can be broken down into simple substances by the action of bacteria, fungi and other micro-organisms, and so returns to the natural cycle. It decomposes comparatively quickly and can be composted to make manure. Examples: kitchen and food waste, and paper. (Also: leaves and garden waste, cow dung, cotton and woollen cloth, wood.)

NON-BIODEGRADABLE waste cannot be decomposed by micro-organisms, so it accumulates and persists in the environment for decades or centuries, and must be recycled or safely stored. Examples: plastic, and glass. (Also: metal cans, synthetic fibres, rubber, electronic waste, and chemical and radioactive waste.)

The distinction is the basis of the whole of waste management, because the two kinds must be SEGREGATED at source and dealt with quite differently — the first by composting, the second by recycling.

Q3. ON LAND. Open dumps SPOIL THE LANDSCAPE, turning usable land into ugly, foul-smelling wasteland and reducing the value of everything around them. They occupy land that a growing country needs for housing and farming. The waste renders the soil INFERTILE, since chemicals, heavy metals and plastics kill soil organisms and prevent aeration and the absorption of water. And rain percolating through a dump produces LEACHATE — a toxic liquid that carries chemicals and heavy metals down into the ground water, poisoning wells and aquifers, sometimes for a long distance around.

ON HEALTH. Rotting waste breeds FLIES, MOSQUITOES, RATS AND STRAY DOGS, which are the vectors of cholera, typhoid, dysentery, malaria, dengue and plague. Decomposition releases methane, hydrogen sulphide and other gases, causing headache, nausea and RESPIRATORY DISEASE, and the burning of waste — especially plastic — releases dioxins and furans, which are carcinogenic. The people worst affected are the rag-pickers and the poor who live beside the dumps, who handle waste without protection and suffer skin disease, infection and injury. Contaminated ground water spreads waterborne illness far beyond the dump itself.

Q4. TERRESTRIAL LIFE. Cattle and stray animals feeding at dumps swallow PLASTIC BAGS, which lodge in the stomach and cause a slow and painful death — a common sight in Indian towns. Chemicals and heavy metals in the soil kill earthworms and the micro-organisms on which soil fertility depends, and enter the food chain through plants. Habitats are destroyed where dumps spread, and the birds and animals that scavenge them — crows, kites, dogs — multiply at the expense of other species, upsetting the local balance.

AQUATIC LIFE. Waste discharged into rivers and lakes reduces the DISSOLVED OXYGEN, because its decomposition consumes oxygen, so fish and other aquatic organisms suffocate. Nutrients from sewage and fertilizer cause EUTROPHICATION — an explosive growth of algae that covers the surface, cuts off light and, on dying, removes the remaining oxygen, killing the water body. Toxic chemicals and heavy metals such as mercury and lead accumulate in the tissue of fish and are magnified up the food chain to human beings.

IN THE SEA, plastic is now the dominant problem: turtles swallow bags mistaking them for jellyfish, birds and fish fill their stomachs with fragments, and animals are entangled in netting and rings. Plastic breaks down into MICROPLASTICS which are eaten by plankton and pass through the entire marine food chain, and oil spills coat sea birds and destroy breeding grounds. Coral reefs, which are exceptionally sensitive, die under silt and pollution.

Q5. Because the volume of waste is growing faster than the means of disposing of it. Population growth, urbanisation, rising incomes and packaged consumer goods have multiplied what each person throws away, while the land available for dumping has not increased.

It is necessary to PROTECT HEALTH — uncollected waste breeds the vectors of cholera, typhoid, malaria and dengue, and contaminates the ground water that people drink. To PROTECT THE ENVIRONMENT — from the poisoning of soil and water, the death of aquatic and animal life, and the methane released by rotting waste, which is a powerful greenhouse gas. To CONSERVE RESOURCES — much of what is thrown away is metal, glass, paper and plastic that can be recycled, and every tonne recycled is a tonne of ore, timber or oil not extracted. To RECLAIM LAND, since managed disposal takes a fraction of the space of open dumping. And to make cities LIVABLE, since a city that cannot deal with its refuse cannot function.

The practical answer lies in the three Rs — REDUCE, REUSE, RECYCLE — supported by segregation at source, composting of the biodegradable fraction and the safe disposal of the rest.
