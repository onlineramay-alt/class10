D. Descriptive Type Questions

Q52. (a) Transpiration: the loss of water, as water vapour, from the exposed/aerial parts of a plant, mainly through the stomata of the leaves. (b) Exudation: the oozing out of fluids (sap, resin, gum) from an injured or cut plant part. (c) Potometer: an apparatus used to measure the rate of water uptake by a cut shoot, taken as an approximate measure of its rate of transpiration. (d) Wilting: the drooping/loss of turgidity of the leaves and young stems of a plant, occurring when the rate of transpiration exceeds the rate of water absorption by the roots. (e) Hydathodes: specialised pores, usually at the tips or margins of leaves, through which excess water is exuded as droplets (guttation). (f) Cuticle: a waxy, waterproof layer secreted by and covering the epidermis of leaves and stems, which reduces water loss (cuticular transpiration).

Official Answer

Q53. (a) Stomata are minute pores in the leaf epidermis, bordered by a pair of guard cells that actively regulate opening/closing, and are the principal site of transpiration; lenticels are small, permanently open pores (no guard cells) on the bark of older woody stems, mainly meant for gaseous exchange (respiration), through which a minor amount of lenticular transpiration also occurs. (b) Guttation is the loss of excess water, as droplets, from the hydathodes at the margins/tips of leaves of intact, uninjured herbaceous plants, driven by root pressure; bleeding is the oozing out of cell sap from a cut or injured plant part, also driven by root pressure, but from a wound rather than a specialised structure. (c) Transpiration is a physiological, biologically regulated process (through stomata/guard cells) confined to the loss of water vapour from the living aerial parts of a plant; evaporation is a purely physical process that can occur from any wet or moist surface, living or non-living, and is not under the plant's physiological control.

Official Answer

Q54. (a) Nerium has sunken stomata covered by hair/trichomes and a thick waxy cuticle — both xerophytic adaptations that cut down water loss. (b) A dicot (dorsiventral) leaf has a greater number of stomata on its lower (abaxial) surface than its upper surface, so more water vapour escapes from below. (c) Wind sweeps away the humid layer of air that collects around the stomata, maintaining a steep vapour-pressure gradient and so speeding up the diffusion of water vapour out of the leaf. (d) At midday, high temperature and light intensity push the transpiration rate above the rate of water absorption by the roots, causing a temporary water deficit and wilting; in the evening, transpiration slows while absorption continues, restoring turgidity. (e) At night, stomata are largely closed and transpiration is minimal, but root pressure continues to push water up, so absorption exceeds transpiration and the excess water is forced out as droplets through the hydathodes. (f) Large-scale transpiration from forest vegetation releases huge quantities of water vapour into the atmosphere, raising local humidity and promoting cloud formation and rainfall.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q55. Transpiration is 'necessary' because it creates the suction pull that drives the ascent of sap, helps distribute absorbed minerals throughout the plant, and cools the plant in hot weather through evaporative heat loss. It is an 'evil' because the same stomata that must stay open to let CO2 in for photosynthesis inevitably lose large quantities of water vapour as an unavoidable side-effect — often far more water than the plant strictly needs — forcing a continuous trade-off between gas exchange and water conservation.
