C. Short Answer Type

Q13. Archduke Francis Ferdinand, the heir to the throne of Austria, was assassinated at Sarajevo, the capital of Bosnia, on 28 June 1914. The assassination was carried out by a secret society called the 'Union of Death', formed by extremist Serbian nationalists whose aim was to unite all Serbians into a single state — and Bosnia, which Austria had annexed in 1908, contained many Serbs.

Austria held Serbia responsible and served an ultimatum making eleven demands. Serbia accepted most of them, but refused those which would have destroyed her sovereignty by allowing Austrian officials to conduct the investigation on Serbian soil. Austria thereupon declared war on Serbia on 28 July 1914.

The alliance system then turned a Balkan quarrel into a European war within a week. Russia mobilised in support of Serbia; Germany, bound to Austria, declared war on Russia and then on France; and when German troops entered Belgium to reach France, Britain declared war on Germany.

Official Answer

Q14. Nationalism: in its aggressive form, national feeling had become a desire to dominate others. France had never accepted the loss of Alsace-Lorraine in 1871 and looked for revanche; Italy claimed Italian-speaking lands still under Austria; and in the Balkans the Pan-Slav movement, led by Serbia and backed by Russia, worked to gather all Slavs into one state, which threatened the very existence of the multinational Austro-Hungarian Empire. The Balkans were therefore called the powder keg of Europe.

Imperialism: the powers competed for colonies as sources of raw materials, markets and investment. Britain and France had taken the greater part of Africa and Asia; Germany, unified only in 1871, arrived late, found little left, and demanded a 'place in the sun', producing the two Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911. Each such collision deepened the hostility between the alliance groups and made a general war more likely when the spark came.

Official Answer

Q15. The Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy, formed in 1882; and the Triple Entente of Britain, France and Russia, completed in 1907.

The system helped cause the war in two ways. First, it divided Europe into two armed camps, each of which regarded any gain by the other as a loss to itself, so that every colonial or Balkan quarrel became a trial of strength between blocs rather than a dispute between two states. Second, and decisively, it removed the possibility of localising a conflict: because each power was bound to support its partners, the Austrian declaration of war on Serbia automatically brought in Russia, then Germany, then France and Britain. A quarrel in the Balkans became a world war in a week because of the treaties, not because the other powers had any interest in Serbia.

Q16. (i) It was a dictated peace. The German delegates were not allowed to negotiate at all: they were summoned to Versailles, handed the terms and required to sign. Germans called it the Diktat.

(ii) The war-guilt clause required Germany to accept sole responsibility for causing the war — which Germans regarded as historically false, since the crisis of 1914 had been the work of several governments — and it was on this admission that the demand for reparations was founded.

(iii) The reparations of £6,600 million were beyond her capacity to pay, and were exacted from a country that had also lost its merchant fleet, its foreign investments and the Saar coalfields; the burden helped produce the ruinous inflation of the following years.

(iv) She lost about a tenth of her population and an eighth of her territory in Europe — Alsace-Lorraine to France, Eupen and Malmedy to Belgium, North Schleswig to Denmark, the Polish Corridor to Poland with Danzig made a free city, and East Prussia cut off from the rest of the country — besides every one of her colonies. And she was disarmed almost to a police force, her army limited to 100,000 men and her navy to six battleships, while the victors did not disarm at all.

Official Answer

Q17. (i) Alsace-Lorraine, taken from her by Germany in 1871, was restored to France — the recovery of which had been the object of French policy for nearly fifty years. (ii) The coal mines of the Saar basin were handed to France for fifteen years in compensation for the French mines destroyed in the war, the territory itself being administered by the League. (iii) Her eastern frontier was made secure: the Rhineland was permanently demilitarised and occupied by Allied troops for fifteen years, and German military power was cut to a hundred thousand men with no tanks, aircraft or submarines, so that the neighbour who had invaded her twice in fifty years was left incapable of doing so again.

Official Answer

Q18. The map of Europe was redrawn on the principle of nationality. The Austro-Hungarian Empire was dissolved altogether, and out of it emerged Austria and Hungary as separate states, together with the new states of Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia. Poland, partitioned out of existence in the eighteenth century, was restored as an independent country and given access to the sea through the Polish Corridor, Danzig becoming a free city under the League.

Germany lost Alsace-Lorraine to France, Eupen and Malmedy to Belgium and North Schleswig to Denmark, and all her colonies were distributed among the victors as mandates of the League. The Ottoman Empire was broken up, its Arab provinces becoming mandates of Britain and France. And from the western borderlands of the former Russian Empire came the independent states of Finland, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania.

Official Answer

Q19. (i) The United States never joined. President Wilson had been the League's chief architect, but the Senate refused to ratify the Treaty, so the most powerful state in the world stayed outside — and Germany and Russia were excluded at first, which made the League look like a club of the victors rather than a body representing the world.

(ii) It had no armed force of its own. It could recommend sanctions, but their application depended on the willingness of member states, and when that willingness was absent — as over Abyssinia — the League could do nothing.

(iii) Its procedure required unanimity in the Council, so that any interested party could block action against itself.

(iv) It failed the tests that mattered and its members deserted it. Japan seized Manchuria in 1931 and left the League when the Lytton Commission reported against her; Italy conquered Abyssinia in 1935 despite half-hearted sanctions; Germany walked out in 1933 and repudiated Versailles clause by clause while Britain and France appeased her. Each unpunished aggression taught the next aggressor that the League could be defied, and the policy of appeasement finished what its own weakness had begun.
