B. Very Short Answer

Q3. A SPRAIN is an injury to a LIGAMENT — the tough tissue joining bone to bone at a joint — caused by the joint being forced beyond its normal range, as in a twisted ankle. The signs are pain, swelling, bruising and instability of the joint.

A STRAIN is an injury to a MUSCLE or a TENDON — the tissue joining muscle to bone — caused by overstretching or sudden violent contraction, as in a pulled hamstring while sprinting. The signs are pain, tenderness, muscle spasm and loss of power.

The one-line distinction: SPRAIN — ligament — joint. STRAIN — muscle or tendon.

Q4. R — REST. Stop the activity at once and rest the injured part, to prevent further damage and allow healing to begin. Continuing to play on an injury turns a minor one into a serious one.

I — ICE. Apply ice or a cold pack, wrapped in a cloth, for 15 to 20 minutes at a time, several times in the first 48 hours. Cold constricts the blood vessels, which limits internal bleeding and swelling, and it numbs the area, reducing pain. Never apply ice directly to the skin.

C — COMPRESSION. Bandage the part firmly with a crepe or elastic bandage — firm enough to limit swelling but not so tight as to cut off circulation. Check for numbness or discolouration beyond the bandage.

E — ELEVATION. Raise the injured part above the level of the heart, so that gravity helps drain fluid away from the injury and reduces swelling.

The purpose of all four together is to CONTROL SWELLING and BLEEDING in the first 48 hours, because it is the swelling that causes much of the pain and delays recovery.

C. Short Answer Type

Q5. WHAT IT IS. The Anterior Cruciate Ligament runs diagonally through the middle of the knee and prevents the shin bone sliding forward in front of the thigh bone; it is one of the knee's main stabilisers, particularly in rotation.

CAUSE. Typically a NON-CONTACT injury: a sudden stop, a sharp change of direction, a landing from a jump with the knee straight, or a twist of the knee while the foot is planted. Common in football, basketball, hockey and skiing. It may also result from a direct blow to the outside of the knee.

SYMPTOMS. Often an audible 'POP' at the moment of injury; severe pain; rapid swelling within a few hours from bleeding into the joint; inability to continue playing; and a feeling that the knee is unstable or 'giving way', particularly when turning.

MANAGEMENT. Immediate R.I.C.E. — rest, ice, compression, elevation — and no weight-bearing on the leg. The athlete must be taken for medical examination, since a torn ACL is diagnosed by clinical tests and MRI. A complete tear does not heal by itself, and in a young sportsperson who wishes to continue in a pivoting sport it usually requires SURGICAL RECONSTRUCTION, followed by a long and supervised rehabilitation of six to nine months to rebuild strength, balance and confidence. Returning too early is the commonest cause of re-injury.

The MCL, by contrast, is on the inner side of the knee, is usually injured by a blow to the outside of the knee, and often heals with rest and bracing without surgery.

Q6. (i) PROPER WARM-UP AND COOL-DOWN. A warm-up of light activity and stretching raises muscle temperature, increases blood flow and prepares the joints, greatly reducing the risk of strains; a cool-down clears waste products and reduces stiffness.

(ii) CORRECT TECHNIQUE. Most overuse injuries come from faulty technique repeated many times — a bowler's action, a runner's stride, a lifter's back position. Coaching in correct form is a safety measure as much as a performance measure.

(iii) PROPER EQUIPMENT AND PROTECTIVE GEAR. Well-fitting footwear appropriate to the surface, and the protective equipment the game requires — helmet, pads, gloves, guards, mouthguard — worn every time, not only in matches.

(iv) SAFE PLAYING SURFACE AND FACILITIES. The ground checked for stones, holes, glass and water; goalposts secured; the court free of obstruction; adequate lighting; and sufficient run-off space beyond the boundaries.

(v) ADEQUATE FITNESS AND GRADUAL PROGRESSION. An unfit player is an injured player. Fitness must be built before the season begins, and training loads increased gradually — the principle of progression is a safety principle.

(vi) OBEDIENCE TO THE RULES, AND FAIR PLAY. Most rules of a contact sport exist precisely to prevent injury — the offside law, the high-tackle law, the obstruction rule — and playing within them, with respect for the opponent, prevents a large share of injuries.

(Also acceptable: adequate rest and recovery; correct nutrition and hydration; medical examination before the season; matching players by age, size and ability; and the presence of a first-aid kit and a trained person at every session.)
