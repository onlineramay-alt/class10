B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q4. (i) He is alone in his orchard, some time after midnight — he has not slept since Cassius first whetted him against Caesar, and he calls for Lucius to light a taper in his study. Lucius has just brought him one of the forged papers that Cassius arranged to be thrown in at his window.

(ii) It costs him his only firm ground. He admits he has no grievance against Caesar — no injury, no personal wrong — which removes every ordinary justification for killing a man. In its place he puts 'the general': the public good, an abstraction. The whole speech is an attempt to build a case for murder out of a principle rather than out of a fact.

(iii) The adder hatches in the warmth of a bright day; the very sunshine that seems benign produces the poisonous thing. Applied to Caesar, the crown is the bright day: it is the elevation that would breed the venom. The image concedes that Caesar is not yet an adder, and is Brutus's way of arguing that one must act before the danger exists — 'that craves wary walking'.

(iv) He concludes that Caesar must be killed. His reasoning is that although Caesar has so far shown no tyranny, greatness commonly disjoins remorse from power; that men climbing an ambitious ladder scorn the base degrees by which they did ascend once they reach the top; and that since the quarrel will bear no colour for the thing Caesar is, it must be fashioned thus — 'think him as a serpent's egg / Which, hatch'd, would as his kind grow mischievous, / And kill him in the shell'.

(v) The honesty is complete and is what makes Brutus admirable. He does not pretend to a grievance he does not have, he does not conceal from himself that he loves Caesar, and he states his own weakest point — that Caesar has done nothing wrong — in his own first sentences.

The weakness is in the logic, and Shakespeare makes it visible. Brutus admits that the case 'will bear no colour for the thing he is' — that is, the evidence does not support the conclusion — and then proceeds anyway, on the grounds of what Caesar might become. He also gives himself away in a phrase: the quarrel 'must be fashion'd'. A man who has to fashion his justification has begun with the conclusion and worked backwards. It is a soliloquy in which a good man reasons himself into an act his own reasoning cannot support, and it is the moral centre of the play.

Q5. (i) To Calpurnia, his wife, who has cried out three times in her sleep 'Help, ho! they murder Caesar!' and is begging him not to stir out of the house that day.

(ii) The watch have seen most horrid sights: a lioness whelped in the streets; graves that yawned and yielded up their dead; fierce fiery warriors fighting upon the clouds in ranks and squadrons, which drizzled blood upon the Capitol; the noise of battle in the air, horses neighing, dying men groaning, and ghosts shrieking in the streets. The augurers, moreover, sacrificing a beast, could find no heart within it.

(iii) A coward, imagining his death over and over, suffers it many times before it actually happens; a brave man suffers it only once, when it comes. Fear kills more often than death does.

(iv) Decius Brutus, one of the conspirators, who has come precisely to fetch him. He does three things. He reinterprets Calpurnia's dream, turning the statue running blood into a favourable omen — that from Caesar great Rome shall suck reviving blood, and great men shall press for tinctures, stains, relics and cognizance. He then reports that the Senate have concluded to give a crown that day to mighty Caesar, and hints they may change their minds if it is postponed. Finally he touches Caesar where he is weakest, suggesting it will be mocked as 'Caesar is afraid' and that Caesar's love for him makes him speak so plainly. Caesar at once calls for his robe: 'How foolish do your fears seem now, Calpurnia! / I am ashamed I did yield to them.'

(v) It shows the same division as Act I, now fatal. Caesar's philosophy is genuinely noble — the lines on cowards and the valiant are among the finest in the play, and he means them. But the scene proves that his courage is bound up with his reputation for courage: he can face death, but not the suggestion that he is afraid. He yields to his wife in private and reverses himself the instant a witness implies that yielding will look weak. Decius has understood him perfectly, and says so beforehand: he can o'ersway him, for Caesar loves to hear that unicorns may be betrayed with trees and men with flatterers. The man who cannot be frightened can be flattered, and it kills him within the hour.

Q6. Because he believes the cause itself is sufficient bond. He argues that if the face of men, the sufferance of their souls and the abuse of the time are weak motives, then let each man go home to his idle bed; but if they carry fire enough to kindle cowards, what need they any spur but their own cause to prick them to redress?

He adds that an oath would insult them: priests and cowards and men cautelous swear, and to bind their honesty with an oath would stain 'the even virtue of our enterprise' and the insuppressive mettle of their spirits. Every drop of blood that every Roman bears is guilty of a several bastardy if he do break the smallest particle of any promise that hath passed from him.

The speech is characteristic. It is high-minded, genuinely fine, and shows Brutus turning a practical question — how do we bind these men? — into a matter of honour. It is also the first sign that he has taken over the leadership of a conspiracy he joined last.

Q7. Cassius proposes that Antony, being so well beloved of Caesar, should fall with him — 'Let Antony and Caesar fall together.' Brutus refuses on two grounds.

First, appearance: 'Our course will seem too bloody, Caius Cassius, / To cut the head off and then hack the limbs.' He wants the killing to look like a sacrifice and not a butchery — 'Let us be sacrificers, but not butchers' — and would carve Caesar as a dish fit for the gods.

Second, contempt: Antony is but a limb of Caesar, and there is no fear in him, for he is given to sports, to wildness and much company. When Caesar's head is off, Antony 'can do no more than Caesar's arm / When Caesar's head is off'.

He is wrong on both counts. The distinction between sacrifice and butchery is one that exists only in Brutus's mind; when the deed is done the conspirators bathe their arms to the elbows in blood, and it looks to Rome exactly like murder. And the estimate of Antony is a catastrophic misreading — Cassius has judged him rightly. Within a single scene Antony will out-manoeuvre Brutus twice, and his oration will destroy the conspiracy. Brutus's error is characteristic: he judges men by what he would do in their place.

Q8. It is a marriage of real affection under strain. Portia has noticed everything — that Brutus stole from his bed, that he walked about musing and sighing with his arms across, that he scratched his head and stamped, that when she asked what was the matter he stared at her with ungentle looks and waved his hand impatiently for her to leave.

She presses him on the ground of the marriage itself: 'Am I yourself / But, as it were, in sort or limitation, / To keep with you at meals, comfort your bed, / And talk to you sometimes? Dwell I but in the suburbs / Of your good pleasure? If it be no more, / Portia is Brutus' harlot, not his wife.' She kneels; she appeals to his 'once commended beauty', to his vows of love, and to that great vow which did incorporate and make them one.

When he will not tell her, she offers proof of her strength — she is Cato's daughter and Brutus's wife, and she has given herself a voluntary wound in the thigh and borne it with patience.

Brutus is deeply moved — 'O ye gods, / Render me worthy of this noble wife!' — and promises to tell her all. The scene matters because it shows what Brutus is sacrificing: the conspiracy is already costing him his sleep, his openness with his wife, and his peace, before it has cost anyone a life. Portia's later death, reported in Act IV, is prepared for here.

D. Long Answer / Essay Type

Q9. The act is built out of attempts to prevent the assassination, each of which fails for a reason that is characteristic of the person who ignores it.

Calpurnia's warning is the fullest. She has dreamed of Caesar's statue running blood; the watch have seen fire in the sky and a lioness whelping in the streets; the augurers find no heart in the sacrificed beast. Caesar dismisses it all first with philosophy — 'Cowards die many times before their deaths' — then yields to her, and then reverses himself the moment Decius suggests that staying at home will look like fear. The warning fails because Caesar cannot bear to seem afraid.

Artemidorus's warning is the most nearly successful, and the most painful to watch. In Scene iii he stands in the street with a paper that names every conspirator — 'If thou read this, O Caesar, thou mayst live; / If not, the Fates with traitors do contrive' — and in Act III Scene i he presses it on Caesar with 'O Caesar, read mine first; for mine's a suit / That touches Caesar nearer.' Caesar replies, 'What touches us ourself shall be last served,' and hands it aside. The warning fails because of Caesar's public dignity: the very greatness of the man is what kills him.

The soothsayer's warning, given in Act I, is repeated in Scene iv when Portia meets him in the street and learns he means to speak to Caesar again. It fails because Caesar has already decided the man is a dreamer.

And Portia's questioning of Brutus is a warning of another kind — an appeal to his honesty that might have broken the secret open. It fails because Brutus is interrupted by a knock at the door.

The cumulative effect is dread rather than suspense. The audience knows what is coming; the act's business is to show that it could have been stopped four times over, and that each failure comes from a fixed quality of character rather than from bad luck. That is what makes it a tragedy and not an accident.

E. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q10. The two wives appear in consecutive scenes, and the parallel is deliberate: each tries to influence her husband at the crisis, and each is placed at the edge of a world of men that has no room for her.

Calpurnia's authority is emotional and superstitious. She has never stood on ceremonies, yet now they fright her; she kneels to Caesar; she wins, briefly, by making his staying her fault rather than his fear — 'Call it my fear / That keeps you in the house, and not your own.' Her failure is instant and complete: Decius, a man, overturns her in twelve lines.

Portia's claim is intellectual and moral. She argues rather than pleads, and her argument is about the nature of marriage — that a wife excluded from her husband's mind is a harlot and not a wife. She is conscious of what is held against her sex and answers it directly: 'Think you I am no stronger than my sex, / Being so father'd and so husbanded?' The self-inflicted wound is her proof, and it is a Roman proof, in the idiom of the men around her.

Both are right and both are ignored. Calpurnia's fears are literally accurate; Portia's judgement of her husband's distress is exact. Their exclusion is shown as a loss to the men: Caesar dies because he will not listen to his wife, and Brutus's isolation from Portia is part of the moral loneliness that destroys him.

Shakespeare also gives them the same end — a private death, reported second-hand. Portia's suicide by swallowing fire is told in Act IV in a few lines between two men discussing military strategy, and the play returns immediately to business. The brutality of that placing is the point.
