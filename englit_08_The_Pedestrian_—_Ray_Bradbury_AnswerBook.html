B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q4. (i) A.D. 2053. What Leonard Mead most dearly loved to do was to walk — to go out alone into the silence of the city on a November evening and walk for hours through the empty streets, which he had done every night for ten years.

(ii) He compares it to walking through a graveyard. The comparison is apt because the houses are dark and silent, with no sign of life in them, like tombs; and because the only light is the faint, flickering grey glimmer of the television screens behind the windows, which Bradbury likens to fireflies over graves. A third reason: the people inside are physically alive but as good as dead — sitting motionless, unspeaking, in front of their screens.

(iii) That he is a writer, which the car does not accept, recording instead 'No profession'; that he was walking simply for air and to see, with no purpose or destination, which the car cannot classify; and that he has no viewing screen in his house — no television — which the car treats as the most damning admission of all. He is also unmarried, which counts against him.

(iv) He is taken to the Psychiatric Center for Research on Regressive Tendencies. As the car drives him away it passes his own house, and the author observes that it is the one house in the whole darkened city with all its lights blazing — a warm, bright, lived-in house among the tombs.

(v) Two dangers. First, that technology, and particularly passive mass entertainment, can hollow out a society: everyone in Bradbury's city is alive, comfortable and utterly inert, and nobody notices that they have stopped living. Second, that a society which cannot understand an individual will treat him as sick — Mead has broken no law that we are told of, and is arrested for being unclassifiable.

An alternative title. 'Regressive Tendencies' would be a good choice, because the phrase is the story's own verdict on Mead and its bitterest irony: the one man still doing something human is classified as going backwards. 'The Last Walker' or 'A.D. 2053' would also be defensible, provided the reason is argued from the text.

Q5. (i) One. In the previous year the crime rate had fallen so far that the police force had been cut from three cars to one, in a city of three million people.

(ii) It is not a human voice but a metallic one produced by the car itself; there is no policeman inside. Mead realises, when he looks into the back seat, that the car is empty — he is being arrested by a machine.

(iii) Because in 2053 writing is not an occupation anyone practises. Nobody reads; magazines and books are not published any more, as Mead observes; so the category does not exist in the car's records and cannot be entered. The moment is the whole story in miniature: a machine that can only recognise what fits its categories, dealing with a man who does not fit any.

(iv) He looks into the back seat and finds it a little cell, a little black jail with bars, smelling of riveted steel and harsh antiseptic; it is too clean and too hard and too metallic. The front seat, where a policeman would sit, is empty.

(v) It is Bradbury's grimmest joke. The crime rate has collapsed and only one police car is needed — which sounds like an achievement until one asks why. There is no crime because there are no people out of doors, no gatherings, no disagreements, nothing happening at all: everyone is indoors watching a screen. The society has achieved perfect order by ceasing to live.

And the single remaining car exists to deal with exactly one kind of offender — the man who goes outside. Order has been purchased at the price of everything that made order worth having, and the machinery that maintains it has no idea what to do with a human being who steps out of the pattern except to classify him as ill.

C. Short Answer Type

Q6. It is a city of three million people in which the streets are entirely empty after dark. The pavements are buckled and grassy from disuse; Mead has walked them for ten years and has never met another person walking. He wears sneakers because leather heels bring dogs barking and lights coming on.

The houses are dark, but not asleep. Behind the windows there is a faint flickering grey light — the viewing screens — and Bradbury compares the effect to fireflies in a graveyard. Inside, the people sit unmoving, tomblike, as the images wash over them.

There is no crime, and therefore almost no police: the force has been cut to a single car for the whole city. There are no readers; books and magazines have ceased to be published. There is no conversation, no gathering, no evening trade.

The overall impression is deliberately funereal — the recurring images are of tombs, graveyards, cemeteries and the dead — and it is achieved almost entirely by describing what is absent.

Q7. Not for anything he has done to anyone, but for failing to fit the pattern. He is out walking at night with no destination and no purpose beyond air and the wish to see; he has no viewing screen in his house; he is unmarried; and he calls himself a writer, a profession the machine cannot record.

In a society organised around passive, indoor, mass entertainment, each of these is an anomaly, and a system that recognises only categories treats anomaly as pathology. That is why he is not taken to a prison but to the Psychiatric Center for Research on Regressive Tendencies: his offence is classified as illness, and specifically as a tendency to go backwards.

Bradbury's point is that a conformist society does not need to forbid individuality. It only needs to stop having a word for it.

Q8. It is the last image of the story and its most eloquent. As the empty car carries Mead away, it drives past a house with every window lit — the one bright, warm house in the whole dark city — and that house is his own.

Its significance is threefold. It shows what Mead is: a man still living, in a city of the technically alive. It measures what is being taken away — not a life of dissent or rebellion, but simply a lit house and an evening walk. And it is placed at the very end so that the reader is left with the image rather than an argument: the lights are on, nobody is coming back, and the car goes on towards the Psychiatric Center 'through the empty streets'.

Bradbury never comments. The lit windows do the work.

D. Long Answer / Essay Type

Q9. Bradbury wrote the story in 1951, when television was new, and set it a hundred years ahead. Read as prediction it is easy to dismiss: our streets are not empty, and books are still published.

Read as warning it is uncomfortably exact. What Bradbury is describing is not a technology but a habit — the substitution of a screen for the world, and of watching for doing. Every detail is chosen to show a society that has not been oppressed into passivity but has chosen it: nobody has forbidden walking, and there is no tyrant in the story. The one police car exists because there is no crime, and there is no crime because there is nobody out of doors.

The warning has three parts. That comfort can be indistinguishable from death — hence the insistent graveyard imagery, the tombs, the fireflies over graves. That a society which stops needing something will stop having a category for it, so that Mead's profession simply cannot be recorded. And that such a society will not persecute the individual out of malice but will process him out of incomprehension, into a Center for Research on Regressive Tendencies.

The method is a warning's method rather than a prophecy's. Bradbury gives us one man, one night and one arrest, with no explanation of how the world got this way and no suggestion that it must. He simply extends a tendency he could already see and asks the reader to look at where it goes. The lit house at the end is not a prediction that we will lose such houses; it is a reminder that we have them.

E. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q10. The imagery is almost entirely of death, and it is applied with complete consistency.

The houses are tombs; the city is a graveyard; the grey flickering of the viewing screens behind the windows is like fireflies over graves. The people inside are described as sitting tomblike, unmoving, as though the light were washing over corpses. Mead walking is repeatedly likened to a man moving through a cemetery.

Against this Bradbury sets a second, smaller set of images belonging to the natural and the living: the frosty November air, the smell of dust and leaves, Mead's breath in the cold, the buckled pavement with grass growing through it. These appear only where Mead is, and they cease the moment the police car arrives.

The car itself is given a third register — metallic, antiseptic, riveted steel, a little black jail with bars — cold and clean and manufactured, opposed to both the tombs and the leaves.

The effect of arranging the whole story from three sets of images is that Bradbury never has to state his argument. Nobody explains that this society is dead; the vocabulary says so from the first paragraph, and the last image — one house blazing with light in a street of tombs — completes it without a word of comment.
