This is the largest chapter of the Morning Star Map Work section and the one Question 1 draws on most. It is about inference rather than measurement: reading settlement, drainage, land use and communication off the sheet, and connecting them. Marks are given for the connection, not the observation.

A. Short Answer Type

Q1. (a) TANKS AND CANALS — that the land is cultivated and that irrigation is practised, because the natural rainfall is insufficient or unreliable. Their presence implies settled agriculture and a stored water supply.

(b) OPEN SCRUB — thorny bushes and coarse grass scattered over poor ground. It indicates low, seasonal rainfall and thin soil, land that cannot support forest or regular cultivation and is used chiefly for grazing.

(c) STONY WASTE — bare, rocky ground with no soil cover, unfit for cultivation or grazing. It indicates severe erosion or exposed rock, and it is usually empty of settlement.

(d) CAUSEWAY — a raised paved crossing over a stream bed, submerged when the stream is in flood. Its presence indicates that the stream is seasonal, dry for most of the year, so that a bridge would not be justified.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q2. Settlement clusters where the conditions of life are easiest, and on a topographical sheet that means three things in combination.

WATER. Villages lie along perennial streams, beside tanks and reservoirs, or where wells are marked. In a dry region the water source is the single strongest control on where people live.

LEVEL LAND AND GOOD SOIL. Settlement concentrates on flat or gently sloping ground — shown by widely spaced contours — and on the areas marked as cultivated, because that is where crops can be grown. Steep slopes, stony waste and dense forest are avoided.

COMMUNICATIONS. Villages grow along metalled roads, at road junctions, near causeways and river crossings, and beside railway lines, because access to markets matters.

So the thickly settled parts of a sheet are typically the low, level, cultivated ground beside a perennial water source and along a road — and the empty quarters are the steep, stony, forested or waterless ones.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q3. DENDRITIC — the streams branch irregularly like the veins of a leaf or the branches of a tree, joining the main stream at acute angles. It indicates that the underlying rock is of uniform resistance and that there is no strong structural control, so the water has simply taken the line of the general slope. It is the commonest pattern and suggests gently sloping, homogeneous ground.

TRELLIS — tributaries join the main stream nearly at right angles, and the pattern looks rectangular. It indicates alternating bands of hard and soft rock, the streams having eaten out the softer bands, and therefore folded or tilted strata.

RADIAL — streams flow outwards in all directions from a central high point, like the spokes of a wheel. It indicates a conical hill, a dome or a volcanic peak.

(Two further patterns worth knowing: CENTRIPETAL, where streams converge inwards into a basin or depression; and DERANGED, an irregular pattern with no clear system, indicating disturbed ground.)

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q4. PHYSICAL FACTORS. Availability of water, which is the first requirement — hence settlement along perennial streams, round tanks and where wells are numerous. Relief: level or gently sloping land is settled, steep slopes are not. Soil: fertile cultivable land attracts settlement, stony waste repels it. Climate: areas of adequate rainfall support denser settlement than arid ones. Natural vegetation: dense forest is generally avoided, and in a reserved forest settlement is prohibited altogether.

ECONOMIC AND HUMAN FACTORS. Means of communication — roads, railways, causeways and river crossings — along which villages string out. Availability of cultivable land and of grazing. Trade and markets, which produce larger nucleated settlements. Mineral deposits and industry. Safety and defence, which historically placed settlements on defensible sites.

In interpreting a sheet, the examiner expects the candidate to link an absence of settlement to a specific cause visible on the map — a reserved forest, a stretch of stony waste, steep contours, or the lack of any water source.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q5. NUCLEATED or COMPACT — houses clustered closely together in a single compact block, usually round a water source, a temple or a road junction, with the fields lying outside. It indicates a settled agricultural community and adequate water in one place.

DISPERSED or SCATTERED — houses standing singly or in twos and threes, spread widely over an area. It occurs in hilly, forested or infertile country where cultivable patches are separated, and where each family lives on its own land.

LINEAR — houses strung out in a line along a road, a railway, a canal, a river bank or the foot of a ridge. The controlling feature is the line itself.

RADIAL — houses arranged outwards along several roads or paths that meet at a central point, such as a road junction, a temple or a tank, so that the settlement resembles the spokes of a wheel.

When a question asks you to COMPARE the patterns in two grid squares, name the pattern in each and then give the reason for the difference — nearly always water, relief or a road.

B. Topographical Sheet Questions (from the papers)

Q6. (a) METHOD. Name the pattern in each square — nucleated, dispersed, linear or radial — and then account for the difference from what else is visible in those squares. A nucleated settlement usually sits beside a tank, a well or a road junction; a dispersed one is found on broken, stony or hilly ground, or where the cultivable patches are separated. The marks are for the explanation, not for the two names.

(b) METHOD. Compare the grid references. 3100 has easting 31, northing 00; 3298 has easting 32, northing 98. The easting increases, so the movement is towards the east; the northing decreases from 00 to 98, so it is towards the south. The direction is therefore south-east.

That arithmetic — easting up means east, northing up means north — will answer every direction question on the sheet without any need to look at the map at all.

Q7. (a) METHOD. Look for the transport symbols in each square and name them exactly as the index gives them — metalled road, unmetalled road, cart track, pack track, footpath, railway line. The answer must state the difference, for example 'in 6435 there is a metalled road, whereas in 6236 there is only a cart track', and it is worth adding what that implies: a metalled road indicates greater traffic and better connection with the outside world.

(b) METHOD. Natural features are those not made by man — a hill, ridge, spur, valley, seasonal or perennial stream, lake, forest, scrub, sandy area, broken ground. Man-made features are settlements, roads, railways, tanks, wells, canals, causeways, temples, embankments and boundary pillars. Distinguishing the two is asked in almost every paper (see ICSE 2025 Q1(v)(a) and 2026 Improvement Q1(v)(a) for the man-made version), so keep the two lists clear in your mind.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q8. (a) Because it is a RESERVED FOREST, marked as such on the sheet. A reserved forest is protected by law: the felling of trees, the grazing of cattle, the collection of firewood and any commercial exploitation are prohibited, and people living nearby have no right to its produce. The purpose is conservation of the forest and of the wildlife and soil it protects.

(b) A fireline is a strip of land cleared of all trees, grass and undergrowth, cut through the forest — appearing on the sheet as a straight open line. Its purpose is to stop the spread of forest fires: with no fuel along the strip, a fire that reaches it cannot cross, and the blaze is confined to one compartment of the forest. Firelines also serve as inspection paths for forest staff.

Official Answer

Q9. METHOD. Trace the road between the two settlements and list the symbols for services and amenities that lie along it. On a Survey of India sheet the amenities to look for are: a post office (P.O.), a post and telegraph office (P.T.O.), a police station (P.S.), a dispensary or hospital, a school, a rest house or dak bungalow, a causeway or bridge, a metalled road itself, and electric transmission lines.

Any two that actually appear along that route will earn the marks. Note that the question says 'modern amenities developed for the villagers' — so a temple or a well, though marked, would not be accepted; the examiner wants public services.

Q10. That the inhabitants of the area are Hindus, and that the temple is the religious and social centre of the settlement — the place where the community gathers for worship and for festivals, and often a landmark and meeting point for the surrounding villages.

On a topographical sheet the presence of a temple, a mosque or a church is read as evidence of the religion of the people living there, and a large or isolated temple may also indicate a place of pilgrimage attracting visitors from outside the area. That is the 'social significance' the question is asking for — not the architecture of the building.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q11. METHOD — and this is the model for every 'why is there no settlement here' question. Look at that quarter of the sheet and identify what is physically present, then state the consequence.

The usual causes, any of which may apply: it is covered by RESERVED FOREST, where settlement is prohibited by law; it is STONY WASTE or broken ground, with no soil to cultivate; the CONTOURS ARE CLOSE TOGETHER, showing steep and rugged relief unsuitable for building or farming; or there is NO PERENNIAL WATER SOURCE — no wells, no tanks, only broken blue seasonal streams.

A complete answer names the feature seen on the map and draws the conclusion from it. Naming the feature alone, or giving the conclusion without the evidence, earns half the mark at best.

C. Long Answer / Application Type

Q12. CLIMATE. Low and highly seasonal rainfall. The evidence is the drainage: almost all the streams are drawn as broken blue lines, meaning they carry water only in the monsoon and are dry for the rest of the year. This is confirmed by the vegetation — open scrub, scattered scrub and stretches of stony waste rather than forest — and by the number of causeways, which are built only where a stream is dry enough, for long enough, that a bridge is not worth the cost.

WATER SUPPLY. Because the surface water fails, the people depend on stored and underground water. The sheets are covered with the symbols for wells and for tanks and reservoirs, the latter formed by throwing a bund across a valley to hold the monsoon flow. Settlements cluster tightly around these sources, and the largest perennial body on the sheet is invariably a man-made reservoir rather than a natural lake.

ECONOMY. Chiefly subsistence agriculture and grazing. Cultivation is confined to the level ground near the water sources and along the streams, and the extent of open scrub and stony waste shows how much of the land supports only grazing. The settlements are small and mostly nucleated round a water point, with a few larger ones at road junctions. Metalled roads are few, cart tracks many, which indicates limited trade with the outside; the amenities marked — a post office, a school, a dispensary at the larger villages — suggest small service centres rather than towns.

The general picture is of a semi-arid region in which the whole pattern of life is organised around the storage of water.
