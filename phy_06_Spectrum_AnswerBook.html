D. Long Answer Questions

Q51. Cause of Dispersion of White Light:

White light is polychromatic — it is composed of seven colours (VIBGYOR), each with a different wavelength.

When white light enters a prism, each colour travels at a different speed inside the glass because the refractive index of the prism material differs for different wavelengths:
• Violet light (shortest λ): highest refractive index → slows the most → deviates the most.
• Red light (longest λ): lowest refractive index → slows the least → deviates the least.

At the first surface, both deviation AND dispersion occur. Different colours separate by different amounts. At the second surface, further deviation (but no additional separation reversal) occurs. The result is that the seven colours emerge at different angles, forming a continuous band of colours called the spectrum.

In a rectangular glass slab, the two surfaces are parallel, so the dispersion at the first surface is exactly cancelled at the second surface — the emerging beam is white. A prism, with its non-parallel surfaces, does not cancel the dispersion, hence a spectrum is produced.

Q52. Dispersion of White Light by a Prism:

When a narrow beam of white light is incident on one face of a glass prism:
• At the FIRST surface: Both dispersion (splitting into colours) AND deviation (bending) occur. Each colour of white light refracts by a different amount because the refractive index differs for each colour. Violet is refracted (and deviated) the most; red the least.
• At the SECOND surface: Only further deviation occurs. The colours are already separated; the second surface bends each colour further but does not produce additional colour separation.

The emergent beam consists of a band of colours — Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, Red — spread out in order, called the visible spectrum. Violet is closest to the base of the prism (deviates most) and red is furthest from the base (deviates least).

Summary:
• First surface: dispersion + deviation
• Second surface: deviation only

Q54. Infrared Radiations:

Infrared radiations are electromagnetic radiations with wavelengths longer than that of visible red light (approximately 700 nm to 1 mm). They are produced by all warm objects and are also called 'heat radiations' or 'thermal radiation' because they cause a heating effect.

Detection:
• Thermopile or bolometer: These instruments absorb infrared radiation and show a rise in temperature (detected as electrical voltage in a thermopile).
• Photography using special infrared-sensitive photographic films.
• A blackened thermometer bulb shows a rapid rise when exposed to infrared radiation.

Uses:
1. Infrared photography: Used for taking photographs in fog or darkness, and in remote sensing from satellites.
2. Medical: Used in physiotherapy to provide warmth to sore muscles and joints (heat treatment).
3. Remote control: TV remote controls and other electrical gadgets use infrared radiation for wireless operation.

Q55. Ultraviolet Radiations:

Ultraviolet (UV) radiations are electromagnetic radiations with wavelengths shorter than visible violet light (approximately 10 nm to 400 nm). They are produced by very hot sources such as the Sun, mercury vapour lamps, and electric arcs.

Detection:
• Photographic film: UV radiation blackens silver chloride or silver bromide-based photographic film.
• Fluorescent substances: Certain materials (like quinine sulphate) glow (fluoresce) when exposed to UV radiation.
• UV-sensitive photoelectric cells.

Uses:
1. Sterilisation: UV lamps are used to sterilise water, air, and surgical instruments by killing bacteria and microorganisms.
2. Security: Used in banknote verification — UV light reveals hidden security markings on currency notes.
3. Study of molecular structure: UV spectroscopy is used in chemistry and biology to study molecular bonds and compounds.

Q56. The Tyndall Effect:

When a beam of white light passes through a medium containing fine particles (colloidal particles or suspended particles much smaller than the wavelength of light), the particles scatter the shorter wavelengths (blue, violet) much more than the longer wavelengths (red, orange). This phenomenon is called the Tyndall effect.

The intensity of scattered light I ∝ 1/λ⁴ (Rayleigh's law). This means violet/blue light (λ ≈ 400–450 nm) is scattered approximately 5–16 times more than red light (λ ≈ 700 nm).

Blue Colour of the Sky:
Sunlight enters the Earth's atmosphere and is scattered by tiny air molecules (N₂, O₂) and fine dust particles. Since blue light is scattered much more than other colours, the light reaching our eyes from all directions of the sky (other than directly from the Sun) is predominantly blue. Hence, the sky appears blue.

Reddish Sun at Sunrise/Sunset:
At sunrise and sunset, sunlight travels a much longer path through the atmosphere (oblique path). By the time it reaches us, most of the blue, violet, and green light has been scattered away. The remaining light — predominantly orange and red (longest wavelengths, least scattered) — reaches our eyes directly, making the Sun appear red or orange.

Q57. Formation of a Rainbow:

A rainbow is a natural spectrum of sunlight produced by the combined effect of refraction, dispersion, and total internal reflection inside raindrops suspended in the atmosphere.

Process of formation:
1. Refraction and Dispersion (Entry): When sunlight enters a spherical raindrop, it refracts at the surface and undergoes dispersion — each colour bends by a different amount (violet most, red least).

2. Total Internal Reflection (Inside the drop): The dispersed light strikes the inner (back) surface of the raindrop. The angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle for water–air interface, so total internal reflection occurs. The light is reflected back inside the drop.

3. Refraction on Exit: When the reflected light tries to exit through the front surface of the drop, it refracts again, causing further separation of colours.

Result: The observer on the ground sees a circular arc of colours in the sky. Red appears on the outer edge (deviated ~42°) and violet on the inner edge (deviated ~40°) of the primary rainbow. A secondary rainbow (colours reversed) may also form due to two internal reflections inside the raindrop.

Q58. The Electromagnetic Spectrum:

The electromagnetic spectrum is the complete range of all electromagnetic radiations arranged in order of increasing wavelength (or decreasing frequency). All EM waves travel at the speed of light (c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s) in vacuum and differ only in wavelength and frequency.

Major regions in order of increasing wavelength:

1. Gamma rays (λ < 0.01 Å): Highest energy, most penetrating. Use: Cancer treatment (radiation therapy), sterilisation of medical equipment.

2. X-rays (0.01 Å – 10 nm): High penetrating power through soft tissue. Use: Medical imaging — detection of bone fractures.

3. Ultraviolet (UV) rays (10 nm – 400 nm): Detected by fluorescent materials and photographic film. Use: Sterilisation; causes tanning and sunburn.

4. Visible light (400 nm – 700 nm): The only part detectable by the human eye (VIBGYOR).

5. Infrared (IR) rays (700 nm – 1 mm): Heat radiation, detected by thermopile. Use: Physiotherapy, remote controls, night-vision cameras.

6. Microwaves (1 mm – 30 cm): Detected by electronic sensors. Use: Satellite communication, cooking (microwave ovens).

7. Radio waves (> 30 cm): Longest wavelength, lowest energy. Use: AM/FM radio broadcasting, television transmission.

Q59. Variation of Angle of Deviation with Angle of Incidence:

As the angle of incidence (i) increases from 0° to 90°, the angle of deviation (δ) first decreases, reaches a minimum value (called the angle of minimum deviation, δₘ), and then increases again.

Key features of the graph:
• At a very small angle of incidence, deviation is large.
• As i increases, δ decreases to a minimum (δₘ) — this is the minimum deviation position.
• Beyond the minimum, as i further increases towards 90°, δ increases again.
• The graph is a U-shaped (parabolic) curve with a clear minimum.
• For a given prism, each colour has its own δₘ — violet has the largest δₘ and red has the smallest.

At minimum deviation:
• The refracted ray inside the prism is parallel to the base.
• The angle of incidence = angle of emergence.

Q64. Given: Equilateral prism → A = 60°; angle of incidence i = 45°; minimum deviation position.

(a) At minimum deviation, the refracted ray inside the prism is parallel to the base, so:
Angle of refraction r = A/2 = 60°/2 = 30°

(b) At minimum deviation:
i = (A + δₘ)/2
⇒ 45° = (60° + δₘ)/2
⇒ 90° = 60° + δₘ
⇒ δₘ = 30°

(c) Refractive index:
μ = sin[(A + δₘ)/2] / sin(A/2)
= sin[(60° + 30°)/2] / sin(60°/2)
= sin 45° / sin 30°
= (1/√2) / (1/2)
= 2/√2
= √2 ≈ 1.41

The refractive index of the prism material is √2 ≈ 1.41.
