B. Short Answer Type

Q4. A map is a representation, on a flat surface and to a chosen scale, of the whole or a part of the earth's surface, showing selected features by means of conventional signs and symbols.

Its essential features are: a title, which states what the map shows; a scale, which fixes the relation between distance on the map and distance on the ground; direction, usually shown by a north line or an arrow; a key or index, which explains the conventional symbols and colour tints used; and a grid or system of reference, by which any point can be located precisely.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q5. The key, or index or legend, is the table printed in the margin of the map explaining the meaning of every conventional sign, symbol, abbreviation and colour tint used on it.

It is necessary because a map cannot show features pictorially. Space is limited and the features must be reduced to standard symbols — a broken blue line for a seasonal stream, a small red circle for a village, particular tints for forest and cultivated land — and these are arbitrary. Without the key the symbols cannot be interpreted, and on a Survey of India sheet the index at the bottom is what the candidate is expected to use in the examination.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q6. Maps are classified in two ways.

By scale: large-scale maps, which show a small area in great detail — cadastral maps of individual fields and plots, and topographical maps such as the Survey of India sheets; and small-scale maps, which show a large area with less detail — wall maps and atlas maps.

By purpose or content: physical maps, showing relief, drainage, climate, soils and natural vegetation; and cultural or political maps, showing man-made features such as boundaries, settlements, roads, railways, population and industries. Thematic or distribution maps, showing a single subject such as rainfall or the distribution of a crop, form a further class.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q7. Topographical maps are large-scale maps that show, in detail and with accuracy, both the natural and the man-made features of a small area — relief by contours, drainage, vegetation, settlements, roads, railways, boundaries and land use. In India they are prepared and published by the Survey of India, and the sheets used in the ICSE examination (for example G43S7 and G43S10) are drawn to a scale of 1:50,000.

Three uses: they allow the relief and slope of an area to be read and understood, so that engineers can plan roads, railways, dams and canals; they show the distribution of settlements, water sources and land use, so that planners and administrators can judge where people live and why; and they are essential to soldiers, surveyors, travellers and trekkers for finding routes and fixing positions on the ground.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q8. The system works by progressive subdivision, and each stage adds one element to the number.

The world is first divided into zones of 4 degrees of latitude by 6 degrees of longitude, each identified by a letter and a number — India and the adjacent countries fall in a series of which NG-43 is one.

Each such 4 by 6 degree rectangle is subdivided into 24 squares of 1 degree by 1 degree, each indicated by a letter running first eastwards and then southwards, beginning with A. Abu (72.7156 degrees E, 24.5926 degrees N) falls in square S of G-43, giving G-43S.

Each 1 degree square is then subdivided into 16 squares of 15 minutes by 15 minutes, numbered serially, increasing first southwards and then eastwards, beginning with 1. The sheet containing Abu is number 10 in that series.

The complete number of the sheet is therefore G-43S-10 — which is exactly the sheet supplied with the ICSE 2025 paper.

C. Topographical Sheet Questions (from the papers)

Q9. (a) METHOD. Find the symbol for a post office (P.O.) at Sanwara. Read the eastings along the bottom margin: take the printed two-figure easting of the line to the LEFT of the feature, then estimate tenths of the square eastwards to the feature — that gives three figures. Then read the northings up the side margin: take the printed northing of the line BELOW the feature, then estimate tenths northwards — three more figures. Write eastings first, then northings, six digits with no space. 'Along the corridor, then up the stairs.'

(b) METHOD. The general slope is read from two things together: the direction in which contour values decrease, and the direction in which the rivers flow — water always runs downhill, so the streams point down the slope. State the slope as a compass direction, for example 'the land slopes generally from north-east to south-west', and support it by naming a high contour or spot height on one side and a lower one on the other.

NOTE — the specific answers depend on the sheet, which is not reproduced here. Work them on the extract in the book's appendix.

Q10. (a) The scale of the Survey of India sheets used in the ICSE examination is 1:50,000 — that is, 2 centimetres on the map represent 1 kilometre on the ground. It is printed both as a representative fraction and as a statement, and there is also a linear (graphical) scale in the margin.

(b) A causeway is a raised, paved crossing built across a stream bed, over which a road passes. It allows traffic to cross when the stream is dry or shallow, and is submerged when the stream is in flood.

The presence of many causeways is itself significant and is regularly examined: it shows that the streams of the area are seasonal — dry for most of the year and carrying water only in the rains — so that a full bridge is not economically justified.

Q11. (a) Because it is a man-made reservoir or tank, formed by building an embankment or bund across a stream so that the rainy-season flow is stored and retained through the dry months. On the sheet it is shown as a permanent body of blue with a bund marked across the valley. In a region of seasonal streams, storage is the only way to hold water into the dry season.

(b) Wells — shown by the conventional symbol for a well — are perennial, because they draw on ground water rather than surface flow. Canals, where present, are also perennial. The distinction the examiner is testing is between surface water, which in this region dries up, and stored or underground water, which does not.

Q12. (a) METHOD. Find the highest point on the extract — usually a triangulated height or a spot height, printed as a dot with a figure beside it — and note its value in metres. Then read the value of the contour line passing through grid square 3203. Subtract the second from the first, and give the answer in metres. Show the subtraction: examiners give credit for the working even where the reading is slightly out.

(b) It is a cart track, or more precisely the symbol for a track or a path — the examiner's intention here is to test whether the candidate reads the index at the foot of the sheet rather than guessing. A single black line of this kind on a Survey of India sheet denotes a route of communication (a cart track, pack track or footpath, according to the exact symbol), not a boundary or a contour.

D. Long Answer / Application Type

Q13. A method, followed in the same order every time, is worth several marks on this question.

1. Before reading a single question, read the SHEET. Note its number, its scale (1:50,000), the contour interval, and the north direction. Then read the INDEX at the foot of the extract, which explains every symbol and colour tint you will be asked about.

2. Get the general picture. Look at the relief — where the high ground is, which way the contour values fall. Look at the drainage — the direction the rivers flow, whether the streams are continuous blue (perennial) or broken blue (seasonal), and the pattern they make. Look at the settlements — where they cluster, and why. Two minutes spent here will answer half the questions before they are asked.

3. Note what the drainage tells you about the climate. Broken blue streams, many causeways, tanks and wells, and scrub or open scrub vegetation together mean a region of low and seasonal rainfall. That single deduction is the basis of a great many questions.

4. Answer in the order set, and answer only what is asked. A question worth one mark wants one fact.

5. For grid references, write eastings before northings, and count tenths carefully. For distances, use the twine supplied for a winding road and a straight edge for a direct distance, then convert using the scale — 2 cm to 1 km. For area, count the whole grid squares: each is 1 square kilometre.

6. Where a question asks 'why' — why the settlements are absent from a corner, why the forest cannot be used, why there are causeways — the answer must connect two things visible on the map. Marks are given for the connection, not for the observation.
