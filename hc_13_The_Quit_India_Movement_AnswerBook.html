C. Short Answer Type

Q11. (i) The failure of the Cripps Mission. In March 1942 Sir Stafford Cripps offered Dominion Status after the war, with the right of any province to stay out of the Union, but no immediate transfer of power and no Indian control of defence. The Congress rejected it, and its failure showed that the British would concede nothing of substance while they held the country.

(ii) The Japanese threat. Japan had overrun Malaya, Singapore and Burma, and stood on India's eastern frontier. The manner of the British retreat from Burma — Europeans evacuated, Indians left to walk out — persuaded Indians that they could not rely on Britain to defend them, and Gandhi argued that if the British left, India would cease to be a target and could defend herself.

(iii) India had been declared a belligerent in the war without any consultation of Indian opinion, and was suffering acutely for it — shortages, steeply rising prices, and the requisitioning of boats and rice in Bengal — while the government's arrogance towards Indians remained unaltered. To these may be added the failure of every constitutional effort since 1935 and the conviction that the moment of Britain's weakness was the moment to act.

Official Answer

Q12. (i) It settled the question of whether Britain could hold India. The government suppressed the movement, but only by using the army against its own civil population on a scale not seen since 1857, and it emerged knowing that it ruled a country in which it had no consent. After 1942 the British question was no longer whether to leave India but when and on what terms.

(ii) It showed the depth and the spontaneity of the national demand. The entire leadership was arrested within hours, before the movement had even begun; yet it spread on its own, through students, peasants, workers and women, with strikes, hartals, the cutting of telegraph lines and attacks on police stations, and with parallel governments running whole districts at Ballia, Tamluk and Satara. A movement that could run itself without leaders was proof that independence was the demand of the people and not of a party.

(iii) It brought forward a new and more radical leadership — Jayaprakash Narayan, Ram Manohar Lohia, Aruna Asaf Ali — and gave the socialists within the Congress a prominence they had not had, which shaped the politics of the final years and of independent India.

Against this must be set one consequence that told the other way: the Muslim League kept out of the movement, and used the three years of Congress leaders' imprisonment to build its organisation in the provinces almost unopposed, so that it emerged in 1945 far stronger than it had entered — with what results the events of 1947 showed.

Official Answer

Q13. Because it had no leaders to direct it. In the early hours of 9 August 1942, within a few hours of the resolution being passed, the government arrested Gandhi, Nehru, Patel, Azad and the whole Congress Working Committee, and declared the Congress illegal. The movement therefore began with its entire leadership already in prison and no plan of action published.

What followed was the people's own. Strikes and hartals closed the towns; crowds attacked the visible symbols of British authority — railway stations, post offices, police stations — and cut telegraph wires; students left their colleges; and in some districts the administration simply collapsed and was replaced by parallel governments run by Indians, at Ballia, Tamluk and Satara. Underground leaders such as Jayaprakash Narayan and Aruna Asaf Ali gave what direction they could, but no central body was controlling events.

It was also, for that reason, the least disciplined of the movements: without Gandhi's restraining hand it was in many places violent, which he had never intended.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q14. With Japan advancing and pressure from the United States and from British opinion to conciliate India, the British government sent Sir Stafford Cripps to Delhi in March 1942. He offered: Dominion Status for India after the war; an elected constituent assembly to frame the constitution; the right of any province unwilling to join the Indian Union to keep its separate status; and, meanwhile, the association of Indian leaders with the war effort.

The Congress rejected it on three grounds. Nothing was offered now — power was to be transferred only after the war, and Gandhi called it a post-dated cheque on a crashing bank. Defence, the one department that mattered while the country was under threat, was to remain wholly in British hands. And the provision allowing provinces to stay out carried within it the principle of partition, which the Congress would not concede. The Muslim League rejected the proposals too, for the opposite reason — that they did not concede Pakistan outright.

E. Source-Based / Picture-Based Questions

Q17. (i) The Quit India Resolution, passed by the All India Congress Committee on 8 August 1942.
(ii) That the struggle was now final and there would be no retreat from it. In his words, 'We shall either free India or die in the attempt; we shall not live to see the perpetuation of our slavery.' Every Indian was to consider himself a free man from that moment and act accordingly, and each was to be his own leader — but the struggle was still to be non-violent, the dying being one's own and not the killing of others.
(iii) Because the movement was left without any leadership within hours of its birth. The government arrested Gandhi and the entire Congress Working Committee in the early morning of 9 August and outlawed the Congress, so the campaign began with no plan issued and no one to direct it. It spread nonetheless — strikes and hartals, attacks on police stations, post offices and railway lines, students leaving colleges — and in Ballia, Tamluk and Satara the British administration collapsed altogether and Indians ran parallel governments in its place. A movement that grew and sustained itself with its leaders in prison had indeed taken on a life of its own.

Official Answer

Q18. (i) The All India Congress Committee session at the Gowalia Tank Maidan, Bombay, which passed the Quit India Resolution on 8 August 1942, demanding an immediate end to British rule in India.
(ii) In the early hours of 9 August, before the movement could begin, the government arrested Gandhi, Nehru, Patel, Maulana Azad and the whole Congress Working Committee, and declared the Congress an unlawful organisation. The consequence was that the movement became leaderless and therefore spontaneous: it spread of its own accord across the country through strikes, hartals and attacks on the symbols of British authority, with parallel governments established at Ballia, Tamluk and Satara — but, lacking Gandhi's restraining hand, it was in many places violent, which he had not intended.
