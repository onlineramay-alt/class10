A. Multiple Choice Questions

Q1. (b) 'Et tu, Brute? Then fall, Caesar!' — 'You too, Brutus?' He resists his assassins until he sees Brutus among them, and then ceases to struggle. 'Speak, hands, for me!' is Casca's line as he strikes the first blow.

Q2. (b) The northern star. Refusing to repeal Publius Cimber's banishment, he declares: 'But I am constant as the northern star, / Of whose true-fix'd and resting quality / There is no fellow in the firmament.' It is the last and greatest of his assertions of inflexibility, and it is spoken moments before he dies.

Q3. (b) 'An honourable man' — repeated with increasing irony until the crowd hears it as its opposite. Antony has sworn not to blame the conspirators, and he keeps the letter of that promise while destroying them with it.

Q5. (b) EULOGY — a formal speech of praise for someone who has died. Antony's funeral oration ('Friends, Romans, countrymen...') is delivered over Caesar's body and, under cover of praising the dead man and repeatedly calling Brutus 'honourable', turns the mob against the conspirators.

Q6. (d) BROKEN PENCILS ARE POINTLESS. Both turn on a PUN — a play on two senses (or two identical sounds) of a word. Antony plays 'hart' (deer) against 'heart', and 'pointless' means both 'without a point' and 'without purpose'. (a) is onomatopoeia, (b) is a metaphor, (c) is imagery.

Official Answer

Q7. (b) STATEMENT II IS THE CAUSE OF STATEMENT I. Antony holds the will back until the crowd is desperate to hear it; when he finally reads that Caesar has left every citizen seventy-five drachmas and his private gardens, gratitude tips into fury, and the mob rushes off to burn the conspirators' houses, ready to 'kiss dead Caesar's wounds'.

Official Answer

Q8. (d) IRRATIONAL. The crowd swings completely from cheering Brutus ('Let him be Caesar') to howling for his death within minutes, is moved by feeling rather than argument, and finally murders Cinna the poet merely for sharing a conspirator's name. Shakespeare presents the mob as fickle, violent and easily led.

Official Answer

Q9. (d) ANTONY COMPARING THE CONSPIRATORS TO APES AND HOUNDS. That is a direct insult (a simile of contempt), not irony — he means exactly what he says. The other three all say the opposite of what is meant or produce an outcome opposite to what is intended: (a) verbal irony, (b) verbal/dramatic irony, (c) situational irony (the crowd wants to crown the man who killed a would-be king).

B. Extract-Based / Context Questions

Q10. (i) Metellus Cimber has knelt to beg for the repeal of his banished brother, Publius Cimber. Brutus and Cassius have both knelt in support — which is, as the audience knows, the pre-arranged signal and the means of surrounding Caesar.

(ii) The northern star — the Pole Star — is the one fixed point in a sky full of moving lights. Caesar means that while other men may be shifted by flattery or entreaty, he alone is immovable, and that this constancy is what distinguishes him from all other men as the Pole Star is distinguished from the countless other stars.

(iii) Cinna and Decius kneel, and then Casca. Casca strikes the first blow with 'Speak, hands, for me!' and the other conspirators follow. Caesar dies with 'Et tu, Brute? Then fall, Caesar!'

(iv) 'Et tu, Brute? Then fall, Caesar!' — 'You too, Brutus?' Their significance is that they show Caesar dying not of his wounds but of the discovery. He has been resisting his attackers; the sight of Brutus among them ends the resistance, because betrayal by the man he loved makes life not worth defending. Two words of Latin do what no speech could: they turn a political assassination into a personal grief.

(v) The speech is Caesar at his most magnificent and his most fatal, and Shakespeare places it deliberately in the last moments of his life. The claim to be constant as the northern star is superb, and there is truth in it — Caesar is not a man who can be begged into changing his mind.

But the audience has watched him change his mind twice already: about Calpurnia's warning in Act II, and about going to the Senate at all. The constancy is a public posture, and the boast is heard by men who are kneeling in front of him only in order to get close enough to stab. There is a terrible irony in a man asserting that he cannot be moved by kneeling while the kneeling itself is the trap.

The effect is to make the death tragic rather than merely deserved. Caesar dies at the height of his self-belief, and the play immediately proves both that his greatness was real and that it was not what he thought it was.

Q11. (i) Brutus has granted permission over Cassius's objection, on three conditions: that Antony shall speak in Caesar's funeral by Brutus's leave, and shall say so; that he shall not blame the conspirators but speak all good he can devise of Caesar; and that he shall speak after Brutus, from the same pulpit. Antony accepts every condition and keeps every one of them to the letter.

(ii) Because the crowd is hostile: Brutus has just spoken and they are crying 'Let him be Caesar' of Brutus himself. To praise Caesar openly would be to lose them in a sentence. By disclaiming any intention to praise, Antony disarms them and buys the silence in which he can work. It is also literally true and quite false at the same time, which is the method of the entire speech.

(iii) Three proofs, each a fact rather than an argument. Caesar brought many captives home to Rome, whose ransoms did the general coffers fill — which does not look like ambition. When the poor have cried, Caesar hath wept; ambition should be made of sterner stuff. And Antony himself thrice presented him a kingly crown at the Lupercal, which he did thrice refuse. He then produces the will, which leaves seventy-five drachmas to every Roman citizen and his private arbours and orchards on this side Tiber as public pleasure-grounds — the most damaging proof of all, because it is a benefit the crowd can count.

(iv) Its effect is cumulative and destructive. At first it appears to be a genuine concession, and it keeps Antony's promise not to blame the conspirators. But it is repeated after each item of evidence pointing the other way, so that the audience is invited each time to weigh Brutus's assertion against a fact — and each time the fact wins. By the fourth repetition the words have been emptied of meaning and refilled with their opposite; the crowd itself begins to say 'they were traitors: honourable men!' Antony never once accuses Brutus, and yet leaves him convicted.

(v) The crowd is turned completely. They cry for revenge, seek fire, and go to burn the conspirators' houses; Brutus and Cassius, we hear at the end of the scene, are riding like madmen through the gates of Rome. Antony's own comment is the coldest line in the play: 'Now let it work. Mischief, thou art afoot, / Take thou what course thou wilt!'

What it shows about the crowd is what Act I prepared us for. These are the same people who cheered Pompey and then cheered Caesar's triumph over Pompey's sons, and who have just been cheering Brutus. They are moved not by argument but by whoever spoke last and best, and the next scene — the tearing to pieces of Cinna the poet for the crime of sharing a name — shows what that fickleness costs when it is roused.

Q12. (i) To OCTAVIUS'S SERVANT. Antony tells him: ride back to Octavius and report what has happened; warn him that it is not yet safe to enter Rome; wait a little, though, and first help carry Caesar's body to the market-place, so that the servant can then tell Octavius how the people react to Antony's funeral speech and 'the state of things'.

(ii) Rome is 'dangerous' because Caesar has just been assassinated and the city is in shock and confusion, with no one yet in control. It is especially unsafe for OCTAVIUS because he is Caesar's great-nephew, adopted son and named HEIR, and so a natural target for the conspirators and for any mob violence.

(iii) Any three: a curse will fall on the limbs of men; fierce civil war ('domestic fury and fierce civil strife') will engulf all Italy; blood and destruction will become so common that mothers will only smile to see their children 'quarter'd with the hands of war'; and Caesar's spirit, 'ranging for revenge', will 'let slip the dogs of war'.

(iv) BRUTUS thought Antony harmless — 'but a limb of Caesar', a man given to sport and revelry who would be powerless once Caesar was dead, and so he let him live and let him speak. CASSIUS thought Antony DANGEROUS and argued against both decisions. CASSIUS was right: Antony's single funeral speech turns the whole city against the conspirators and drives Brutus and Cassius out of Rome.

(v) Antony shows himself a SHREWD POLITICIAN and superb ORATOR, masking his purpose with professions of grief and modesty and playing the crowd with perfect timing. He is also LOYAL to Caesar and courageous in facing the armed conspirators. But Act IV reveals a colder, more RUTHLESS side: he coolly marks down men to be killed (including his own nephew), plans to cut down Caesar's bequests to the people, and speaks with contempt of his fellow-triumvir Lepidus as a mere pack-animal 'to be sent on errands'.

Q13. (i) The NERVII were a fierce Gallic tribe whom Caesar had defeated in one of his hardest-fought battles. Antony names CASSIUS, CASCA and BRUTUS while pointing to the rents in the cloak so that the crowd will picture each specific 'honourable' friend driving in his dagger — turning an abstract 'assassination' into a vivid, personal betrayal and rousing the mob's rage.

(ii) Any three: Caesar brought many captives home to Rome whose ransoms 'did the general coffers fill'; he wept when the poor cried, showing he was not hard-hearted; he three times refused the crown that Antony offered him at the Lupercal; and he made a will leaving his wealth and gardens to the citizens.

(iii) Antony calls Brutus 'Caesar's angel' and well-beloved because Caesar had loved and trusted Brutus above all the others. That is exactly why his blow is 'the most unkindest cut of all' — it was the INGRATITUDE and treachery of his dearest friend, more than the wound itself, that broke Caesar's heart: 'This was the most unkindest cut of all; / For when the noble Caesar saw him stab, / Ingratitude... quite vanquish'd him.'

(iv) (1) Brutus speaks in PROSE and appeals to REASON; Antony speaks in VERSE and appeals to EMOTION. (2) Brutus is brief, plain and abstract ('honour', 'freedom'); Antony is concrete and dramatic — the cloak, the wounds, the will, the body itself. (3) Brutus asks the crowd to trust his honour and take his word; Antony pretends to trust the crowd's judgement and lets them 'discover' for themselves that they have been wronged, while claiming he is 'no orator, as Brutus is'.

(v) The scene highlights the mob's FICKLENESS and irrationality — it cheers Brutus, then within minutes screams for his death. Two traits of Antony: he is a MASTERLY MANIPULATOR (he withholds the will, uses the body as a prop, and stage-manages every pause for maximum effect); and he is a DEVOTED FRIEND to Caesar (his grief, though used, is real — 'My heart is in the coffin there with Caesar').

Q14. (i) They are in the CAPITOL / the Senate House at Rome. 'PUISSANT' means POWERFUL, mighty. 'Metellus Cimber throws before thy seat a humble heart' means that Metellus KNEELS in humble supplication before Caesar's chair, begging a favour — the repeal of his banished brother Publius Cimber's sentence. (iii) TREBONIUS was to draw MARK ANTONY away and keep him out of the Capitol so that he could not defend Caesar. CASCA was to strike the FIRST BLOW ('Speak, hands, for me!'). Cassius grows nervous because POPILIUS LENA wishes him success 'in your enterprise to-day' and then goes and speaks privately to Caesar — making Cassius fear the plot has been discovered.

(iv) Artemidorus wants Caesar to READ HIS SCROLL at once, because it names the conspirators and warns him of the plot on his life. Caesar refuses to give it priority: 'What touches us ourself shall be last served' — he will not put his own safety before public business, and so the warning is never read.

(v) A PERSONAL RESPONSE; either side may be defended. For CAESAR: he is murdered by men he trusted, on a false pretext, while doing his public duty; his last words, 'Et tu, Brute?', make the betrayal unbearably personal; and the chaos and civil war that follow show that killing him solved nothing. For the CONSPIRATORS (at least Brutus): they act, or believe they act, to stop one man making himself king and ending the Republic; Brutus in particular kills 'not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more', and sacrifices his own peace of mind to do it. C. Short Answer Type

Q15. He first sends a servant ahead with a message of extraordinary humility: if Brutus will vouchsafe that Antony may safely come to him and be resolved how Caesar hath deserved to lie in death, then Mark Antony shall not love Caesar dead so well as Brutus living, and will follow his fortunes with all true faith.

He then comes in person, and does three things at once. He grieves openly over the body — 'O mighty Caesar! dost thou lie so low?' — and offers his own life on the spot, telling them there is no hour so fit as Caesar's death hour, nor no instrument of half that worth as those their swords made rich with the most noble blood of all this world. He shakes each conspirator's bloody hand in turn, naming them all. And he asks for two things only: that they will give him reasons why Caesar was dangerous, and that he may speak at the funeral.

The conduct reveals a superb tactician. Every word is calculated: the flattery secures his safety, the handshaking secures his standing as a friend rather than an enemy, and the modest request secures the only thing he actually wants. The moment he is alone with the body he shows what he really feels — 'O, pardon me, thou bleeding piece of earth, / That I am meek and gentle with these butchers!' — and prophesies civil war, that Caesar's spirit shall 'Cry "Havoc!" and let slip the dogs of war'. Cassius was right about him, and Brutus was wrong.

Q16. Brutus speaks in prose, briefly, and appeals to reason and to his own reputation. His structure is a balanced antithesis — 'Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more' — and he puts the case as a question of principle: had you rather Caesar were living and die all slaves, than that Caesar were dead, to live all free men? He offers no evidence at all that Caesar was ambitious; he asks the crowd to take it on his honour. He then makes the fatal error of leaving, so that Antony has the last word and no one to answer him.

Antony speaks in verse, at length, and appeals to emotion through evidence. He never asserts; he produces facts and lets the crowd draw the conclusion. He uses repetition ('honourable man'), praeteritio (saying he will not read the will, and thereby making them demand it), physical props (the mantle, the wounds, the body itself), and a feigned breakdown — 'Bear with me; / My heart is in the coffin there with Caesar' — timed so that the crowd argues his case for him while he recovers.

The contrast is the contrast between the two men. Brutus assumes his hearers are rational and honourable like himself, and addresses them as he would wish to be addressed. Antony knows exactly what they are, and gives them what will move them. Brutus wins the argument and loses the crowd; Antony never makes an argument and wins everything.

Q17. It is only about forty lines and contains no principal character, yet Shakespeare places it immediately after Antony's oration and immediately before the proscription scene, and its position is the point.

It shows the consequence. Antony has said 'Now let it work'; this is the work. A harmless poet, walking out because he dreamt he feasted with Caesar, is stopped by the mob, questioned in a mounting rhythm of demands, and torn to pieces for having the same name as a conspirator. His protest — 'I am Cinna the poet… I am not Cinna the conspirator' — is met with 'It is no matter, his name's Cinna'.

It also passes judgement on the assassination. Brutus killed Caesar to prevent tyranny and preserve Roman liberty; within hours of the deed a Roman citizen is murdered in the street by a mob for his name. The scene answers Brutus's political case more devastatingly than any speech could, and it does so without comment.

D. Long Answer / Essay Type

Q18. There is a strong case for the claim, and the play is constructed to support it.

The assassination succeeds. Caesar dies; the conspirators hold the Capitol; and when Brutus speaks to the people they are entirely with him, crying 'Let him be Caesar' and offering to bring him home in triumph. At that moment the conspiracy has won. Nothing about the deed itself has failed.

What destroys it is a decision taken minutes earlier, over Cassius's explicit objection. Cassius draws Brutus aside and warns him: 'You know not what you do: do not consent / That Antony speak in his funeral: / Know you how much the people may be moved / By that which he will utter?' Brutus overrules him, on the grounds that he will speak first, will explain the reasons, and will announce that Antony speaks by permission — so that it shall advantage more than do them wrong. Cassius yields with 'I know not what may fall; I like it not.'

Every element of the catastrophe follows from that concession. Antony gets the pulpit, the body, the will and the last word. He turns the crowd inside an hour, and by the end of the scene Brutus and Cassius are riding out of Rome like madmen.

It is also the third and decisive instance of a single pattern. Brutus overruled Cassius on the oath, on sparing Antony, and now on the funeral; he will overrule him once more, on marching to Philippi. Cassius is right every time. The tragedy is not that Brutus is wicked but that he is confident, and that his confidence rests on assuming other men are as honourable as himself — he expects Antony to keep faith because Brutus would have kept faith.

One qualification is worth making. The assassination is the moral turning point even if the funeral is the practical one: it is the act that cannot be undone and that the rest of the play judges. But as the hinge of the plot, Cassius's warning and Brutus's refusal to heed it decide everything that follows.

E. Critical Appreciation and Theme

Q19. The play is unusually preoccupied with persuasion: almost every major event is brought about by one man talking another into something.

Cassius talks Brutus into the conspiracy, and admits in soliloquy that he is doing it. Decius talks Caesar out of staying at home, and tells us in advance exactly how he will do it — Caesar loves to hear that men may be betrayed with flatterers. Brutus talks the conspirators out of an oath and out of killing Antony. Antony talks the crowd into a riot. In each case Shakespeare shows us the technique, often before we see it used, so that the audience watches persuasion as a craft.

The forum scene is the set piece, and it makes the play's argument. Brutus's speech is honest, reasoned and true to what he believes; Antony's is dishonest in intention, scrupulously truthful in every particular, and devastating. The better man loses, and he loses because he assumes that reasons persuade. Antony knows that what persuades is a torn mantle, a will, a body, a pause, and a phrase repeated until it turns into its opposite.

The play is not cynical about this so much as clear-eyed. It shows that the same instrument that makes public life possible also makes it dangerous, and that a republic which is governed by whoever speaks best is not securely governed at all. Cinna the poet, killed for his name forty lines after the oration, is the play's judgement on where unchecked rhetoric leads.
