Q45. (a) Amplitude: The maximum displacement of a vibrating particle from its mean (rest) position. It is measured in metres (m).
(b) Frequency: The number of complete vibrations (oscillations) made by a particle of the medium in one second. Unit: hertz (Hz).
(c) Wavelength: The distance between two consecutive particles of the medium that are in the same phase (e.g., two consecutive crests or troughs). Symbol: λ. Unit: metre (m).
(d) Wave velocity: The distance travelled by the wave in one second in the direction of propagation of the wave. Unit: m/s. Relationship: v = fλ.

Q52. SONAR stands for Sound Navigation And Ranging. It is a device used to detect and locate underwater objects (submarines, fish, sunken ships) and to measure the depth of sea.

where V is the speed of sound in water and t is the time for echo to return.

Q61. Method to determine speed of sound by echo:
Let a person stand at a measured distance d from a tall, rigid wall or cliff. The person produces a sharp, loud sound (e.g., a clap or a gun shot) and notes the time t between the original sound and the echo. Since the sound travels to the wall and back, the total distance = 2d.
Speed of sound V = 2d / t.

Calculation:
Given: V = 350 m/s, t = 1 s.
Let d = distance of hill from the boy.
2d = V × t = 350 × 1 = 350 m
d = 350/2 = 175 m.
The hill is 175 m from the boy.

Q62. Resonance: Resonance is a special case of forced vibrations in which the frequency of the applied periodic force is exactly equal to the natural frequency of the vibrating body. At resonance, the body vibrates with maximum amplitude and produces a loud sound.

Experiment: Take two identical tuning forks A and B of the same frequency, mounted on separate sound boxes with open ends facing each other. Strike fork A with a rubber hammer to set it vibrating. After some time, muffle fork A (stop it) and bring fork B near the ear. Fork B will also be heard vibrating.
Explanation: The sound waves from fork A travel through air and set the air column in box A vibrating. This air column transmits its vibrations to the air between the two boxes. When these waves reach fork B, they exert a periodic force on it. Since the frequency of fork A equals the natural frequency of fork B, resonance occurs — fork B vibrates with maximum amplitude. This demonstrates resonance.

Q67. Even though both notes have the same pitch (same fundamental frequency) and same loudness (same amplitude), the wave patterns are different because they differ in quality (timbre).

Explanation: When a string or instrument vibrates, it does not vibrate only in its fundamental mode. It simultaneously produces harmonics (overtones) — vibrations at 2f, 3f, 4f, etc. Different instruments produce different harmonics in different proportions and at different relative amplitudes. The superposition of the fundamental and its harmonics produces the overall waveform. Since violin and piano have different construction, materials, and resonance boxes, the number and relative intensities of harmonics produced differ — leading to a different wave pattern (waveform) for each instrument, even for the same note.

Q69. Sound ranging: Sound ranging is the method of determining the direction and distance of an object by reflecting sound waves from it. The reflected sound (echo) is used to determine the range (distance) of the object.

Use: Sound ranging is used in SONAR to detect submarines, measure ocean depth, and locate underwater obstacles.

Q70. Why different thickness strings: The natural frequency of vibration of a stretched string is inversely proportional to its radius (or thickness) — f ∝ 1/r. Thicker strings vibrate at lower frequencies (produce bass/low-pitched notes) while thinner strings vibrate at higher frequencies (produce shrill/high-pitched notes). By having strings of different thicknesses under the same tension, the instrument can produce notes of widely different frequencies, giving it a larger range.

Two ways to increase frequency of a stretched string:
(i) Increase the tension in the string (f ∝ √T).
(ii) Decrease the vibrating length of the string (f ∝ 1/l) — done by pressing the string against the fingerboard (frets).

Q92. Given: l₁ = 80 cm, f₁ = 256 Hz, f₂ = 1024 Hz.
By the law of vibrating strings: f × l = constant (for same tension and wire).
f₁ × l₁ = f₂ × l₂
l₂ = f₁ × l₁ / f₂ = 256 × 80 / 1024 = 20480/1024 = 20 cm.
The wire should be 20 cm long to produce 1024 Hz.
