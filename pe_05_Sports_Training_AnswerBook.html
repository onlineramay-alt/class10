B. Very Short Answer

Q3. SPORTS TRAINING is the systematic, planned and scientific process of preparing a sportsperson for the highest possible performance in a chosen sport, carried out over a long period under expert guidance.

The words that matter are SYSTEMATIC, PLANNED and SCIENTIFIC — random hard work is not training.

TWO OBJECTIVES: to develop the physical fitness components the sport demands — strength, speed, endurance, flexibility and coordination; and to perfect the TECHNIQUE and TACTICS of the sport so that skill becomes automatic and can be applied under pressure. (Also acceptable: to build the psychological qualities of confidence, concentration and determination; to prevent injury; and to bring the athlete to peak form at the right moment.)

C. Short Answer Type

Q4. (i) PRINCIPLE OF INDIVIDUALITY. Every athlete differs in age, sex, build, fitness, experience and rate of recovery, so a programme must be designed for the individual. A schedule that suits one player may under-train a second and injure a third.

(ii) PRINCIPLE OF SPECIFICITY. Training must be specific to the sport, to the muscle groups used, to the energy system involved and to the movement patterns required. A swimmer improves by swimming; running will not make him a better swimmer, however fit it makes him in general.

(iii) PRINCIPLE OF OVERLOAD. Improvement occurs only when the body is worked at a level GREATER than it is accustomed to. Training at a comfortable load maintains fitness but does not increase it.

(iv) PRINCIPLE OF PROGRESSION. The overload must be increased GRADUALLY and in steps. Too rapid an increase causes injury, overtraining and staleness; too slow an increase produces no improvement.

(v) PRINCIPLE OF RECOVERY. The body adapts and grows stronger during REST, not during the work itself. Adequate rest, sleep and nutrition between sessions are part of the training, and without them hard work produces deterioration rather than improvement.

(vi) PRINCIPLE OF REVERSIBILITY. Whatever is gained by training is lost when training ceases — 'use it or lose it'. Fitness declines within a few weeks of stopping, and endurance is lost faster than strength, which is why off-season maintenance is necessary.

(Others in the syllabus: ADAPTATION, the body's adjustment to a repeated stress; VARIANCE, the need to vary activities to prevent boredom and overuse; FREQUENCY and CONTINUITY, that training must be regular and uninterrupted; ACTIVE PARTICIPATION, that the athlete must understand and willingly commit to the programme; PERIODIZATION, the division of the year into preparatory, competitive and transition phases so that peak form coincides with the major competition; and INTENSITY, the degree of effort in each session.)

Q5. PERIODIZATION is the division of the training year into definite phases or periods, each with its own aim, volume and intensity, so that the athlete reaches PEAK FORM at the time of the major competition. Nobody can remain at peak all year; periodization plans when the peak shall come.

THE THREE PHASES:

(i) PREPARATORY PERIOD — the longest phase, in the off-season. General conditioning first, building endurance, strength and flexibility with HIGH VOLUME and LOW INTENSITY; then a specific sub-phase in which the work becomes more like the sport itself. Technique is built and rebuilt here, because there is time to work on it.

(ii) COMPETITIVE PERIOD — the season. Volume is REDUCED and INTENSITY RAISED; the emphasis shifts from building fitness to maintaining it and to sharpening technique, tactics and psychological readiness. Competitions themselves become part of the training.

(iii) TRANSITION PERIOD — after the season. Active rest: light and varied activity, often in a different sport, to allow physical and mental recovery from the year's demands while avoiding a complete loss of fitness. Injuries are treated and rehabilitated here.

The cycle then begins again, at a higher base than the year before.
