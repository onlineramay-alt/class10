B. Short Answer Type

Q5. COTTON. Temperature 21 to 30 °C, with at least 210 frost-free days and abundant bright sunshine. Rainfall MODERATE, 50 to 100 cm, and — this is the key point — a DRY, BRIGHT period at picking, because rain at harvest stains and rots the bolls. Soil: BLACK soil above all, which retains moisture; also alluvial and red soil. Chief areas: Gujarat, Maharashtra, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Punjab and Haryana. Processing: ginning, to separate the lint from the seed.

JUTE. Temperature 24 to 35 °C, HIGH HUMIDITY, and HEAVY rainfall of 150 to 200 cm — much wetter than cotton. Soil: light sandy or clayey ALLUVIUM, renewed every year by flooding, which is why the Ganga–Brahmaputra delta suits it exactly. Chief areas: West Bengal (over three-quarters of the crop), Bihar, Assam and Odisha. Processing: retting, then stripping, washing and drying.

THE CONTRAST. Both are fibres and both are kharif crops, but cotton wants moderate rain and a dry harvest on black soil in the west and the Deccan, while jute wants very heavy rain, high humidity and fresh alluvium in the north-east. The two are grown at opposite ends of the country for exactly that reason.

NOTE — jute production has declined because of competition from synthetic substitutes, and because Partition left most of the jute-growing land in East Bengal and most of the mills in West Bengal.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q6. CLIMATE. A hot, wet, humid climate: temperature 20 to 30 °C, never below about 16 °C, with high humidity and rainfall of 150 to 250 cm WELL DISTRIBUTED THROUGH THE YEAR — tea needs frequent light showers rather than a concentrated monsoon, because the young leaves are plucked continually. Long dry spells and frost are both harmful; shade is beneficial, so shade trees are planted among the bushes.

SOIL. Deep, fertile, well-drained LOAMY soil, rich in humus and iron, and slightly ACIDIC — tea will not tolerate lime. Laterite and forest soils suit it.

WHY HILL SLOPES. Because tea cannot bear WATERLOGGING — stagnant water at the roots kills the bush — and a slope drains naturally, carrying the heavy rainfall away at once. Slopes also give the altitude that keeps the temperature moderate in a tropical latitude, and the mist and cloud of the hills provide the humidity the plant needs. That is why the gardens are on the slopes of the Assam hills, the Darjeeling hills and the Nilgiris rather than on the plains below.

CHIEF AREAS: Assam (over half the crop), West Bengal (Darjeeling and the Dooars), Tamil Nadu and Kerala (the Nilgiris and Annamalai).

PROCESSING: plucking of the two leaves and a bud, then withering, rolling, fermenting, drying and grading.

Q7. CLIMATE. A warm, humid, equable climate: temperature 15 to 28 °C, rainfall 150 to 250 cm, and — importantly — SHADE, so coffee is grown under the canopy of taller trees. It needs a dry spell at ripening for the berries to mature and be picked. Frost, strong sun and strong wind are all harmful, so it is grown on sheltered hill slopes at 900 to 1,800 metres.

SOIL. Well-drained, deep, friable LOAM rich in humus, iron and potash; the weathered lava and forest soils of the Western Ghats are ideal. Good drainage is essential, hence the slopes.

THE TWO VARIETIES. ARABICA — the finer, more aromatic and more valuable, but more delicate and more liable to disease; grown at higher altitudes. ROBUSTA — hardier, higher-yielding, more disease-resistant, with a stronger and less refined flavour and more caffeine; grown at lower altitudes. India grows both, and is noted for its shade-grown coffee.

CHIEF AREAS: Karnataka, which produces about seventy per cent — Chikmagalur, Coorg and Hassan; then Kerala and Tamil Nadu.

Q8. (a) RETTING (JUTE) — steeping the cut jute stalks in water for two or three weeks so that bacteria rot the gummy tissue and the fibre can be separated from the stem.

(b) GINNING (COTTON) — the process of separating the cotton FIBRE (lint) from the SEEDS after picking, done in a ginning mill; the lint goes to the spinning mill and the seed to the oil mill.

(c) PRUNING (TEA) — regularly cutting back the tea bush to keep it about a metre high (a ‘plucking table’) and bushy. This makes it put out many tender new shoots — the ‘two leaves and a bud’ that are plucked — and keeps picking easy.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q9. (a) A deep, well-drained, porous, slightly ACIDIC soil rich in humus and iron — the forest loam and laterite of the hill slopes; the tea bush cannot stand waterlogging at its roots.

(b) Tea needs plenty of rain but its roots ROT in standing water, so it is planted on SLOPES where the surplus water drains away at once. The slopes of Assam, Darjeeling, the Nilgiris and the Western Ghats also give the cool, moist, misty conditions the plant likes.

(c) Pruning keeps the bush LOW and SPREADING, forces it to throw out a mass of tender young shoots (the part that is plucked), makes plucking quick and easy, and removes old, diseased and woody growth, so the yield and the quality of the leaf stay high.

Q10. (a) A ‘FINE PLUCK’ means plucking only the youngest, tenderest growth — the terminal BUD together with the TWO topmost leaves (‘two leaves and a bud’). It gives the best-quality tea; a ‘coarse pluck’ takes three or more leaves and gives more weight but poorer tea.

(b) KARNATAKA produces about seventy per cent of India’s coffee (the Kodagu, Chikmagalur and Hassan districts), followed by Kerala and Tamil Nadu.

(c) Because cotton needs 6–8 months of warm, frost-free weather with the MONSOON RAIN falling during its growing period and then BRIGHT DRY SUNSHINE while the bolls ripen and are picked. Sowing with the June monsoon (kharif) and picking in the clear weather of October–December gives exactly this sequence.

(b) Because cotton is PICKED BY HAND. The bolls on a plant do not all open at once, so the field must be gone over several times, and each open boll is picked separately; hoeing, weeding and thinning the young crop also take many hands. There is no cheap machine substitute in Indian conditions, so cotton demands a large, cheap labour force.
