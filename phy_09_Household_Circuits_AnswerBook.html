D. Long Answer Questions

Q52. Transmission of Electric Power:

1. Generation: Power is generated at 11 kV, 50 Hz at the power station using generators (alternators).

2. Step-up: A step-up transformer at the generating station increases the voltage from 11 kV to 132 kV. This reduces the current, minimising transmission losses (P_loss = I²R).

3. Long-distance transmission: High-voltage (132 kV) power is transmitted through overhead cables over long distances to the main sub-station.

4. Step-down at main sub-station: A step-down transformer reduces voltage from 132 kV to 33 kV.

5. Step-down at local sub-station: Another step-down transformer reduces 33 kV to 11 kV, then a distribution transformer brings it to 220 V.

6. Supply to homes: 220 V, 50 Hz a.c. is supplied to homes via the live (brown) and neutral (blue) wires. The neutral and earth wires are connected together at the local sub-station (both at 0 V).

Diagram: [Generating Station (11 kV)] → [Step-up Transformer (132 kV)] → [Main Sub-station (33 kV)] → [Local Sub-station (11 kV)] → [Distribution Transformer (220 V)] → [Home]

Q53. Ring Main Circuit:
In the ring main circuit, the live wire and the neutral wire each form a complete ring (loop) around the house, starting and ending at the consumer unit (fuse box). All sockets and fixed appliances are connected in parallel between the live and neutral rings. Each socket also has an earth wire.

Diagram: Consumer unit → Live wire ring → sockets/appliances (all in parallel) → back to consumer unit; similarly for neutral and earth wires.

Advantages of ring main circuit:
1. Each appliance operates at the full mains voltage (220 V).
2. Any appliance can be switched on or off independently without affecting others.
3. Current is supplied from both sides of the ring, so the wires carry only half the total current, allowing thinner (cheaper) cables.
4. If one section of the ring is broken, appliances can still be supplied from the other side of the ring.

Q55. (a) Bulb A is connected in parallel directly with the mains. It glows at 220 V (full mains voltage).
(b) Bulbs B and C are connected in series with each other (this series combination is in parallel with A). Assuming equal resistance, each shares the voltage equally. Bulb B glows at 110 V.
(c) If bulb B gets fused: The series branch (B and C) is broken. Bulb A — no effect (remains glowing at 220 V, since it is in a separate parallel branch). Bulb C — does not glow (circuit through B and C is broken).
(d) If bulb A gets fused: Bulb A was in a separate parallel branch. Its failure does not affect the other parallel branch. Bulbs B and C — both remain unaffected and continue to glow at 110 V each.

Q56. Fuse in a Lighting Circuit:
A rewirable (kit-kat) fuse consists of a porcelain base with two brass terminals and a porcelain bridge (fuse carrier) that holds the fuse wire. The fuse wire (lead-tin alloy, 5 A rating for lighting circuit) is stretched between the two terminals on the carrier and the carrier clips into the base.

Diagram: [Porcelain base] with [brass terminals] → [Fuse carrier] with [fuse wire of lead-tin alloy connecting both terminals]

Two reasons why copper wire must NOT be used as a fuse:
1. Copper has a very high melting point (~1085°C). It will not melt when excess current flows, so it cannot protect the circuit from overloading or short circuits.
2. Copper has a very low resistance. Even large excess currents will not heat it sufficiently to melt it, defeating the purpose of a fuse. Instead, the wires and appliances would overheat and catch fire.

Q58. Purpose of terminals in a three-pin plug:
- Live (L) terminal (right pin, brown wire): Connects the appliance to the live (230 V) supply. Current enters the appliance through this pin.
- Neutral (N) terminal (left pin, blue wire): Connects the appliance to the neutral supply. Current returns to the supply through this pin.
- Earth (E) terminal (top pin, green/yellow wire): Connects the metallic body of the appliance to the earth (ground). Provides a safe path for leakage current, preventing electric shock.

Diagram description: Three-pin plug viewed from the front, looking at the pins — the top (longest, thickest) pin is Earth (E), the bottom-right pin is Live (L), and the bottom-left pin is Neutral (N).

Note: The earth pin is made longer and thicker than the others so that the earth connection is made first (before live and neutral) when the plug is inserted, and broken last when the plug is removed — ensuring safety.

Q59. Dual Control (Two-way) Switch for Staircase Lighting:

Circuit: Live wire → Switch S₁ (at bottom of stairs) → two connecting wires (travellers) → Switch S₂ (at top of stairs) → Bulb → Neutral wire.

Working:
A two-way switch has one common terminal and two switching terminals (A and B).
- When both S₁ and S₂ are in the same position (both connecting through wire A, or both through wire B), the circuit is complete and the bulb glows.
- When one switch is flipped, the circuit is broken and the bulb goes off.
- Flipping either switch will toggle the bulb ON or OFF, regardless of the other switch's position.

This allows the light to be controlled from either the bottom or the top of the staircase independently.

Q60. Circumstances leading to electric shock:
1. If the insulation of the live wire inside the appliance is damaged and the live wire comes into contact with the metallic body/case of the appliance — touching the body will cause a shock.
2. If a person touches the live terminal of the appliance directly (e.g., a loose wire, faulty socket, or uninsulated live pin) while standing on a conducting surface (wet floor).

Preventive measure: Earthing. The metallic case of the appliance must be connected to the earth wire. If insulation fails and current leaks to the metallic body, the leakage current flows harmlessly to earth (instead of through the user's body). The large current that flows also blows the fuse, cutting off the supply.
