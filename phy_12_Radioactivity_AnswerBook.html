A. Multiple Choice Questions

Q1. (d) both (a) and (c)

In a neutral atom, the number of protons equals the atomic number (Z). Since the atom is electrically neutral, the number of electrons also equals the number of protons. Hence protons = atomic number = number of electrons.

Official Answer

Q2. (b) Number of protons = A − Z

This relation is incorrect. The number of protons equals the atomic number Z (not A − Z). It is the number of neutrons that equals A − Z. The other three relations are all correct.

Official Answer

Q3. (c) 8

Number of neutrons = Mass number − Atomic number = 15 − 7 = 8.

Official Answer

Q4. (a) Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same atomic number Z, hence same number of protons) but with different mass numbers (different number of neutrons). Example: ¹H, ²H, ³H are isotopes of hydrogen.

Official Answer

Q5. (d) Tin

Tin (Sn) has 10 stable isotopes, the largest number of stable isotopes of any element. Hydrogen has 2 stable isotopes, carbon has 2, and chlorine has 2.

Official Answer

Q6. (d) Isotones

As given in the textbook answer key, ⁴⁰K and ⁴⁰Ca are classified as isotones.

Official Answer

Q7. (d) All of the above

Uranium, Polonium, and Actinium are all naturally occurring radioactive elements. They emit radiation (α, β, or γ) spontaneously due to nuclear instability.

Official Answer

Q8. (b) α radiation

An alpha particle consists of 2 protons and 2 neutrons (a helium-4 nucleus), giving it a mass of approximately 4 a.m.u. Beta particles (electrons) have mass ≈ 1/1836 a.m.u. and gamma rays are massless photons. Hence alpha radiation is the heaviest.

Official Answer

Q9. (c) carbon

Carbon-14 (¹⁴C) dating (radiocarbon dating) is used to determine the age of archaeological specimens. Living organisms maintain a constant ratio of ¹⁴C to ¹²C, but after death, ¹⁴C decays with a half-life of about 5730 years, allowing age estimation.

Official Answer

Q10. (b) one place higher

When a β-particle (electron) is emitted, the atomic number increases by 1 (a neutron converts to a proton). Since atomic number increases by 1, the element moves one place higher (to the right) in the periodic table.

Official Answer

Q11. (c) ᴬ_ZP → ᴬ_ZP + γ

Gamma emission does not change the mass number (A) or atomic number (Z). The nucleus remains the same element but transitions from an excited state to a lower energy state, releasing energy as a gamma photon. Hence the representation is ᴬ_ZP* → ᴬ_ZP + γ.

Official Answer

Q12. (a) isotope of the parent

Let n = number of α particles emitted, then 2n = number of β particles emitted.
Change in atomic number: −2n (from α) + 2n (from β) = 0.
Change in mass number: −4n (from α) + 0 (from β) = −4n.
Since the atomic number is unchanged but mass number changes, the daughter is an isotope of the parent.

Official Answer

Q13. (d) decay of the neutron in the nucleus

Beta particles (electrons) originate from the decay of a neutron inside the nucleus: n → p + e⁻ + antineutrino. The electron is not a pre-existing orbital electron but is created during this nuclear process.

Official Answer

Q14. (c) assertion is false but reason is true

The assertion is false: γ radiation has the maximum penetrating power (can penetrate several centimetres of lead), not β-particles. The reason is true: α-particles (helium nuclei with 2p + 2n) are indeed the heaviest of the three radiations.

Official Answer

Q15. (a) both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A

Isobars are atoms with the same mass number but different atomic numbers. ¹⁴C (Z=6, A=14) and ¹⁴N (Z=7, A=14) both have mass number 14, so they are isobars. The reason correctly explains the differing nuclear compositions that make them different elements with the same mass number.

Official Answer

Q16. (b) E = mc²

Einstein's mass-energy equivalence relation is E = mc², where E is energy, m is mass, and c is the speed of light (3 × 10⁸ m/s). In nuclear reactions, the energy released equals the mass defect (Δm) multiplied by c², i.e., E = Δmc².

Official Answer

Q17. (d) both (a) and (b)

In nuclear fission, both mass number (total number of nucleons) and atomic number (total charge) are conserved. However, the actual mass is NOT conserved — a small fraction is converted into energy (E = Δmc²).

Official Answer

Q18. (d) Both (b) and (c)

Moderators slow down fast neutrons to thermal (slow) neutrons that can sustain the fission chain reaction. Graphite (carbon) and heavy water (D₂O) are used as moderators. Cadmium rods are used as control rods (they absorb neutrons to control the reaction rate, not to slow them down).

Official Answer

Q19. (c) hydrogen

The sun and stars derive their energy from thermonuclear fusion of hydrogen nuclei (protons). Four protons fuse to form a helium nucleus, releasing tremendous energy. This process is called the proton-proton chain.

Official Answer

Q20. (c) moderator is used to slow down the neutrons

The moderator (graphite or heavy water) slows down fast neutrons produced in fission to thermal speeds, making them more effective at causing further fissions. Control rods (cadmium or boron) absorb neutrons to control the reaction rate. The coolant transfers heat away from the reactor core.

Official Answer

Q21. (d) 931 MeV

The rest mass of a proton is approximately 1 a.m.u. Since 1 a.m.u. = 931 MeV (energy equivalent), the rest mass energy of a proton is approximately 931 MeV. (The electron rest mass energy is 0.51 MeV.)

Official Answer

Q22. (d) (i) and (iv) Nuclei are positively charged

Fusion requires nuclei to come extremely close together. Two reasons prevent this at ordinary temperatures: (i) Nuclear forces are short range (act only at < 10⁻¹⁵ m), and (iv) Nuclei carry positive charges and strongly repel each other (Coulomb repulsion). Only at temperatures of about 10⁷ K do nuclei have enough kinetic energy to overcome this repulsion.

Official Answer

Q23. (a) α, β, γ

Step 1: ²¹²₈₃X → ²⁰⁸₈₁Y — A falls by 4 and Z falls by 2, which is α emission.

Step 3: ²⁰⁸₈₂B → ²⁰⁸₈₂B — neither A nor Z changes, which is γ emission.

Q24. (c) Alpha particles

Alpha particles have the maximum ionising power because they carry a charge of +2e and have a relatively large mass. They interact strongly with matter, causing dense ionisation along their short path. Alpha particles ionise about 100 times more than beta particles and 10,000 times more than gamma rays.

Q25. (c) X-rays

Gamma rays and X-rays are both electromagnetic radiations (photons). They travel at the speed of light, have no charge and no mass, and differ only in their wavelength and origin. Gamma rays (λ ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ m) have shorter wavelengths than X-rays (λ ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m) and originate from the nucleus.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q26. (c) Gamma radiation

Gamma rays are electromagnetic radiation with no electric charge. Since deflection in electric and magnetic fields requires a charge, uncharged gamma rays pass straight through both fields undeflected. Alpha particles (+2 charge) and beta particles (−1 charge) are deflected.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q27. (b) decreases by 4

An alpha particle consists of 2 protons and 2 neutrons (mass number = 4). When it is emitted, 4 nucleons leave the nucleus, so the mass number of the daughter nucleus decreases by 4. The atomic number also decreases by 2.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q28. (c) α < β < γ

Alpha particles have the least penetrating power (stopped by a few cm of air or a sheet of paper). Beta particles are more penetrating (stopped by a few mm of aluminium). Gamma rays have the maximum penetrating power (require several cm of lead to stop them).

Q29. (c) 200 MeV

The fission of one uranium-235 nucleus releases approximately 200 MeV (more precisely about 190–200 MeV) of energy. This is an enormous amount compared to chemical reactions (which release a few eV per reaction), making nuclear fission a very powerful energy source.

Q30. (d) Both fission and fusion

A hydrogen bomb (thermonuclear bomb) uses nuclear fission (from a conventional atomic bomb trigger) to create the extreme temperature (~10⁷ K) needed to initiate nuclear fusion of hydrogen isotopes (deuterium and tritium). The primary energy comes from fusion, but fission is needed to start it.

Q57. No change in radioactivity will occur when a radioactive substance is oxidised.

Reason: Radioactivity is a nuclear phenomenon. It depends only on the nucleus of the atom, not on the orbital electrons. Chemical reactions (like oxidation) involve changes in the orbital electrons and do not affect the nucleus in any way. Since the nucleus remains unchanged during a chemical reaction, the radioactivity remains unaffected.

This is one of the key properties of radioactivity — it cannot be altered by any physical or chemical means.

Q58. Alpha and beta particles are deflected in electric and magnetic fields because they are charged particles:
- Alpha particles carry a charge of +2e (positively charged)
- Beta particles carry a charge of −e (negatively charged)

Charged particles experience a force in electric and magnetic fields (Coulomb force and Lorentz force respectively), causing them to deflect from their straight-line path.

Gamma rays are not deflected because they are electromagnetic radiation (photons) with no electric charge and no mass. Uncharged particles experience no force in electric or magnetic fields, so gamma rays travel in straight lines through such fields.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q59. No, it is not possible to deflect γ radiations using electric or magnetic fields.

Reason: Gamma rays are electromagnetic radiation consisting of high-energy photons. They have:
- No electric charge (neutral)
- No rest mass

Since deflection in electric and magnetic fields requires a charged particle, gamma rays (being uncharged) cannot be deflected. They pass straight through electric and magnetic fields without any deviation, unlike α particles (+2e) and β particles (−e) which are deflected.

Q60. The statement 'Radioactivity is a nuclear phenomenon' means that radioactivity originates from the nucleus of the atom, not from the orbital electrons.

Evidence and implications:
1. Chemical changes: When a radioactive element forms a chemical compound, the radioactivity is unaffected — because chemical changes involve orbital electrons, not the nucleus.
2. Physical conditions: Temperature, pressure, electric field, magnetic field — none of these affect the rate of radioactive decay, confirming it is purely a nuclear process.
3. Nature of emissions: Alpha and beta particles are emitted from the nucleus. Gamma rays are also emitted when the nucleus transitions between energy states.

Conclusion: Since radioactivity depends entirely on the instability of the nucleus and the spontaneous rearrangement of nucleons, it is correctly called a nuclear phenomenon.

Q65. Medical uses of radioactivity:
1. Cancer treatment (radiotherapy): Gamma radiation from Co-60 is used to destroy cancer cells/tumours.
2. Diagnosis: Radioactive tracers (e.g., ¹³¹I for thyroid, technetium-99m for bone scans) are used to study organ function.

Radiations from radioactive substances can damage living tissues and cells. Exposure to high doses can:
- Cause radiation burns and radiation sickness
- Damage DNA, leading to mutations and cancer
- Destroy blood cells, leading to leukaemia
- Cause genetic damage affecting future generations

Q66. Three safety precautions while handling radioactive substances:

1. Use lead shielding: Radioactive sources must be stored in thick lead containers, as lead effectively absorbs all three types of radiation (α, β, γ). Workers must stand behind lead/concrete shields.

2. Use remote-handling tools: Radioactive materials should never be touched with bare hands. Use long-handled tongs, robotic arms, or gloves to maintain maximum distance from the source (intensity of radiation decreases with distance as per inverse square law).

3. Wear dosimeters and protective clothing: Workers must wear radiation badges (dosimeters) to monitor their exposure and wear protective clothing. Exposure time should be minimised.

Q67. Nuclear waste refers to the radioactive by-products produced in nuclear reactors and nuclear fuel processing plants after the fission reaction. This waste contains unstable radioactive nuclei that continue to emit harmful radiation for thousands of years.

Types of nuclear waste: spent fuel rods, radioactive isotopes produced as fission products.

Safe disposal method:
The most common method is deep geological storage — nuclear waste is sealed in thick lead-lined, corrosion-resistant containers and buried deep underground (several hundred metres) in geologically stable rock formations, away from groundwater sources. This prevents radiation from reaching the biosphere.

Q68. Background radiation is the low-level ionising radiation that is present everywhere in our environment, to which all living things are continuously exposed, even in the absence of any artificial radioactive source.

Two sources:
1. Natural sources: Cosmic rays from outer space, radioactive gases (radon from rocks), naturally occurring radioactive elements in soil, rocks (uranium, thorium), and food.
2. Artificial sources: Medical X-rays and nuclear medicine procedures, radioactive fallout from nuclear weapons tests, nuclear power plants.

Can we keep away from it? No, it is not possible to completely avoid background radiation. Natural background radiation is present everywhere — in the air, soil, food, and even in our own bodies (e.g., potassium-40 in body cells). We can minimise exposure from artificial sources but cannot eliminate natural background radiation entirely.

Q69. Nuclear energy is the energy released during nuclear reactions — specifically nuclear fission or nuclear fusion.

What is responsible for its release:
Nuclear energy is released due to the conversion of mass into energy, as described by Einstein's mass-energy equivalence: E = mc².

In nuclear reactions, the total mass of the products is slightly less than the total mass of the reactants. This 'mass defect' (Δm) is converted into a tremendous amount of energy:
E = Δm × c²

Since c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s is very large, even a tiny loss in mass (Δm) results in an enormous amount of energy. For example, 1 kg of mass completely converted gives 9 × 10¹⁶ J of energy.

Q70. Nuclear fission is the process in which a heavy nucleus (such as uranium-235) splits into two medium-sized nuclei (called fission fragments) when bombarded by a slow (thermal) neutron, releasing a large amount of energy and 2 or 3 more neutrons.

Substance used: Uranium-235 (²³⁵₉₂U) is most commonly used. Plutonium-239 (²³⁹₉₄Pu) is also used.

Fission reaction:
²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ⁹⁰₃₈Sr + ¹⁴³₅₄Xe + 3¹₀n + energy (~200 MeV)

The neutrons released can trigger further fission reactions, leading to a chain reaction.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q71. Chain reaction: In nuclear fission, each fission of a uranium-235 nucleus releases 2 or 3 neutrons. Each of these neutrons can trigger further fission in nearby uranium nuclei, which in turn release more neutrons. This self-sustaining process, where each fission triggers multiple further fissions, is called a chain reaction.

- Uncontrolled chain reaction: results in an explosion (atomic bomb)
- Controlled chain reaction: produces steady energy (nuclear reactor)

How it is controlled in a nuclear reactor:
1. Control rods (cadmium or boron): These absorb excess neutrons, reducing the number available to cause fission. Inserting rods deeper slows the reaction; withdrawing them speeds it up.
2. Moderator (graphite or heavy water): Slows fast neutrons to thermal speeds, making them suitable for causing ²³⁵U fission without letting the reaction run away.

Q74. Similarity:
In both nuclear fission and nuclear fusion, a small amount of mass is converted into a large amount of energy, according to Einstein's relation E = Δmc². Both reactions release nuclear energy.

Differences:
1. In fission, a heavy nucleus splits into lighter nuclei; in fusion, light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus.
2. Fission can occur at ordinary temperatures (triggered by slow neutrons); fusion requires extremely high temperatures (~10⁷ K) to overcome Coulomb repulsion.
3. Fission produces radioactive waste (fission fragments); fusion produces mainly helium (non-radioactive), making it cleaner.
4. Energy per unit mass: fusion releases more energy per unit mass of fuel than fission.

Q83. The Nucleus of an Atom:
The nucleus is the tiny, extremely dense, positively charged core at the centre of an atom. It contains almost all the mass of the atom.

Size comparison:
- The diameter of an atom is approximately 10⁻¹⁰ m (1 Å).
- The diameter of the nucleus is approximately 10⁻¹⁵ m (1 fm).
- The nucleus is about 10⁵ times (100,000 times) smaller than the atom.
- Despite its tiny size, the nucleus contains 99.9% of the atom's mass.

Constituents of the nucleus:
The nucleus contains two types of particles, collectively called nucleons:
1. Protons: positively charged, mass ≈ 1 a.m.u., charge = +e
2. Neutrons: neutral (no charge), mass ≈ 1 a.m.u.

Determination by atomic number and mass number:
- Atomic number (Z) = number of protons in the nucleus
- Mass number (A) = total number of nucleons = protons + neutrons
- Therefore, Number of neutrons = A − Z

Example: For ²³⁸₉₂U:
- Protons = Z = 92
- Neutrons = A − Z = 238 − 92 = 146
- Total nucleons = 238

(b) Radiations deflected by electric field:

(d) Radiation travelling at the speed of light:

(e) Highest ionising power:
Alpha (α) radiation has the highest ionising power — it ionises about 100 times more than β particles and 10,000 times more than γ rays.

(f) Same particles as cathode rays:
Beta (β) radiation consists of electrons, which are the same particles as cathode rays (streams of electrons). Both carry the same charge (−e) and have the same mass.

Q92. A Nuclear Reactor:
A nuclear reactor is a device in which a controlled, self-sustaining nuclear fission chain reaction is maintained to produce energy.

Main Components:

1. Nuclear fuel:
- Uranium-235 (²³⁵U) or Plutonium-239 (²³⁹Pu) in the form of fuel rods.
- Fission of ²³⁵U releases ~200 MeV per nucleus and 2-3 neutrons.

2. Moderator:
- Material: Graphite (carbon) or Heavy water (D₂O)
- Role: Slows down fast neutrons produced in fission to thermal (slow) neutrons. Slow neutrons are more effective at causing further fission of ²³⁵U, maintaining the chain reaction.

3. Control rods:
- Material: Cadmium or Boron
- Role: Absorb excess neutrons to control the rate of the chain reaction. Inserting rods deeper reduces reaction rate; withdrawing them increases it. They prevent the reactor from going supercritical.

4. Coolant:
- Material: Water, heavy water, or liquid sodium
- Role: Carries heat away from the reactor core to a heat exchanger (steam generator). The steam produced drives turbines to generate electricity.

5. Shielding:
- Thick walls of concrete and lead surround the reactor to absorb harmful radiation (neutrons and γ rays) and protect workers.

Working:
Slow neutrons bombard ²³⁵U → fission occurs → 2-3 fast neutrons + energy. Moderator slows the neutrons → more fission → controlled chain reaction → heat generated → coolant carries heat away → steam → turbine → electricity.
