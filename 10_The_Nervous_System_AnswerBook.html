D. Descriptive Type

Q100. (a) Neuron — the structural and functional unit of the nervous system, specialised to receive stimuli and transmit them in the form of electrical impulses.
(b) Nerve — a bundle of nerve fibres (axons) of separate neurons, enclosed together in a tubular protective sheath.
(c) Stimulus — any sudden change in the external or internal environment that produces a response in an organism or part of it.
(d) Synaptic cleft — the minute gap between the axon terminal of one neuron and the dendrite(s) of the next neuron, across which impulses are chemically transmitted.
(e) Reflex action — an automatic, quick, involuntary response of the body to a stimulus.
(f) Corpus callosum — a sheet of nerve fibres connecting the two cerebral hemispheres.

Official Answer

Q101. (a) Cerebrum is the seat of intelligence, memory, reasoning and voluntary actions; Cerebellum coordinates muscular activity and maintains posture/balance.
(b) Sympathetic — ganglia lie close to the spinal cord, prepares the body for emergencies ('fight or flight'). Parasympathetic — ganglia lie close to/within the organs supplied, brings the body back to a relaxed, normal state.
(c) Sensory nerve carries impulses from receptors TO the CNS; Motor nerve carries impulses FROM the CNS to effectors.
(d) In the cerebrum, cytons (grey matter) form the outer cortex and axons (white matter) lie inside; in the spinal cord, this is reversed — axons (white matter) form the outer part and cytons (grey matter) lie inside.
(e) 12 pairs of cranial nerves versus 31 pairs of spinal nerves.
(f) Nerve impulse is a slower, electrochemical wave of depolarisation/repolarisation along a nerve fibre; flow of electricity through a wire is a much faster, purely physical flow of electrons.
(g) Medulla oblongata controls vital involuntary functions (heartbeat, breathing) and some reflexes; Cerebellum coordinates voluntary muscular movement and balance.

Q104. A reflex action is a quick, automatic, involuntary response to a stimulus, not controlled by will and usually mediated by the spinal cord or medulla oblongata (self-protective in nature). Official Answer

Q105. A neuron consists of a cell body (cyton/perikaryon) containing the nucleus and Nissl's granules; short, branched dendrites that carry impulses towards the cyton; and a single long axon (up to about a metre long) that carries impulses away from the cyton. Many axons are covered by a fatty, insulating myelin sheath (with gaps called nodes of Ranvier) and an outer neurilemma, and end in swollen axon terminals that release neurotransmitters at a synapse.

Official Answer

Q106. The nervous system is divided into the Central Nervous System (brain and spinal cord) and the Peripheral Nervous System (all the nerves outside the CNS). The peripheral nervous system comprises the 12 pairs of cranial nerves (arising from the brain) and the 31 pairs of spinal nerves (arising from the spinal cord); it is further functionally divided into the somatic system (voluntary, controlling skeletal muscle) and the autonomic system (involuntary, controlling internal organs, itself divided into sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions).

Official Answer

Q107. The spinal cord is a long, cylindrical structure running through the vertebral column, continuous with the medulla oblongata of the brain. In cross-section it shows a central, H- (or butterfly-) shaped mass of grey matter (containing cell bodies) surrounding a fluid-filled central canal, with white matter (myelinated axons) arranged around the outside. It is covered by the same three meninges as the brain and protected externally by the bony vertebral column.

Official Answer

Q108. Unconditioned (natural) reflexes are inborn, present from birth, and require no prior learning or experience — e.g. sneezing, blinking, the knee-jerk. Conditioned reflexes are not present at birth but are acquired/learned through repeated experience and involve the cerebral cortex, so they can be modified or lost over time — e.g. salivating at the sight of a favourite food, or applying cycle brakes on sighting an obstacle.
