C. Short Answer Type

Q14. (i) Constitutional reform — the expansion of the legislative councils, a larger elected Indian element in them and wider powers for them, with the eventual goal of self-government within the Empire on the model of the self-governing colonies. (ii) The Indianisation of the services — simultaneous Indian Civil Service examinations in India and England, and a higher age limit, together with the separation of the judiciary from the executive. (iii) Economic reform — a reduction of the crushing land revenue and of military expenditure, the abolition of the salt tax, the protection and development of Indian industries, and above all an end to the drain of India's wealth to England. They also demanded the ordinary civil rights of free speech, a free press and free association, and the repeal of the Arms Act.

Q18. They had faith in it because they were themselves the product of Western education, and had learnt in English books to admire English liberty and the British constitution. They believed that British rule in India was providential, that the British people were fair-minded, and that the injustices Indians suffered were the fault of a badly informed administration rather than of a deliberate policy — so that once the facts were placed before Parliament and the British public, redress would follow.

That faith proved misplaced. Twenty years of petitions produced almost nothing: the Indian Councils Act of 1892 gave a shadow of representation and no power; the age limit for the Civil Service was not changed; the drain of wealth continued; and Curzon's Partition of Bengal in 1905 showed that the government would act against Indian opinion whenever it chose. The demonstration that prayers achieved nothing was itself the making of the Assertive Nationalists.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q19. Their concrete gains were small: the Indian Councils Act of 1892 slightly enlarged the legislative councils and allowed a limited indirect election, and a Royal Commission was appointed on Indian expenditure — little else.

Their real achievement lay elsewhere. They created a national awakening, and for the first time an organisation — the Congress — that could speak for India as a whole rather than for a province or a community. They exposed the economic character of British rule: the drain of wealth, the destruction of Indian industry and the burden of the land revenue were all demonstrated by them, and every later movement used their findings. They trained a whole generation in political work, in constitutional argument and in public organisation. And by failing, they proved that prayer and petition would not move the government, which drove their successors to bolder methods. Their weaknesses were equally real — they represented only the small educated class, never reached the masses, and clung too long to a faith in British justice that the facts did not support.

(i) Constitutional reform — enlarged legislative councils with more elected Indians and greater powers, moving towards self-government within the Empire; Indianisation of the services, with simultaneous Civil Service examinations in India and England and a higher age limit, and separation of the judiciary from the executive; and economic reform — lower land revenue and military expenditure, abolition of the salt tax, protection of Indian industry, and an end to the drain of wealth.

Everything was done strictly within the law.

(iii) Gokhale founded the Servants of India Society in 1905 and was Gandhi's political guru; and as the leading Indian member of the Imperial Legislative Council he made famous budget speeches and pressed for free and compulsory primary education. Naoroji proved the Drain of Wealth theory in 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India', which became the central economic argument of the movement; and he founded the East India Association, became in 1892 the first Indian elected to the House of Commons, and declared Swaraj the goal of the Congress at Calcutta in 1906.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q21. (i) Because they were moderate both in their aims and in their methods. They did not ask for independence but for reform within the British connection — self-government within the Empire, on the model of the self-governing colonies — and they pursued it by lawful, gradual and constitutional means, professing loyalty to the Crown throughout.

(ii) Their method was constitutional agitation: petitions and memorials to the government and to Parliament, resolutions passed at Congress sessions, speeches, newspaper articles, and deputations to England. It rested on the belief that the British people were just, and that the wrongs done in India were the result of ignorance rather than intention. Their critics called this the mendicant policy — the politics of begging — because year after year the Congress asked, the government refused, and the Congress asked again, with nothing to compel it to concede.

(iii) They achieved little in immediate terms — the Indian Councils Act of 1892 was almost their only legislative gain. But they built the first all-India political organisation, exposed the economic basis of British rule in arguments every later movement used, and trained a generation in political work. They failed in that they never reached beyond the educated middle class to the masses, and clung to a faith in British fair play which the Partition of Bengal finally destroyed — a failure that itself produced the Assertive Nationalists.
