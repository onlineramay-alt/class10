Q13. The state of hostility, tension and rivalry that existed after 1945 between the United States and its allies on one side and the Soviet Union and its allies on the other. It was fought by every means short of direct armed conflict between the two — propaganda, espionage, diplomatic pressure, economic aid and blockade, an arms and space race, rival military alliances such as NATO and the Warsaw Pact, and wars fought by proxy in other countries. C. Short Answer Type

Q14. (i) Dissatisfaction with the Treaty of Versailles. Germany regarded it as a humiliation to be undone, and Italy and Japan believed they had been cheated of their share of the spoils; the settlement therefore left the defeated determined on revenge and two of the victors discontented.

(ii) The rise of Fascism and Nazism. Both creeds glorified war and expansion, and both dictators rearmed in open defiance of the treaties — Hitler reintroducing conscription and remilitarising the Rhineland, Mussolini conquering Abyssinia.

(iii) The policy of appeasement. Britain and France conceded demand after demand — the Rhineland, Austria, the Sudetenland at Munich — and each concession persuaded Hitler that they would never fight.

(iv) The failure of the League of Nations. It could not stop Japan in Manchuria in 1931 or Italy in Abyssinia in 1935, and each unpunished aggression taught the next aggressor that collective security was an empty phrase. The Japanese invasion of China, and finally Hitler's attack on Poland on 1 September 1939, brought the war itself.

Official Answer

Q15. It was the policy pursued by Britain and France in the 1930s of giving way to Hitler's demands in the hope that, once satisfied, he would keep the peace. Its motives were understandable enough: the horror of the last war was fresh, both countries were unprepared for another, and many in Britain privately felt that Versailles had been too harsh and that Germany had a case.

In practice it meant accepting, without resistance, the reintroduction of German conscription in 1935, the remilitarisation of the Rhineland in 1936, the annexation of Austria in March 1938, and — at the Munich Conference of September 1938, where Czechoslovakia was not even represented — the transfer of the Sudetenland to Germany. Chamberlain returned from Munich announcing 'peace for our time'.

Its result was to make war certain rather than to prevent it. Hitler concluded that the western powers would always yield; he took the rest of Czechoslovakia in March 1939, and attacked Poland that September expecting no more than protests. Appeasement had also destroyed the League's credit and left the Soviet Union, excluded from Munich, ready to sign the pact with Germany that made the attack on Poland safe.

Official Answer

Q16. (i) Danzig and the Polish Corridor. Danzig was a German-speaking port which Versailles had made a free city under the League, and the Corridor — the strip of Polish territory giving Poland access to the sea — cut East Prussia off from the rest of Germany. Hitler demanded the return of Danzig and a road and railway across the Corridor, and made these grievances the justification for war.

(ii) Lebensraum. His long-declared aim was living space for the German people in the east, at the expense of the Slav peoples whom Nazi doctrine held to be inferior; Poland lay directly in the path of that expansion.

(iii) He was confident the western powers would not fight. They had conceded the Rhineland, Austria and the Sudetenland without resistance, and he did not believe they would act now. The Nazi-Soviet Non-Aggression Pact of August 1939 removed his last anxiety, by securing him against a war on two fronts — and, in its secret protocol, by arranging the partition of Poland with Stalin.

Official Answer

Q17. (i) The defeat of the Axis Powers and the destruction of the dictatorships. Germany, Italy and Japan were beaten and occupied; Hitler and Mussolini were dead; and Germany itself was divided among the victors, a division that hardened into two German states.

(ii) The formation of the United Nations Organisation in 1945, to succeed the discredited League and to maintain international peace and security — this time with the United States a founding member, and with a Security Council empowered to take enforcement action.

(iii) The beginning of the Cold War and the eclipse of Europe. The wartime alliance broke down at once, and the world divided into an American and a Soviet bloc locked in an armed rivalry that lasted forty years; the United States and the Soviet Union emerged as superpowers, while Britain and France were exhausted. That exhaustion, and the nationalism the war had stimulated in Asia and Africa, brought the rapid end of the European empires — India's independence in 1947 being the first great instance.

Official Answer

Q18. The alliance between the western powers and the Soviet Union had held only while there was a common enemy; with Germany defeated, the differences between them came at once to the surface. The Soviet Union kept its armies in the countries of eastern Europe it had liberated and installed communist governments there, which the West regarded as the imposition of an 'iron curtain' across the continent; the Soviet Union in turn saw the American monopoly of the atomic bomb, and the economic reconstruction of western Europe under the Marshall Plan, as preparation for an attack on it.

The result was a permanent division of the world into two hostile blocs, each armed against the other. Rival military alliances were formed — NATO in 1949 and the Warsaw Pact in 1955 — and the rivalry was pursued through propaganda, espionage, economic pressure, a nuclear and space race, and wars fought by proxy in Korea, Vietnam and elsewhere. The two superpowers never fought each other directly, which is why it is called a cold war; but it dominated international affairs for four decades, and it was in reaction to it that the newly independent countries, led by India, founded the Non-Aligned Movement.

Official Answer

Q21. (i) The immediate cause was Hitler's invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939, which brought British and French declarations of war two days later. His justifications were: that Danzig was a German city wrongly taken from Germany by Versailles and made a free city, and should return to the Reich; that the Polish Corridor cut East Prussia off from the rest of Germany, and that he required a road and railway across it; and that Germans living under Polish rule were being persecuted — a claim he had used over the Sudetenland the year before. Behind these lay his real purpose, Lebensraum for the German people in the east.

(ii) The Treaty left a defeated but unbroken Germany with an overwhelming sense of injustice — the war-guilt clause, reparations beyond her means, the loss of a tenth of her population and all her colonies, and disarmament while the victors kept their arms. It was a peace harsh enough to enrage but not harsh enough to disable. Its repudiation became the central promise of Hitler's politics, and every step towards war between 1935 and 1939 was presented as the undoing of one of its clauses.

(iii) The Cold War was the hostility between the American and Soviet blocs that followed the war. With the common enemy gone, the Soviet Union's installation of communist governments across eastern Europe and the West's rearmament and reconstruction of western Europe each appeared to the other as aggression. Rival alliances were formed — NATO in 1949, the Warsaw Pact in 1955 — and the struggle was waged by propaganda, espionage, economic pressure, a nuclear arms race and proxy wars in Korea and Vietnam, but never by direct war between the two superpowers, which is why it was 'cold'. It divided the world for forty years and prompted the newly independent nations, led by India, to found the Non-Aligned Movement.

E. Source-Based / Picture-Based Questions

Q22. (i) In 1931. Japan, a crowded industrial country lacking raw materials, wanted Manchuria's coal and iron, its land for settlement and its market for Japanese goods; her militarist government also aimed at an empire dominating East Asia.
(ii) That it had no means of enforcing its own principles. It could investigate and report — the Lytton Commission duly found against Japan — but its members would not act, and Japan simply left the League in 1933 and kept Manchuria. The episode showed every would-be aggressor that the League's condemnation carried no penalty, and Mussolini took the lesson in Abyssinia four years later.
(iii) The United States, whose President had designed it, never joined, and Germany and Russia were at first excluded, so it was never a body of all the powers; it had no armed force of its own and depended on members' willingness to apply sanctions; its Council required unanimity, so an interested party could block action against itself; and its failures over Manchuria and Abyssinia, followed by the withdrawal of Japan, Germany and Italy and by the Anglo-French policy of appeasement, destroyed what authority it had.
