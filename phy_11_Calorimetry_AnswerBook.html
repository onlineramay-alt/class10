A. Multiple Choice Questions

Q1. (c) thermal energy

The total internal energy (sum of kinetic and potential energies of all molecules) of a substance is called thermal energy. Heat energy refers to the energy in transit, but the internal energy stored in a substance is termed thermal energy.

Official Answer

Q2. (d) Both (b) and (c)

Heat is a form of energy. Its SI unit is the Joule (J). The older CGS unit is the Calorie (cal), where 1 cal = 4.2 J. Both are accepted units of heat.

Official Answer

Q3. (a) 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹

The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ (or equivalently 4.2 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹). This means 4200 J of heat is required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of water by 1 K (or 1°C).

Official Answer

Q4. (a) flow of heat

Temperature determines the direction of flow of heat. Heat flows spontaneously from a body at higher temperature to a body at lower temperature. Temperature is the quantity that decides the direction, not the amount, of heat flow.

Official Answer

Q5. (d) all of the above

The heat energy Q = mcΔT shows that the heat content depends on all three: mass (m), specific heat capacity (c, which depends on material), and the temperature (ΔT). Therefore, all three factors determine the heat energy contained in a body.

Official Answer

Q6. (a) C = m × c

Heat capacity C = mass × specific heat capacity = m × c. Heat capacity is an extensive property (depends on mass), while specific heat capacity is an intensive property (property of the material). Unit of C is J/K and unit of c is J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Official Answer

Q7. (a) low, high

A good conductor of heat has a low specific heat capacity, meaning it absorbs and releases heat quickly with a large change in temperature. A bad conductor has a high specific heat capacity — it absorbs heat slowly and retains it longer.

Official Answer

Q8. (d) hydrogen

Among the given options, hydrogen has the highest specific heat capacity (≈ 14300 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹). Among common liquids, water has the highest specific heat capacity (4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹).

Official Answer

Q9. (c) both (a) and (b)

The principle of calorimetry states that heat lost by the hot body = heat gained by the cold body. This is based on the law of conservation of energy (energy is neither created nor destroyed), and within the heat domain, it is equivalent to the law of conservation of heat.

Official Answer

Q10. (c) principle of calorimetry

Heat (quantity of thermal energy) is measured using the principle of calorimetry, i.e., by measuring the temperature change it produces in a known mass of water. A thermometer measures temperature (not heat), and a barometer measures atmospheric pressure.

Official Answer

Assertion A is correct: specific heat capacity is indeed the heat required per unit mass per unit temperature rise. Reason R is FALSE: specific heat capacity IS a characteristic property of a substance — it differs from substance to substance and is used to identify materials.

Q12. (a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A

Assertion A correctly describes the method of mixtures. Reason R (law of conservation of energy) correctly explains why heat lost = heat gained in an isolated system. No energy is created or destroyed, so the heat transferred from the hot body equals the heat absorbed by the cold body.

Q13. (d) absorbed, rejected

During melting (solid → liquid), the substance absorbs heat (latent heat of fusion) to break intermolecular bonds without any rise in temperature. During freezing (liquid → solid), the same amount of heat is rejected (released) to the surroundings at constant temperature.

Official Answer

Q14. (c) increasing the potential energy of molecules

During melting, temperature remains constant, so the kinetic energy of molecules does not change. The latent heat absorbed is used to overcome intermolecular attractions, thereby increasing the potential energy of molecules (increasing intermolecular separation).

Official Answer

Q15. (c) Heat absorbed by A is twice of that by B

Since Q = mcΔT, and both bodies are made of the same material (same c) and heated through the same temperature (same ΔT), but mass of A = 2 × mass of B:
Q_A = 2m × c × ΔT = 2 × (m × c × ΔT) = 2 × Q_B
Therefore, A absorbs twice as much heat as B.

Official Answer

Q16. (c) depends on the material

Specific latent heat is the heat absorbed or released per unit mass during a change of state at constant temperature. It is a characteristic property that depends only on the material and the type of phase change (fusion or vaporisation), not on the mass or temperature change.

Official Answer

Q17. (b) energy is absorbed and temperature remains constant

During melting, ice absorbs latent heat of fusion (336 J g⁻¹) from the surroundings to break the rigid lattice bonds. Since all this energy goes into changing the phase (breaking bonds) rather than increasing kinetic energy, the temperature remains constant at 0°C throughout the melting process.

Official Answer

Q18. (d) 0.5 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹

Specific heat capacity c = Thermal capacity / mass = 2.5 / 5 = 0.5 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.
Specific heat capacity is an intensive property — it is a characteristic of the material and does NOT change when mass changes. Whether the mass is 5 g or 15 g (tripled), the specific heat capacity remains 0.5 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Official Answer

Q19. (d) remains the same

Specific heat capacity is an intrinsic (intensive) property of a material. Changing the shape or size of a substance (stretching, cutting, melting) does not alter its specific heat capacity. Therefore, stretching the wire to twice its length leaves the specific heat capacity unchanged.

Official Answer

Q20. (c) latent heat of fusion

Latent heat of fusion is the total heat energy required to melt a given mass of a substance at its melting point without any change in temperature. It equals mass × specific latent heat (Q = mL). The 'specific' latent heat of fusion is the heat per unit mass. 'Latent heat of fusion' applies to the given mass, making (c) correct.

Official Answer

Q21. (b) Substance B

Since all substances have the same mass and heat is absorbed at the same rate, the substance that takes the longest time to melt absorbs the most heat. Q = mL, so longer time → more heat absorbed → higher specific latent heat L. B takes 7 minutes (the longest), so B has the highest specific latent heat.

Official Answer

Q22. (a) Substance A will experience a smaller temperature change than substance B

From Q = mcΔT → ΔT = Q/(mc). Since Q and m are the same for both, ΔT is inversely proportional to c. Substance A has higher c, so it will have a smaller temperature rise (ΔT_A < ΔT_B).

Q49. Effect of increase of pressure on the melting point of ice: Increase in pressure lowers the melting point of ice (anomalous behaviour).

Explanation: Ice is less dense than water. When pressure is applied, it favours the state with the smaller volume, which is water. Hence, ice melts at a lower temperature under increased pressure.

Adding salt (or any dissolved substance) to water increases its boiling point. This is called elevation of boiling point. For example, salt water boils at a temperature slightly above 100°C.

Practical application: A pressure cooker operates on this principle — the sealed vessel increases the pressure, raising the boiling point of water to ~120°C, cooking food faster.

Q54. Assumption: No heat is lost to the surroundings (the system is perfectly insulated).

Derivation:
Let the final temperature of the mixture = t
Since t₁ > t₂, the first substance cools from t₁ to t, and the second substance warms from t₂ to t.

Heat lost by substance 1:
Q₁ = m₁c₁(t₁ − t)

Heat gained by substance 2:
Q₂ = m₂c₂(t − t₂)

By the principle of calorimetry (heat lost = heat gained):
m₁c₁(t₁ − t) = m₂c₂(t − t₂)
m₁c₁t₁ − m₁c₁t = m₂c₂t − m₂c₂t₂
m₁c₁t₁ + m₂c₂t₂ = t(m₁c₁ + m₂c₂)

∴ t = (m₁c₁t₁ + m₂c₂t₂) / (m₁c₁ + m₂c₂)

This is the formula for the final temperature of the mixture.

Note: This formula is valid only when t₂ < t < t₁ and no phase change occurs.

Q60. (a) Change of phase: A change of phase (or change of state) is a process in which a substance transforms from one physical state to another (e.g., solid to liquid, liquid to gas, or vice versa) at a constant temperature and pressure.

(b) Temperature during phase change: No, there is no change in temperature during a change of phase. Even though heat is being supplied or removed, the temperature remains constant until the phase change is complete. For example, ice remains at 0°C throughout its melting process.

(c) Heat during phase change:
- Solid → Liquid (melting): Substance absorbs heat.
- Liquid → Gas (vaporisation): Substance absorbs heat.
- Gas → Liquid (condensation): Substance liberates (releases) heat.
- Liquid → Solid (freezing): Substance liberates (releases) heat.

(d) The energy absorbed during a phase change is called Latent Heat (L). 'Latent' means hidden, because this heat does not cause a temperature change — it is hidden in the change of state.
Formula: Q = mL, where m = mass, L = specific latent heat.

Q61. Anomalous Expansion of Water:
Most substances expand on heating and contract on cooling. Water behaves differently in the temperature range 0°C to 4°C — it contracts on heating (density increases) from 0°C to 4°C, and expands on heating above 4°C (density decreases). This unusual behaviour is called anomalous expansion of water.

At 4°C, water has its maximum density (1000 kg/m³).

Importance for Aquatic Life:
In cold winters, as the surface of a lake or pond cools:
1. Water at 4°C (maximum density) sinks to the bottom.
2. Cooler water (0°C to 4°C), being less dense, floats to the top.
3. When the temperature falls below 4°C, the surface water eventually freezes at 0°C.
4. Ice, being less dense than water (0.917 g/cm³), floats on the surface, forming an insulating layer.
5. This ice layer prevents further heat loss, keeping the water below at ≈4°C.
6. Aquatic animals and plants survive in the liquid water beneath the ice.

If water did not show anomalous expansion, lakes would freeze from the bottom up, killing all aquatic life.

Q62. (i) Heating of solid (temperature rising):
When heat is supplied to a solid, the molecules vibrate faster about their mean positions. The average kinetic energy of the molecules increases. Temperature rises. The potential energy also increases slightly as molecular distances increase marginally, but the dominant change is in kinetic energy.

(ii) Change of state from solid to liquid (at melting point, constant temperature):
At the melting point, the supplied heat (latent heat of fusion) does NOT increase the kinetic energy of molecules — temperature remains constant. Instead, the heat energy is used to overcome the intermolecular attractive forces binding the solid lattice. Molecules gain enough energy to break free from their fixed positions. The average potential energy increases significantly (molecules are farther apart). Average kinetic energy remains unchanged (temperature is constant).

This is why latent heat does not cause a temperature rise — it goes entirely into increasing the potential energy (breaking bonds).

(iii) Heating the liquid (temperature rising above melting point):
Once the substance is fully liquid, further heating increases the average kinetic energy of the molecules (temperature rises). Molecules move faster and more randomly. The average potential energy also increases slightly as molecules move farther apart, but the dominant change is in kinetic energy.
